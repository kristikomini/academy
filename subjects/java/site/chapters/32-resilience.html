Timeouts, retries and circuit breakers

A retry without a budget turns one slow dependency into an outage across every service that calls it. This chapter is the small set of patterns that decide whether a distributed system degrades or collapses — and they are almost entirely about doing less, not more.

From the advert

Not in any advert. Implied by every one of them that says microservizi: the moment a call crosses the network, failure stops being exceptional and becomes a normal operating condition.

Timeouts, first and always

🧒 Simple

If you call another service and it never answers, your request waits. While it waits, it holds a thread and often a database connection.

Enough of those and your service stops responding — even though nothing in it is broken.

🎓 Professional

A missing timeout is the most common cause of cascading failure, and it is the cheapest thing to fix. Set both a connect timeout and a read timeout on every outbound call, and make them shorter than your own caller’s timeout — otherwise you are still working on a request that nobody is waiting for any more.

Think in terms of a latency budget: if your endpoint promises 2 seconds and calls three services, they cannot each be allowed 2 seconds. Failing fast is a feature; a slow success nobody is waiting for is pure cost.

Retries, and the storm they cause

🧒 Simple

If a call fails, trying again often works. But if the other service is failing because it is overloaded, everyone retrying makes it worse.

🎓 Professional

Retry only idempotent operations (chapter 17) and only transient failures — a connection reset or a 503, never a 400 or a 422, which will fail identically forever. Use exponential backoff with jitter: without jitter, every client retries at the same instant and you have rebuilt the thundering herd you were avoiding.

Cap the total attempts, and remember retries multiply through a call chain: three retries at each of three hops is twenty-seven requests for one user action. That multiplication is why the pattern needs a budget rather than a number, and why retrying at only one layer — usually the outermost — is often the right design.

⚠ Trap

Retrying a non-idempotent POST after a timeout is how duplicates are created. The first attempt may have succeeded and only the response was lost. Either make the operation idempotent with a key, or do not retry it — those are the only two honest options.

Circuit breakers and bulkheads

🧒 Simple

A circuit breaker watches how often calls to a service fail. Once too many fail, it stops calling for a while and fails immediately instead — giving the other side room to recover.

🎓 Professional

Resilience4j is the standard choice now that Hystrix is retired. The breaker moves between closed (calls pass), open (calls fail immediately, no load on the dependency) and half-open (a few probes decide whether to close again). It converts a slow, resource-consuming failure into a fast, cheap one.

Pair it with a bulkhead — a separate, bounded pool of concurrent calls per dependency — so that one misbehaving downstream cannot consume every thread in your service. And design the fallback deliberately: cached data, a partial response, or a clear error. A fallback that silently returns an empty list turns an outage into wrong data, which is worse.

Golden rules
  • Every outbound call gets a timeout, shorter than your caller’s.
  • Retry only idempotent operations, only on transient failures.
  • Exponential backoff with jitter. Without jitter you have built a herd.
  • Retries multiply through a chain. Budget them; prefer retrying at one layer.
  • A breaker turns a slow failure into a fast one, which is what protects you.
  • An empty-list fallback is worse than an error. Wrong data outlives an outage.

“Un servizio da cui dipendete diventa lento. Cosa succede al vostro?”

“Se non ho timeout, i miei thread restano appesi in attesa e alla fine smetto di rispondere anch’io, pur non avendo niente di rotto: è il fallimento a cascata. Quindi timeout su ogni chiamata, più corti del timeout di chi chiama me, e un circuit breaker che dopo una certa percentuale di errori smette di provarci e fallisce subito — così smetto anche di caricare un servizio già in difficoltà. I retry solo su operazioni idempotenti e con backoff esponenziale e jitter, altrimenti tutti i client riprovano nello stesso istante. E il fallback deve essere esplicito: dati in cache o un errore chiaro, mai una lista vuota che sembra un risultato valido.”
