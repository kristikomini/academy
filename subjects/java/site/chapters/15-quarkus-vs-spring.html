Quarkus, and when it is chosen instead

One of the six adverts — a public-administration contract — asks for Quarkus alongside Spring Boot. You do not need to have shipped it. You do need to be able to say what it moves and what that buys, without either dismissing it or overselling it.

From the advert

“framework come Quarkus e Spring Boot”, from the remote public administration posting, which also asks for “ottima conoscenza di Java 11+”.

Moving work to build time

🧒 Simple

Spring works out how to wire your application when it starts. Quarkus works most of it out when you build, so there is far less to do at start-up.

The result starts in milliseconds instead of seconds and uses much less memory.

🎓 Professional

Quarkus performs augmentation at build time: annotation scanning, dependency-injection wiring and framework metadata are resolved by build-time extensions and baked into generated bytecode, rather than being discovered by reflection during start-up. Less reflection also makes the application amenable to GraalVM ahead-of-time compilation into a native binary.

The trade is directly the one from chapter 01: you give up the JIT’s profile-guided optimisation, so peak throughput on a long-running service is typically lower than a warm JVM. You gain start-up time and memory footprint.

When that trade is worth it

🧒 Simple

If your service starts once and runs for weeks, start-up time does not matter. If it scales up and down all day, or you pay per megabyte, it matters a lot.

🎓 Professional

Quarkus and native images win where instances are short-lived or dense: serverless functions, aggressive autoscaling, CLI tools, and clusters where you are paying for RAM per pod and running many small services. They lose where a handful of long-lived instances serve steady traffic — which is most of the work behind these six adverts.

The honest framing for an interview is that this is not a Spring-versus-Quarkus question but a start-up-versus-steady-state question, and that Spring Boot 3 with GraalVM native support has narrowed it considerably. Also worth knowing: Quarkus is built on Jakarta EE standards (CDI, JAX-RS via RESTEasy, JPA via Hibernate), so a Spring developer reads it easily — the annotations differ, the concepts do not.

⚠ Trap

Do not claim a native image is simply “faster”. An interviewer who has used one will ask about throughput, and the correct answer is that it starts faster and uses less memory while typically peaking lower. Claiming both is the tell that you have read a marketing page.

Golden rules
  • Quarkus moves wiring to build time. Less reflection, less start-up work.
  • Faster start, smaller footprint, lower peak throughput. All three, honestly.
  • It is a start-up-versus-steady-state question, not a tribal one.
  • Built on Jakarta standards — CDI, JAX-RS, JPA. A Spring developer reads it fine.
  • Spring Boot 3 + GraalVM narrowed the gap. Say so; it shows you are current.

“Conosce Quarkus? Che differenza c’è con Spring Boot?”

If you have not used it, say so and then show you understand the design — that is a much better answer than a vague yes: “Non l’ho usato in produzione. La differenza principale è che Quarkus sposta a build time gran parte del lavoro che Spring fa all’avvio — scansione delle annotazioni e wiring — quindi parte in millisecondi e consuma molta meno memoria, ed è adatto a native image con GraalVM. Il prezzo è il throughput a regime, perché si rinuncia alle ottimizzazioni del JIT. Ha senso per funzioni serverless o per molti pod piccoli; per un servizio che sta su per settimane il vantaggio è minore.”
