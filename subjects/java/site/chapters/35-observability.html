Logs, metrics and traces

Once there is more than one service, “it is slow” is unanswerable without this. The three signals, what each is for, and the correlation id that turns a complaint into a log query.

From the advert

Not in any advert. And the fastest way to sound senior in a system-design conversation, because the follow-up to any distributed design is “how would you know it was broken?”

The three signals

🧒 Simple

Logs say what happened. Metrics say how much and how often. Traces say where the time went across services.

You need all three, and they answer different questions.

🎓 Professional

Metrics are cheap, aggregated numbers — ideal for alerting and dashboards, useless for explaining one specific request. Logs are detailed and expensive at volume; they explain a single case. Traces show one request’s path across services with timing per hop, which is the only practical way to answer “which of these six services made it slow”.

In Spring: Micrometer for metrics with an Actuator endpoint Prometheus scrapes; Micrometer Tracing exporting to OpenTelemetry for traces; structured JSON logs shipped somewhere searchable. The important word is OpenTelemetry — it is vendor-neutral, so the backend can change without touching application code.

Correlation ids

🧒 Simple

Give every incoming request an id, pass it to every service you call, and put it in every log line. Then one complaint becomes one search.

🎓 Professional

A trace id generated at the edge, propagated by headers (W3C traceparent), and put into the logging MDC so every line carries it. Spring Boot with Micrometer Tracing does this for you; the piece people forget is propagating it into asynchronous work — a message published to Kafka must carry the trace context in its headers, or the trail stops at the broker.

Return the correlation id in error responses too (chapter 18). It costs one field and turns “it failed this morning” into an exact query, which is worth more than most dashboards.

⚠ Trap

The context is thread-local, like the SecurityContext in chapter 20. Hand work to an ExecutorService or a plain thread and the trace id vanishes, so the logs from your background task are the ones you cannot correlate — which is exactly where you needed it. Use the framework’s context-propagating wrappers rather than a raw executor.

What to alert on

🧒 Simple

Alert on things users notice — errors and slowness — not on things that merely look dramatic, like CPU going up.

🎓 Professional

Alert on symptoms, not causes: error rate, latency at the 95th and 99th percentiles, saturation of a bounded resource (the connection pool from chapter 22, the consumer lag from the Kafka course). High CPU with healthy latency is not an incident; low CPU with a growing queue is.

Never alert on averages. An average latency of 200 ms can hide 5 % of users waiting eight seconds, and those are the users who complain. Percentiles are the whole point, and saying that is a small, reliable signal of having operated something.

Golden rules
  • Metrics to alert, logs to explain one case, traces to find the slow hop.
  • OpenTelemetry so the backend is replaceable without touching code.
  • One trace id, propagated everywhere — including into message headers.
  • Context is thread-local. It does not follow you into an executor.
  • Alert on symptoms: errors, p95/p99 latency, saturation.
  • Never alert on an average. It hides the users who are suffering.

“Un utente dice che ‘a volte è lento’. Come lo affronta?”

“Prima guardo le percentili, non la media: se la p99 è alta e la mediana no, ‘a volte’ è esattamente quello che mi aspetto di vedere. Poi uso il tracing per capire dove va il tempo: con un trace distribuito vedo la latenza per ogni hop e scopro se il problema è il mio servizio, una chiamata a valle o l’attesa di una connessione dal pool. Se ho bisogno del caso singolo, cerco per correlation id, che metto in ogni log e restituisco anche nelle risposte di errore. La media è l’unica cosa che non guardo: nasconde proprio gli utenti che si stanno lamentando.”
