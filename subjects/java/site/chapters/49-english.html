The English the advert means

One advert asks for excellent English. What that means in practice is narrow and learnable: you need to survive a stand-up, a code review and a technical call — not to discuss literature. Twenty phrases carry most of it.

From the advert

“Excellent knowledge of English language for international work environments”.

What level they actually need

🧒 Simple

They need you to understand a call, be understood, and write a clear message. An accent is fine. Perfect grammar is not the requirement.

🎓 Professional

In practice this is B2: you can follow a technical discussion at natural speed, express a disagreement, and write documentation somebody else can act on. The failure mode companies are screening for is not imperfect grammar — it is a candidate who goes silent in a call because they did not understand and did not say so.

Which gives the single most useful habit: ask for repetition without apology. “Sorry, could you repeat that?” and “Just to confirm I understood — you mean X?” are normal, professional sentences that native speakers use with each other constantly. Using them reads as competence, not weakness.

The phrases that carry a technical call

🧒 Simple

Most of a technical conversation is a small number of repeated moves: disagreeing, asking for time, explaining a delay, proposing an alternative.

🎓 Professional

Learn these as fixed phrases, so they cost you nothing under pressure:

Disagreeing: “I see it differently — my concern is…” / “That would work, but it means we…”
Buying time: “Let me check and come back to you.” / “I would rather not guess — can I confirm and reply this afternoon?”
Reporting a delay: “It is taking longer than I estimated. The reason is X. I expect it by Thursday.”
In review: “This is a suggestion, not a blocker.” / “Could you walk me through why you chose this?”
Not understanding: “Sorry, I did not catch that — could you say it again?”

⚠ Trap

Two false friends that cause real confusion. Eventually does not mean eventualmente — it means alla fine; the word you want is possibly or if needed. And actually does not mean attualmente; it means in realtà. “Actually we are using Java 11” sounds like a correction, not a status report.

Written English, which is most of it

🧒 Simple

Most of your English will be written — pull requests, tickets, chat. Short sentences are better English than long ones, and easier to write.

🎓 Professional

Put the conclusion first — international teams skim — then the detail. Short sentences and simple tenses are not a limitation; they are what good technical writing looks like in any language. A ticket that opens with “The nightly import fails when a customer has no VAT number” is better than three paragraphs building to it.

For asynchronous work across time zones, include what you need and by when, because a reply may be twelve hours away. “Could you confirm whether X, so I can continue tomorrow morning?” saves a full day compared with “What do you think?”

Golden rules
  • B2 is the bar: follow, be understood, write clearly. The accent is not the problem.
  • Ask for repetition without apologising. Silence is the failure they screen for.
  • Learn fixed phrases for disagreeing, buying time and reporting delay.
  • Eventually ≠ eventualmente; actually ≠ attualmente.
  • Conclusion first, then detail. Short sentences are good technical English.
  • Async: say what you need and by when.

“How would you describe your English level?”

Answer in English, briefly, and give evidence rather than a self-assessment number: “I would say B2. I read technical documentation and follow calls without difficulty, and I write pull requests and documentation in English every day. Speaking is where I am least practised — I sometimes need a moment to find a word — but I have no problem asking someone to repeat something, and I would rather do that than pretend I understood.” Naming the weaker skill and how you handle it is more convincing than claiming C1.
