Microservices: the shape and the cost

One advert asks for microservices by name. The valuable answer in an interview is never enthusiasm — it is being able to say what splitting buys, what it charges, and how to recognise the distributed monolith that most “microservice” systems actually are.

From the advert

“Sviluppare microservizi in Java 11+ utilizzando Spring Boot e Spring Cloud”, from the Bologna industrial posting.

What splitting actually buys

🧒 Simple

A microservice architecture splits one application into several small ones that talk over the network. Each can be deployed, scaled and rewritten on its own.

The gain is mostly about teams, not about performance.

🎓 Professional

The real benefits are independent deployability, independent scaling of the part that actually needs it, fault isolation, and technology freedom per service. Notice that three of those four are organisational: microservices are a way of letting several teams ship without coordinating a release, and their main payoff appears when the number of teams is the bottleneck.

Which gives the honest position for an interview: a monolith is the correct default for a small team, and splitting is a response to a problem you can name. Saying that calmly reads as more senior than advocating microservices, because the interviewer has usually lived through a split that did not pay for itself.

What it charges

🧒 Simple

Every method call that becomes a network call can now be slow, fail, or arrive twice. Every piece of data that used to be one transaction is now spread across services.

🎓 Professional

You give up the two things a monolith gave you for free: the local call (which never partially failed) and the transaction (which made several writes atomic). In exchange you inherit partial failure, eventual consistency, distributed tracing as a requirement rather than a nicety, versioned contracts between services, and a deployment topology someone has to operate.

The right unit is a bounded context — a piece of the domain with its own language and its own data. Splitting by technical layer instead (“the database service”, “the validation service”) produces services that cannot change without each other, which is the worst of both worlds.

⚠ Trap

Shared database is the tell. If two services read and write the same tables, they are one service in two deployments: neither can change its schema, both must be released together, and you have paid every network cost for none of the independence. Each service owns its data, and others go through its API.

Recognising the distributed monolith

🧒 Simple

If you cannot deploy one service without deploying the others at the same time, you have not got microservices. You have got one application spread over a network.

🎓 Professional

The symptoms, in order of severity: a shared database; a release train where everything ships together; a chain of synchronous calls five services deep, so the slowest one sets everyone else’s latency and any failure cascades; and a shared library of DTOs that every service must upgrade in lockstep.

The usual remedies are asynchronous messaging where the caller does not need an answer now (chapter 33), consumer-driven contracts instead of a shared model jar, and moving a boundary so that two things which always change together live in one service again. Merging two services is a legitimate, senior move, and saying so is a good signal.

Golden rules
  • The main benefit is organisational. Independent deployment by independent teams.
  • A monolith is the right default for a small team. Split in response to a named problem.
  • Split by bounded context, never by technical layer.
  • Shared database means one service in two deployments.
  • You trade local calls and transactions for partial failure and eventual consistency.
  • Merging two services back together is a valid answer.

“Quando spezzerebbe un monolite in microservizi?”

Resist the expected enthusiasm: “Quando c’è un problema preciso che lo giustifica: più team che si bloccano a vicenda sulle release, oppure una parte del sistema con un profilo di carico completamente diverso dal resto che vorrei scalare da sola. Se siamo un team solo, il monolite modulare è quasi sempre la scelta giusta: paghi tutti i costi del distribuito — fallimenti parziali, consistenza eventuale, tracing — senza prenderti il beneficio, che è organizzativo. E il primo segnale che lo split è andato male è il database condiviso: a quel punto sono due deploy di un servizio solo.”
