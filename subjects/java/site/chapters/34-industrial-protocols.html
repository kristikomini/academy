OPC UA, MQTT and Modbus

Emilia-Romagna builds machines — packaging, ceramics, automotive, food processing — and a large share of its Java work is bridging those machines to systems that speak HTTP. This is the chapter no general Java course has, and it is the one that makes advert 1 make sense.

From the advert

“progettazione e implementazione di API REST/GraphQL per l’integrazione con sistemi industriali (OPC UA, MQTT, Modbus)”, from the Bologna industrial posting.

The three protocols, and what each is for

🧒 Simple

Modbus is very old and very simple: read and write numbered registers on a device. MQTT is lightweight publish/subscribe, designed for unreliable networks. OPC UA is the modern industrial standard: not just values, but a described model of the machine, with types and security.

🎓 Professional

Modbus (RTU over serial, TCP over Ethernet) exposes coils and registers by number. There are no types beyond 16-bit words, no discovery, and no security whatsoever — meaning is entirely in the vendor’s register map, a PDF you must obtain. Endianness and word order vary by device, which is why a float arrives as nonsense until you get the byte order right.

MQTT is a broker-based pub/sub with topics, three QoS levels, retained messages and a last-will message that announces a client’s death. It is the usual transport for telemetry from many devices over links that drop. OPC UA carries an address space: nodes with types, units, historical access and subscriptions, plus certificates and encryption. It is the one to prefer for new work, and the heaviest.

Bridging to an HTTP API

🧒 Simple

The job is usually the same shape: read values from machines continuously, store them, and expose them to the rest of the company as a normal web API.

🎓 Professional

A gateway service subscribes (OPC UA subscriptions or MQTT topics) or polls (Modbus, which has no push), normalises the readings into domain events, and publishes them — typically onto Kafka, which is exactly why advert 5 pairs Kafka with this kind of work. Libraries: Eclipse Milo for OPC UA, Eclipse Paho or HiveMQ for MQTT, jlibmodbus or Modbus4J for Modbus.

Two design points carry most of the value. Do not expose register numbers in your API — translate to domain terms at the edge, or every consumer inherits the vendor’s PDF. And timestamp at the source where the protocol allows it: OPC UA gives you a source timestamp, and using your own arrival time silently mislabels data that was buffered during a network outage.

⚠ Trap

Industrial networks are not IT networks. A PLC may have a hard limit of a few concurrent connections and can be destabilised by aggressive polling — and on a production line that is not a slow dashboard, it is a stopped machine. Poll rates are negotiated with the automation engineers, never chosen by the backend developer alone.

What the work is actually like

🧒 Simple

Expect old equipment, incomplete documentation, and no test environment — because the test environment is the factory.

🎓 Professional

The realities worth naming in an interview: devices from the 1990s alongside new ones; register maps that disagree with the hardware; no staging line, so you build a simulator (which is a genuinely good thing to have written); and OT/IT segregation, so your service reaches the machines through a firewall a different department controls.

Also expect data volume to be larger than anyone estimated — a hundred machines at ten readings a second is not a relational-database workload, which is why time-series storage and the Kafka retention questions arrive at the same time. If you can say that having thought about it, you are ahead of most candidates for advert 1.

Golden rules
  • Modbus is registers and a PDF. No types, no discovery, no security — and check the word order.
  • MQTT for telemetry over bad links; QoS, retained messages, last will.
  • OPC UA for new work — a typed address space with security, and a source timestamp.
  • Never expose register numbers in your API. Translate at the edge.
  • Timestamp at the source. Arrival time lies after an outage.
  • Poll rates are negotiated, not chosen. A PLC is not a web server.

“Ha esperienza di integrazione con sistemi industriali?”

If you have not, show that you understand the shape of the problem rather than bluffing: “Non ho lavorato in produzione su una linea, ma so come è fatto il problema. Ho un gateway che si sottoscrive via OPC UA o MQTT, oppure fa polling su Modbus, normalizza le letture in eventi di dominio e le pubblica — tipicamente su Kafka. Due cose a cui starei attento: non esporre i numeri di registro nell’API, altrimenti ogni consumatore si porta dietro il PDF del fornitore; e usare il timestamp della sorgente invece di quello di arrivo, perché dopo un’interruzione di rete i dati bufferizzati arriverebbero con l’ora sbagliata.”
