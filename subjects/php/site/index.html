The shape of the job, in one picture

Almost every requirement in that list is one layer of a single request travelling through a system. Learning them in isolation is what makes them feel like twelve unrelated subjects. They are not. They are floors of one building.

🧒 Simple

Imagine a restaurant. The browser is the customer. Nginx and PHP-FPM are the front door and the person who assigns you a table. Laravel is the waiter who takes the order and checks it makes sense. Your PHP is the chef who decides what to actually cook. Eloquent is the kitchen porter who runs to the store cupboard. MySQL is the cupboard — the only place food is really kept. Redis is the small shelf by the stove with things you already prepared, and also the spike where orders wait their turn. Git is the recipe book where every change is written down.

One thing is different from most kitchens: when the customer leaves, the chef forgets everything. Every order starts from nothing. That is PHP.

🎓 Professional

The adverts describe a standard layered PHP stack: a server-rendered or SPA client over an HTTP boundary handled by PHP-FPM and a framework, an application layer in plain PHP, an ORM mapping to a relational store, and two auxiliary data systems — a cache and a search index — that exist because a relational database is bad at exactly two things: repeating cheap answers, and free-text relevance.

The property that distinguishes this stack from a Java or .NET one is shared-nothing: each request gets a worker process with a clean memory space and nothing survives it. That single fact explains PHP's deployment story, its caching story, why there is no application-level connection pool, and why "just use a static" is the wrong answer roughly every time it is suggested.

Beyond the advert

The requirements above are what companies write down. These chapters are not in them — they are here because the same skills turn up again and again in PHP work across Modena, Bologna, Reggio Emilia and Milano, and because interviewers ask about them whether the posting mentioned them or not. Two of them — maintaining legacy PHP, and deploying to shared cPanel hosting — are large, unglamorous parts of the actual regional job that almost no course prepares anyone for, and three more are the rounds you have to pass before anyone reads your code at all.
