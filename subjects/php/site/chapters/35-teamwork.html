Analysis, autonomy and code review

"Capacità di analisi, autonomia e problem solving" is the vaguest line in any advert and one of the most decisive. It means: can you turn a two-sentence request into something buildable, do you ask the question before spending three days on the wrong thing, and can you review someone’s code without making them defensive. None of that is about PHP, and all of it is what gets you kept.

From the advert

"Capacità di analisi, autonomia e problem solving". Autonomia is frequently a statement about the team — nobody has time to onboard you thoroughly. It does not mean "never ask"; it means "ask well, and do not ask twice".

Turning a vague request into a task

🧒 Simple

Somebody asks for a feature by describing the solution they imagined. Your job is to find out what they were actually trying to achieve, because there is often a simpler answer they did not know existed.

🎓 Professional

Requests arrive as solutions: "serve un pulsante per esportare in Excel". The analysis is the step back — who exports, how often, and what do they do with the file afterwards? Often the answer is that somebody re-imports it into the accounting system every Monday, and the real feature is a scheduled transfer nobody has to remember.

Questions that reliably find the shape of a task:

  • Who is it for, and what do they do afterwards? Reveals the real workflow.
  • What happens today without it? The existing manual workaround is the specification, and it is usually more accurate than the request.
  • How often, and how much data? Ten rows or ten million is a different design (ch. 04, 21).
  • What should happen when it fails? Almost never specified, and it is half the work.
  • Who else is affected? The other application writing that table (ch. 10b, 33).
  • What is explicitly out of scope? Written down, agreed, and referred back to later.

Then write the understanding back in three or four lines and get a yes before building. Costs five minutes; catches the misunderstanding while it is still free. Do it in writing even after a verbal conversation — not defensively, but because a shared written understanding is the artefact, and memory is not.

Asking well: the autonomy that is not silence

🧒 Simple

Being autonomous does not mean struggling alone for two days. It means arriving with the question already narrowed down, so answering it takes someone two minutes instead of thirty.

🎓 Professional

Two failure modes, and juniors alternate between them: asking before trying anything, and not asking until Thursday. The cure is a rule, agreed with your lead: a timebox. Thirty minutes, or an hour, stuck on the same thing — then ask. Predictable in both directions, and it makes the asking a process rather than an admission.

A good question has four parts and takes a minute to write: what I am trying to do; what I tried; what happened, exactly, with the error; and what I think is going on. That last part is what makes the difference — it shows you thought, and it lets the other person correct your model instead of starting from zero.

Ask in a channel rather than a DM where the culture allows it, so the answer is searchable and the next person finds it. And when you get the answer, write it down where the next person will look — the README, the runbook (ch. 25b), a comment. Being the person who leaves the trail behind them is quietly one of the most valued things you can be, and it is entirely within your control from week one.

⚠ Trap

Assuming instead of asking, then building for three days. Almost every large piece of wasted work in a career starts with a small ambiguity somebody decided not to raise — usually out of a fear of looking inexperienced. The maths is unforgiving: the question costs two minutes of someone’s time; the wrong assumption costs three days of yours plus the rework. Nobody has ever been thought junior for asking a precise question on day two; plenty have been for delivering the wrong feature on day five.

Code review, in both directions

🧒 Simple

A review is two people making the code better, not one person marking the other’s homework. Almost all the friction comes from wording, not from the substance of what is being said.

🎓 Professional

As the reviewer. Review the code, never the person: "questa query fa un N+1", not "non hai pensato alle performance". Ask rather than instruct when you are not certain — "cosa succede se l’array è vuoto?" is stronger than an assertion, because it is genuinely a question and occasionally the answer is "it cannot be".

Separate severity explicitly, because otherwise everything reads as equally blocking. A convention like blocking: / suggestion: / nit: costs nothing and removes most of the tension in review culture. Anything about formatting should have been handled by the linter before a human saw it (ch. 27) — if you are commenting about spacing, fix the pipeline, not the PR.

Say what is good, specifically, and not as a ritual. And approve with minor comments rather than blocking a second round-trip for a variable name; a review process that always takes two days is one people start routing around.

As the author. Small PRs — a 40-file PR gets "LGTM" and a 4-file PR gets genuinely read (ch. 26). Write the description for the reviewer: what changed, why, how you tested, and what you are unsure about, which invites the review you actually want. Answer every comment, even if only to say you disagree and why. Do not take it personally: the comment is about a diff, and every senior developer has had far worse said about far worse code.

When you disagree, disagree once with a reason. If the reviewer holds their position and it is not a correctness issue, do it their way and move on — and if it is a correctness issue, escalate it as a question about the code rather than a contest.

Try it. The same three review comments, written twice. The substance is identical; only one version produces a good conversation.

✗ "Questo è sbagliato."
✓ "blocking: qui `$order->customer` dentro il foreach fa una query per riga —
   con 500 ordini sono 500 query. Un `with('customer')` nella query di partenza
   lo risolve. (Se mi sfugge qualcosa e i customer sono già caricati, ignorami.)"

✗ "Perché non hai usato un repository?"
✓ "suggestion: valuterei di spostare questa query in un metodo dedicato — la vedo
   ripetuta anche in OrderController. Non blocca il merge, se preferisci apriamo
   un ticket a parte."

✗ "Nomi orribili."
✓ "nit: `$d` qui è la data di consegna? `$deliveryDate` mi aiuterebbe a rileggerlo
   fra sei mesi. Nit, non blocca."

Communicating with people who are not developers

🧒 Simple

The client does not want to know about the queue. They want to know whether it will be ready and what it will do. Translate outwards, always.

🎓 Professional

Lead with the answer, then the reason. "Sì, per giovedì" before the explanation, not after four sentences of context. Non-technical people read the first line and skim the rest.

Talk in their units. Not "the job is in failed_jobs", but "le fatture di ieri non sono partite; sono recuperabili e le rimando entro le 14" (ch. 25b). Impact, then remedy, then timing.

Never say "è impossibile" when you mean expensive. Give the trade: "si può fare, sono circa cinque giorni; oppure c’è una versione più semplice che copre il novanta per cento dei casi in uno". That sentence turns you from an obstacle into an adviser, and it is the single most useful phrase in client work.

Bad news early and in writing. A delay announced on Tuesday is a plan; the same delay announced on Friday afternoon is a crisis. And write down decisions taken verbally — a short message summarising what was agreed protects everyone, including the person who agreed to it.

Golden rules
  • Requests arrive as solutions. Ask what they are trying to achieve.
  • The manual workaround is the specification.
  • Write the understanding back before building. Five minutes, catches everything.
  • Timebox before asking, then ask with what you tried and what you think.
  • Write the answer down where the next person will look.
  • Review the code, not the person, and label severity.
  • Small PRs, and say what you are unsure about.
  • Never "impossible" when you mean expensive. Offer the trade.

"Le arriva una richiesta poco chiara dal cliente. Cosa fa?"

Show a method, not good intentions: "Non parto a scrivere codice. Prima capisco cosa vuole ottenere, non cosa ha chiesto: le richieste arrivano già sotto forma di soluzione, e spesso c’è una via più semplice. Chiedo chi la userà, cosa fa oggi senza, con che frequenza e con quanti dati, e cosa deve succedere quando qualcosa va storto — quest’ultima non la specifica mai nessuno ed è metà del lavoro. Poi scrivo tre righe con quello che ho capito e cosa resta fuori dallo scope, e mi faccio dire di sì prima di iniziare: costa cinque minuti e mi ha evitato più volte di costruire la cosa sbagliata. Se rimango bloccato su un dubbio, mi do un’ora e poi chiedo, ma arrivando con cosa ho provato e cosa penso che stia succedendo."
