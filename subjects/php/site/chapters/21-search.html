Search: Elasticsearch and Meilisearch

Every e-commerce and every gestionale reaches the day when LIKE '%term%' stops being acceptable. Knowing why it stops — and what shape the replacement has — separates you from the candidate whose answer is a bigger server. You do not need to have run an Elasticsearch cluster; you need to be able to explain an inverted index and name the three real options.

From the advert

Not in any advert. Search arrives as a ticket, not as a requirement: "la ricerca prodotti è lenta e non trova niente". It is the most common performance problem after the N+1, and the one least likely to be solved by an index on a column.

Why LIKE '%term%' fails, twice

🧒 Simple

An index is like a phone book: it helps you find things by how they start. Searching for a word in the middle means reading every entry. And even when it finishes, it only finds exactly what you typed — not a typo, not a plural, not a synonym.

🎓 Professional

Two independent failures, and both need saying:

Performance. A B-tree is ordered by prefix, so LIKE 'tra%' can use it and LIKE '%tra%' cannot — the leading wildcard makes the query non-sargable and forces a full scan (ch. 11). Add a couple of ORed columns and a JOIN, and a catalogue of 200 000 products takes seconds per keystroke.

Quality, which is the worse half. LIKE has no notion of relevance: everything either matches or does not, so results come back in whatever order the table felt like. It cannot handle a typo (trapno for trapano), stemming (trapani should match trapano), accents (perforatóre), word order, or synonyms. Users type two words and expect both to count. That is not something an index makes better — it is a different data structure.

⚠ Trap

Reaching for Elasticsearch first. Adding a distributed search cluster to a project with 40 000 products means a JVM to operate, a schema to maintain, an indexing pipeline that will drift out of sync with the database, and a new class of "the search index is stale" bug. MySQL full-text or Meilisearch would have solved it in an afternoon. In an interview, proposing the smallest thing that works and naming when you would escalate is a much stronger answer than naming the biggest tool you know.

The inverted index and the analyser

🧒 Simple

Instead of a list of documents each containing words, build a list of words each containing the documents it appears in. Then searching is a lookup, not a scan — and it is the same idea as the index at the back of a book.

🎓 Professional

Document → terms → posting list. Query time becomes a set intersection over small lists instead of a scan over the corpus, so cost scales with the number of matching documents rather than the size of the table.

The analyser is where the quality lives, and it is the part people skip. The same pipeline must run over the document at index time and over the query at search time, or they will never meet:

  1. Tokenise — split into terms.
  2. Lowercase and fold accents — so perforatóre finds perforatore. Non-negotiable for Italian.
  3. Remove stop words — il, di, per. Carefully: it destroys phrase searches for titles that are mostly stop words.
  4. Stem — reduce to a root, so trapani and trapano collapse together. Italian stemming is a real feature and a real requirement here.
  5. Synonyms — cacciavite / giravite, and every domain has a list the sales team already knows.

Relevance is then scored, classically with BM25: a term is worth more when it is rare across the corpus and frequent in this document, with a correction so long documents do not win by length alone. You do not need the formula — you need to know that scoring exists, that it is tunable, and that boosting a field (product name over description) is how you make the results feel right.

Typo tolerance is edit distance (Levenshtein) over the term dictionary; and facets — the counts beside each filter in a shop sidebar — come from aggregating the matching set, which is why they are nearly free in a search engine and expensive in SQL.

The three options, and how to choose

🧒 Simple

Start with what the database already gives you. Move to a small dedicated search engine when that stops being good enough. Reach for the big distributed one only when you actually need what it does.

🎓 Professional
  • MySQL/MariaDB FULLTEXT. Free, transactional, no extra service, no sync problem — the index is updated by the same transaction as the row. Supports natural-language and boolean modes and gives you a relevance score. Weaknesses: no typo tolerance, weak Italian stemming, awkward facets, and it competes with your OLTP workload. Genuinely sufficient for tens of thousands of rows, and it is the answer nobody expects you to give.
  • Meilisearch (or Typesense). A single small binary, typo tolerance and instant search out of the box, sane defaults, easy to operate. The sweet spot for a catalogue or a gestionale, and by far the most likely right answer for the projects in this market.
  • Elasticsearch / OpenSearch. When you need aggregations over large volumes, complex relevance tuning, multi-tenancy at scale, or you are also doing log analytics. Powerful and a genuine operational commitment — a JVM, memory tuning, cluster state.
  • Algolia when you would rather pay than operate. Excellent, priced per operation, and a real dependency on someone else’s availability.

In Laravel, Scout abstracts all of these behind one interface, which is the practical reason to start small: Searchable on the model, a driver in config, and changing your mind later is a configuration change rather than a rewrite.

Try it. The two ends of the range on the same problem. Run the first against any MySQL table with a fulltext index; the second is the Scout version of the same feature.

-- Option 1: the database you already have.
ALTER TABLE prodotti
  ADD FULLTEXT INDEX ft_prodotti (nome, descrizione);

-- Boolean mode: + requires, * is a prefix. The score comes back as a column,
-- so relevance ordering is free and you can boost by expression.
SELECT id, nome,
       MATCH(nome, descrizione) AGAINST ('trapano percussione' IN NATURAL LANGUAGE MODE) AS score
  FROM prodotti
 WHERE MATCH(nome, descrizione) AGAINST ('+trapano +percuss*' IN BOOLEAN MODE)
 ORDER BY score DESC
 LIMIT 20;

<?php declare(strict_types=1);
// Option 2: Laravel Scout over Meilisearch — swap the driver, not the code.

namespace App\Models;

use Illuminate\Database\Eloquent\Model;
use Laravel\Scout\Searchable;

final class Prodotto extends Model
{
    use Searchable;

    /** Index only what is searched or filtered — not the whole row. */
    public function toSearchableArray(): array
    {
        return [
            'id'         => (string) $this->id,
            'nome'       => $this->nome,
            'descrizione'=> $this->descrizione,
            'categoria'  => $this->categoria->nome,   // eager-loaded, or this is an N+1 per document
            'prezzo'     => $this->prezzo_cents,
            'attivo'     => $this->attivo,
        ];
    }

    /** Never index what must not be findable. */
    public function shouldBeSearchable(): bool
    {
        return $this->attivo && $this->deleted_at === null;
    }
}

// Usage: typo tolerance, filters and facets are the engine's job, not yours.

Keeping the index in sync, which is the real work

🧒 Simple

The moment search lives outside the database, you have two copies of the truth. Everything hard about search after the first week is keeping them from disagreeing.

🎓 Professional

Index on write, through model events, and do it on a queue — a synchronous call to the search engine inside a request couples your write path to its availability, so their outage becomes your outage (ch. 22). Scout does this for you with the queue driver enabled, and it is worth checking that it is.

The parts that bite:

  • Bulk updates bypass model events. A mass UPDATE from a query builder, a migration or a stored procedure changes rows without firing anything, and the index silently drifts. Re-import after any bulk operation.
  • Deletions and soft deletes. A soft-deleted record that stays in the index is a product customers can find and cannot buy — use shouldBeSearchable().
  • Reindexing must be online. Build into a new index and swap an alias; never delete the live index and rebuild it, unless the plan is for search to return nothing for eleven minutes.
  • Permissions. If some documents are restricted, filtering after retrieval breaks pagination and leaks the count. Filter inside the query, with the permission as an indexed field.
  • Log what users searched for and found nothing. It is the cheapest product-improvement data you will ever collect, and it is how the synonym list gets written.
Golden rules
  • LIKE '%x%' cannot use an index — and has no relevance, which is worse.
  • The analyser must be identical at index and query time. Accents and stemming, always, for Italian.
  • Start with FULLTEXT, escalate to Meilisearch, escalate to Elasticsearch only for a reason you can name.
  • Index only what is searched or filtered.
  • Index on a queue, or their outage is your outage.
  • Bulk updates skip model events. Re-import after them.
  • Reindex into a new index and swap the alias.
  • Log the zero-result searches. That is your synonym list.

"La ricerca prodotti è lenta e trova male. Cosa propone?"

Diagnose both halves, then propose the smallest thing: "Sono due problemi diversi. La lentezza è il LIKE '%...%': con il wildcard iniziale l’indice non si può usare e va in scansione completa. Ma il problema più grave è la qualità: LIKE non ha rilevanza, non tollera i refusi e non gestisce accenti e plurali, e in italiano servono tutti e tre. La soluzione è un indice invertito. Se il catalogo è di qualche decina di migliaia di righe partirei dal FULLTEXT di MySQL, che è gratis e resta dentro la transazione; se serve tolleranza ai refusi e faccette metterei Meilisearch dietro Scout, con l’indicizzazione su coda. Elasticsearch lo terrei per quando servono davvero aggregazioni su grandi volumi — è un servizio in più da gestire."
