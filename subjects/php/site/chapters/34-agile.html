Agile, Scrum and the daily rhythm

Every advert says metodologie Agile and most companies mean "we have a stand-up and a board". That is not a criticism, and pretending otherwise in an interview is a mistake — the useful position is to know what each ceremony is for, so you can tell when one has degenerated and contribute rather than complain.

From the advert

"Capacità di lavorare in team con metodologie Agile". Translated: you can be interrupted, you can estimate badly in public without taking it personally, and you say when something is stuck before the day it was due.

The ceremonies, and what each is actually for

🧒 Simple

Scrum is a small set of recurring meetings and roles. Each one exists to answer a specific question, and when a team forgets the question the meeting turns into a ritual everybody resents.

🎓 Professional
  • Daily stand-up — 15 minutes, and the real question is "is anything blocked?". It is coordination, not reporting to a manager. The failure mode is a status round-robin where each person recites yesterday to the scrum master; the fix is to talk to the board rather than to a person, and to take detail offline.
  • Sprint planning — agreeing what the team commits to and, crucially, what "done" means for each item. Weak planning is where most sprint failures are actually caused.
  • Refinement (grooming) — the most valuable meeting and the first one cancelled when things get busy. This is where a vague request becomes a task with acceptance criteria, and where you ask the questions that would otherwise be discovered mid-implementation (ch. 35).
  • Review / demo — show working software to the people who asked for it. The point is feedback, not applause.
  • Retrospective — the only ceremony that changes how the team works. A retro that produces no action item is a complaint session; one action, with an owner, per retro is enough.

Roles, briefly: the Product Owner owns priority and the "why"; the Scrum Master owns the process and removes impediments; the team owns the "how" and the estimate. In small Italian software houses these are frequently the same two people wearing several hats, and that is fine as long as somebody is genuinely deciding priority.

Kanban is the alternative worth naming: continuous flow, WIP limits, no sprints. It fits support-heavy and agency work far better than Scrum — interrupt-driven work destroys sprint commitments (ch. 25b) — and many teams end up with "Scrumban", which is a sensible destination rather than a failure.

Estimation, which is where trust is won or lost

🧒 Simple

Estimates are guesses about work you have not done yet, so they will be wrong. What matters is not being right — it is saying early when the guess turns out to be wrong.

🎓 Professional

Story points measure size and uncertainty, not hours, which is why they are relative: this is about twice that. Their only legitimate use is capacity forecasting through velocity over several sprints. The moment points are converted to hours or compared between teams, they become a performance metric and everybody starts inflating them — and being able to explain why that happens is a very good answer to a question about estimation.

Practical habits:

  • Estimate as a range or a confidence: "two days if the API is documented, five if I have to reverse-engineer it". That is more honest and more useful than a single number, and it surfaces the risk that actually decides the outcome.
  • Split anything above a few days. Large estimates are not estimates, they are hopes; splitting forces the unknowns into the open where somebody may already know the answer.
  • Include the parts people forget: code review, testing, deployment, the migration, writing the documentation. A "two-day" feature that needs a schema change is not a two-day feature (ch. 29).
  • Raise it the day you know, not the day it was due. A day-three warning is a scheduling conversation; a day-ten surprise is a credibility problem. This single habit does more for how you are perceived than the accuracy of any estimate.
  • A spike — a timeboxed investigation — is the right response to "I cannot estimate this". Ask for two days to find out, then estimate.
⚠ Trap

Padding silently. Doubling every estimate in your head protects you once and destroys the information the team is trying to build — and it is quickly noticed, because your tasks always finish exactly on time. Estimate what you actually believe, name the risks separately, and let the person planning add the buffer. That is their job, and it is done at the level of the plan, not hidden inside each ticket.

Tickets, definition of done, and the board

🧒 Simple

A ticket should say what someone needs and how you will know it is finished. If it says only "fix the orders page", the argument about whether it is done has merely been postponed.

🎓 Professional

The user-story form — come [ruolo] voglio [azione] per [beneficio] — is useful precisely because of the third clause: the benefit is what lets you propose a cheaper solution that achieves the same thing. INVEST is the checklist worth remembering (independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, testable), and testable is the one that does the work: if you cannot describe how to verify it, it is not ready to start.

Acceptance criteria beat prose. Given/When/Then, or a plain checklist — what matters is that "done" was agreed before implementation rather than negotiated after it.

Definition of Done is the team-wide bar that applies to every ticket: reviewed, tested, documented, deployed to staging, no new PHPStan errors (ch. 30). Writing it down once converts a hundred future arguments into a link.

On the board: WIP limits are the highest-value practice in this chapter. Three things at 80% finished are worth nothing; one finished thing is worth something. Starting a new ticket because yours is blocked is how a team ends up with eleven open branches and no releases, and "finish before you start" is a phrase that lands well with anyone who has run a team.

Try it. Not code — rewrite one of your own tasks. This is the difference between a ticket that generates three interruptions and one that can be picked up by anybody.

Agile as it actually is, and how to talk about it

🧒 Simple

Most companies do a partial version. The right attitude is neither cynicism nor purism: work with what exists, and improve one thing at a time from the inside.

🎓 Professional

Recognise the common degenerations so you are not surprised by them: a stand-up that is a status report to a manager; sprints with scope added mid-sprint; velocity used as a productivity target (which guarantees inflation); "we are agile" meaning there is no documentation and no plan; and a retro whose action items never happen.

In an agency the deeper tension is real and worth naming: clients buy fixed-price, fixed-scope projects, and that is the opposite of agile by construction. Teams cope with a fixed budget and a negotiable scope, or with change requests. Understanding that tension makes you far more useful in the estimation conversation than someone quoting the manifesto.

How to answer the interview question. Never say "we did agile properly" — it sounds like a criticism of them. Describe your actual rhythm concretely, name what worked and one thing that did not, and ask what their process looks like. Curiosity about their process reads as someone who will fit into it.

Golden rules
  • Stand-up asks "is anything blocked". It is not a status report.
  • Refinement is the meeting to protect. It is where vague becomes doable.
  • Points are size and uncertainty, never hours, never a target.
  • Estimate as a range and name the risk.
  • Raise the slip the day you know it. The single highest-trust habit.
  • Never pad silently. The buffer belongs to the plan, not the ticket.
  • Testable acceptance criteria, agreed before starting.
  • Finish before you start. WIP limits over multitasking.

"Come lavorava nel team precedente? Usavate Scrum?"

Concrete, honest, and curious about theirs: "Sprint di due settimane, stand-up quotidiano, refinement il mercoledì e retro alla fine. La cosa che funzionava meglio era il refinement: è lì che una richiesta vaga diventava un task con criteri di accettazione, e ci ha risparmiato parecchie reimplementazioni. Quella che funzionava peggio erano le stime, perché all’inizio non contavo review e deploy: ho preso l’abitudine di stimare a intervallo — 'due giorni se l’API è documentata, cinque se devo capirla da solo' — e soprattutto di dire subito quando sto sforando, il giorno che lo capisco e non il giorno della scadenza. Voi come siete organizzati? Avete sprint o lavorate a flusso continuo con l’assistenza?" That last question is not filler: the answer tells you whether the job is Scrum or Kanban in practice, which is genuinely worth knowing.
