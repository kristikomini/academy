Microservices — and when a monolith wins

This gets asked in interviews far more often than it gets built. That is exactly why it is worth preparing: the candidate who answers "microservizi" to everything sounds like someone repeating a conference talk, and the one who can say precisely when not to distribute sounds senior. The honest position — a well-structured monolith first, distribution when a specific force requires it — is also the correct one for almost every project in this market.

From the advert

Not in any advert. No PHP posting in Emilia-Romagna asks for microservices. Several interviewers ask about them anyway, usually to find out whether you can reason about a trade-off or only recite an architecture.

What distribution actually buys

🧒 Simple

Splitting one application into several lets different teams work and deploy without waiting for each other, and lets you give more machines to the one part that needs them. Those are the two real reasons. Everything else on the list is usually available without splitting anything.

🎓 Professional

The benefits that survive scrutiny:

  • Independent deployment. The genuine one. Five teams shipping without a shared release train is a real organisational gain — and note that it is an organisational gain, which is why Conway’s law is the honest framing: the architecture that works is the one that matches how the teams are actually split.
  • Independent scaling. The PDF renderer needs eight instances and the admin panel needs one. Real, though a queue with dedicated workers (ch. 22) delivers most of it inside a monolith.
  • Fault isolation — if you build it in. Without timeouts, circuit breakers and fallbacks, distribution converts one service’s outage into everyone’s.
  • Technology choice per service. Occasionally decisive — a Python service for a model — and frequently an excuse for a rewrite in a language somebody wanted to try.

The one that does not survive: "it will be faster". An in-process function call becomes a network round trip with serialisation, and page latency goes up, not down.

What it costs, stated honestly

🧒 Simple

Once the pieces talk over a network, everything that used to be guaranteed becomes something you have to handle: the call can fail, arrive twice, or arrive in the wrong order — and no single database transaction covers two services.

🎓 Professional

The costs, in the order they hurt:

  • You lose the transaction. This is the big one. "Create the order and reserve the stock" is one COMMIT in a monolith and a distributed consistency problem across two services. The answer is a saga with compensating actions — and a compensating action for "the payment succeeded but stock reservation failed" is a business process, not code.
  • You gain eventual consistency whether or not you wanted it. The customer updates their address and the shipping service has not heard yet. That is now a product decision someone must make.
  • Debugging becomes archaeology. One user action spans four services and four log files. Distributed tracing and a correlation id stop being nice-to-have and become the minimum viable operations (ch. 25).
  • Testing gets much harder. Integration testing across services needs contract tests, or you find the breakage in production.
  • The operational surface multiplies. Eight deployment pipelines, eight sets of secrets, eight dashboards, service discovery, versioned APIs between them all.
  • The eight fallacies of distributed computing apply in full: the network is not reliable, latency is not zero, bandwidth is not infinite, the network is not secure.
⚠ Trap

The distributed monolith — the worst outcome available, and the most common. Services split by technical layer instead of by business capability, so every user action needs four of them, they share one database, and they must be deployed together. You have paid every cost of distribution and bought none of the benefits. The diagnostic question is simple: can each service be deployed on its own, without coordinating? If not, it is one system wearing several containers.

The modular monolith, which is usually the answer

🧒 Simple

There is a middle option people forget: one application, one deployment, but organised inside into clear modules with real boundaries. You get most of the tidiness and none of the network.

🎓 Professional

A modular monolith keeps modules (Ordini, Magazzino, Fatturazione) that communicate through published interfaces or in-process events, each owning its tables, with no module reaching into another’s. It ships as one artefact and one transaction.

The critical part is that the boundaries are enforced, not documented. In PHP that is deptrac or phparkitect in CI, failing the build when Fatturazione imports a class from Magazzino\Internal (ch. 23, 30). Without enforcement the modules erode in a quarter, which is how every ball of mud started.

This is also the strategically right move if you do expect to split later: getting the boundaries right is the hard part, and doing it in-process means a mistaken boundary is a refactor rather than a migration. Extract a module into a service when a specific force demands it — a team that needs to deploy independently, a component with a genuinely different scaling profile, a compliance boundary. One force, one service, deliberately.

Try it. Boundaries as a build failure rather than a convention. This is the single highest-value file in a modular monolith, and it is about fifteen lines.

# deptrac.yaml — run in CI: vendor/bin/deptrac analyse
parameters:
  paths:
    - ./src

  layers:
    - name: Ordini
      collectors:
        - type: directory
          value: src/Ordini/.*
    - name: Magazzino
      collectors:
        - type: directory
          value: src/Magazzino/.*
    - name: MagazzinoApi          # the only part of Magazzino others may touch
      collectors:
        - type: directory
          value: src/Magazzino/Api/.*
    - name: Fatturazione
      collectors:
        - type: directory
          value: src/Fatturazione/.*

  ruleset:
    # Ordini may use the published API of Magazzino — and nothing else inside it.
    Ordini:
      - MagazzinoApi
    Fatturazione:
      - MagazzinoApi
    # Magazzino depends on nobody: it is downstream of everything.
    Magazzino: ~
    MagazzinoApi: ~

Answering the question well

🧒 Simple

The interviewer is not asking whether you can define a microservice. They are asking whether you decide with reasons. So answer with the conditions, not with a preference.

🎓 Professional

A structure that works: name the default, name the forces that would change it, name the cost you would be accepting.

It also helps to know the counter-examples, because they show you have read past the marketing: several well-known engineering organisations have publicly consolidated services back into monoliths and reported large cost and latency improvements. The lesson is not "monoliths won" — it is that the right granularity is a function of the organisation and the workload, and it changes over time in both directions.

Finally, be careful with a company’s own architecture. If they have microservices and you say the word "sbagliato", the interview is over. "Capisco perché l’avete scelto" followed by a question about how they handle consistency across services is the same knowledge, delivered as curiosity.

Golden rules
  • Independent deployment is the real benefit, and it is organisational.
  • Distribution never makes a page faster. It adds a network hop.
  • Losing the transaction is the biggest cost. A saga needs a business decision, not just code.
  • Split by business capability, never by technical layer.
  • If they cannot deploy separately, it is a distributed monolith.
  • Modular monolith first, with boundaries enforced in CI.
  • One force, one service. Extract for a named reason.
  • Never call their architecture wrong. Ask how they handle consistency instead.

"Userebbe i microservizi per questo progetto?"

Default, forces, cost — in that order: "Di default no, partirei da un monolite modulare: moduli con confini veri, ognuno padrone delle sue tabelle, e i confini li faccio rispettare da deptrac in CI, non da una convenzione. Così mantengo la transazione singola, che è la cosa che si perde per prima quando si distribuisce — 'crea l’ordine e impegna la merce' passa da un COMMIT a una saga con azioni di compensazione, e quella è una decisione di business, non tecnica. Estrarrei un servizio quando c’è una forza precisa: un team che deve rilasciare in autonomia, o un componente con un profilo di scalabilità davvero diverso. Il rischio peggiore è il monolite distribuito: se i servizi vanno rilasciati insieme, ho pagato tutti i costi senza nessun vantaggio."
