Event-driven architecture: the shape

Choreography and orchestration, what coupling you actually remove, and what you take on in exchange. The adverts ask for “a solid understanding of distributed systems and event-driven architecture” — this is what that sentence means.

From the advert

“a solid understanding of distributed systems and event-driven architecture”, from the aggregated Kafka-developer requirement lines.

What it actually removes

🧒 Simple

With a direct call, the caller must know who to call, and both must be up at the same time. With events, the publisher announces what happened and does not know or care who listens.

🎓 Professional

Be precise about which couplings go and which stay. It removes address coupling (the producer does not know its consumers) and temporal coupling (they need not be available simultaneously). It does not remove schema coupling — everyone still agrees on the event’s shape, which is why chapters 20 and 21 exist.

The practical benefit is that adding a consumer requires no change to the producer. A new service wanting order events subscribes; nobody deploys anything else. That is the thing worth naming, because it is what actually changes how a team works.

Choreography or orchestration

🧒 Simple

Either each service reacts to events and the process emerges from their reactions (choreography), or one component tells each service what to do in turn (orchestration).

🎓 Professional

Choreography keeps services independent and makes the overall process invisible: it exists only as the sum of reactions, so nobody can read it in one place and debugging “why did this order not ship” means correlating logs across five services. Orchestration puts the process in one component that is readable and testable, at the cost of a coordinator that knows about everyone and becomes a change bottleneck.

The useful rule: choreography for notification, orchestration for a business process with a defined outcome. “An order was placed” broadcast to whoever cares is choreography; “take payment, reserve stock, book shipping, and compensate if any step fails” is a process with an outcome somebody must own, and that is chapter 40’s saga.

⚠ Trap

Pure choreography past three or four services produces a system nobody can describe. The test is simple: ask someone to draw the order flow on a whiteboard. If they cannot, the process is implicit, and every change becomes an archaeology exercise. That is the point at which introducing an orchestrator is a correction, not a retreat.

What you take on

🧒 Simple

Everything becomes eventually consistent, failures happen elsewhere and later, and testing means running several services.

🎓 Professional

Eventual consistency is the big one and it is a product decision, not a technical one: after placing an order, the customer’s order list may not show it for a second, and somebody has to decide whether that is acceptable and how the UI handles it. Debugging requires distributed tracing (Java chapter 35) or it is guesswork. Testing needs contract tests and Testcontainers with a real broker, because a mocked producer proves nothing about the contract.

So the honest answer to “should we go event-driven?” is the same shape as the microservices answer in Java chapter 30: it buys decoupling and independent evolution, it charges you consistency and observability, and it is worth it when you have several consumers of the same facts — not because it is modern.

Golden rules
  • It removes address and temporal coupling, not schema coupling.
  • Adding a consumer requires no producer change. That is the real benefit.
  • Choreography for notification, orchestration for a process with an outcome.
  • If nobody can draw the flow, the process is implicit. Time for an orchestrator.
  • Eventual consistency is a product decision, and the UI has to handle it.
  • Distributed tracing is not optional once the failure happens elsewhere.

“Quando sceglierebbe un’architettura event-driven?”

“Quando più consumatori sono interessati agli stessi fatti e non voglio che il produttore li conosca: così aggiungere un servizio che ascolta gli ordini non richiede di toccare chi li produce. Toglie l’accoppiamento sull’indirizzo e quello temporale, ma non quello sullo schema — lì serve un registry con regole di compatibilità. Il prezzo è la consistenza eventuale, che è una decisione di prodotto prima che tecnica, e il fatto che senza tracing distribuito il debugging diventa archeologia. E terrei la coreografia per le notifiche: quando c’è un processo con un esito da garantire preferisco un orchestratore, perché il processo deve poter essere letto in un posto solo.”
