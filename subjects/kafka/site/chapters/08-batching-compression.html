Batching, linger, compression and throughput

Three settings account for most producer performance work, and they are a single explicit trade-off between latency and throughput — which is what makes this a favourite interview topic: there is a right answer only once you have asked what the system is for.

From the advert

Not in any advert. And the first thing asked when someone says they have “tuned Kafka”.

batch.size and linger.ms

🧒 Simple

The producer groups records going to the same partition into a batch. Bigger batches mean less overhead per record.

linger.ms is how long it is willing to wait for more records before sending what it has.

🎓 Professional

batch.size is a byte ceiling per partition batch; linger.ms is a time ceiling. The batch is sent when either is reached. The default linger.ms=0 does not mean no batching — records that accumulate while the previous request is in flight still batch together — but it means the producer never deliberately waits.

Setting linger.ms to 5–20 ms is usually a large throughput win for a small, bounded latency cost, because fewer, larger requests mean less per-request overhead on both sides and better compression ratios. This is the tuning change with the best effort-to-benefit ratio in Kafka.

Compression

🧒 Simple

Batches can be compressed before they are sent. Less network, less disk — at the cost of some CPU.

And the broker usually stores them still compressed, so the saving lasts.

🎓 Professional

Compression applies to the whole batch, which is why it works well — and why it works better with larger batches, so linger.ms and compression reinforce each other. lz4 and snappy are fast with moderate ratios; zstd gives noticeably better ratios at somewhat more CPU and is usually the right default now; gzip is slow and rarely the right choice.

The broker stores the compressed batch as received and serves it compressed, so the saving extends to disk and to consumer network. That only holds while the format is unchanged: if the broker has to recompress — a topic-level compression type differing from the producer’s, or an old message format — it decompresses and recompresses every batch, which is a large and invisible CPU cost.

⚠ Trap

A larger batch.size does nothing on its own when traffic is low: with linger.ms=0 and a trickle of records, batches are sent immediately and remain tiny. People raise batch.size, see no change, and conclude the setting does not work. It is the pair that matters.

Which end of the trade-off you are on

🧒 Simple

There is no fast setting. There is a setting for “answer quickly” and a setting for “move a lot”, and you have to know which you need.

🎓 Professional

For a user-facing action where a person is waiting, keep linger.ms low and accept smaller batches. For pipelines, telemetry, CDC and anything where nobody is watching an individual record, raise linger.ms and batch.size, enable zstd, and let throughput win.

Measure rather than guess: producer-side record-send-rate, batch-size-avg, compression-rate-avg and request-latency-avg tell you immediately whether batches are actually filling. A batch-size-avg far below batch.size means linger.ms is your limiting factor, not the ceiling.

Golden rules
  • batch.size is bytes, linger.ms is time. Either one triggers a send.
  • linger.ms=0 still batches — it just never waits on purpose.
  • 5–20 ms of linger is usually a large win for a bounded latency cost.
  • Compression is per batch, so bigger batches compress better. zstd by default.
  • Mismatched compression forces broker recompression. Invisible, expensive.
  • Check batch-size-avg. Far below the ceiling means linger is the limit.

“Come aumenterebbe il throughput di un producer?”

Ask about latency first — committing without asking is the wrong answer: “Prima chiederei quanta latenza posso permettermi, perché è esattamente lo scambio. Se è una pipeline dove nessuno aspetta il singolo record, alzo linger.ms a una decina di millisecondi e batch.size, e attivo la compressione zstd: batch più grandi significano meno overhead per richiesta e anche un rapporto di compressione migliore, perché si comprime il batch intero. Poi guardo batch-size-avg: se è molto sotto il batch.size configurato, il limite è il linger e non la dimensione.”
