Monitoring with Prometheus and Grafana

The metrics that matter out of the hundreds Kafka exposes, how to get them, and why the alert that wakes you should be about lag rather than about CPU.

From the advert

“monitoring systems (e.g., Prometheus, Grafana)”, from the aggregated Kafka-developer requirement lines.

Getting the metrics out

🧒 Simple

Kafka publishes its metrics through JMX, a Java mechanism. An exporter translates those into the format Prometheus can scrape.

🎓 Professional

Brokers and clients expose JMX MBeans; the JMX Exporter runs as a Java agent inside the broker and publishes a Prometheus endpoint. For consumer lag specifically, prefer a dedicated exporter — Kafka Lag Exporter or Burrow — because it reads __consumer_offsets centrally and reports lag for every group, including applications that do not export their own metrics.

Client-side metrics matter as much as broker ones and are often forgotten. In Spring, Micrometer binds Kafka client metrics automatically, which gives you producer batch sizes and consumer fetch behaviour alongside your application’s own metrics — and those are what you need for chapter 08’s tuning questions.

The metrics worth a dashboard

🧒 Simple

A handful of numbers tell you almost everything: how far behind consumers are, whether replicas are keeping up, and whether anything is failing.

🎓 Professional

Consumer lag, per group and per partition — the single most informative number (chapter 14). Under-replicated partitions: should be zero, and anything else means the ISR has shrunk and your durability guarantee is not what you configured (chapter 04). Offline partitions: should be zero, and non-zero means data is unavailable right now. Active controller count: should be exactly one across the cluster.

Then the ones that explain a problem rather than announce it: request latency by type (produce and fetch), broker disk usage and its trend against retention, producer record-error-rate, and consumer rebalance-rate — which is how you see the rebalance loop from chapter 13 as a picture rather than as a log line.

Try it. Four alerting rules that cover most of what will actually page you. Note that the lag rule alerts on growth, not on a threshold.

groups:
  - name: kafka
    rules:
      - alert: UnderReplicatedPartitions          # durability is not what you configured
        expr: kafka_server_replicamanager_underreplicatedpartitions > 0
        for: 5m

      - alert: OfflinePartitions                  # data is unavailable now
        expr: kafka_controller_kafkacontroller_offlinepartitionscount > 0
        for: 1m

      - alert: NoActiveController
        expr: sum(kafka_controller_kafkacontroller_activecontrollercount) != 1
        for: 2m

      - alert: ConsumerLagGrowing                 # trend, not threshold
        expr: delta(kafka_consumergroup_lag[15m]) > 0
             and kafka_consumergroup_lag > 1000
        for: 15m
        annotations:
          summary: "{{ $labels.consumergroup }} is falling behind on {{ $labels.topic }}"

What should wake somebody

🧒 Simple

Alert on the things a user would notice, and on the things that mean you are about to lose data. Not on CPU.

🎓 Professional

Page on: offline partitions, no active controller, under-replicated partitions that persist, and consumer lag growing steadily on a consumer that matters. Those are either “something is broken now” or “your durability is degraded”.

Do not page on high CPU, high disk I/O or high message rate — those are Kafka working. And prefer time-based lag where the exporter supports it: “billing is twenty minutes behind” is actionable and comprehensible to everyone, while “lag is 847,000” means nothing without knowing the rate. This is the Kafka instance of the general rule in Java chapter 35.

⚠ Trap

A consumer group with no lag at all is not necessarily healthy — it may have stopped consuming entirely, in which case lag stops growing because nothing is being produced to it either, or because the group has disappeared and is no longer reported. Alert on a group vanishing from the metrics as well as on its lag, or a dead consumer looks exactly like a well-behaved one.

Golden rules
  • JMX exporter for brokers, a lag exporter for groups. The second covers apps that export nothing.
  • Under-replicated and offline partitions should be zero; active controllers exactly one.
  • Lag per group and per partition is the most informative number you have.
  • Alert on symptoms and durability, never on CPU or message rate.
  • Time-based lag beats record counts. “Twenty minutes behind” is actionable.
  • A group that disappears looks healthy. Alert on its absence too.

“Cosa monitora su un cluster Kafka?”

“Tre cose che devono stare a zero o a uno: under-replicated partitions a zero, perché se l’ISR si è ridotto la durabilità non è quella che ho configurato; offline partitions a zero; e un solo controller attivo. Poi il lag per gruppo e per partizione, che è la metrica più informativa perché è end-to-end. Allarmo sulla crescita sostenuta del lag, meglio ancora sul tempo di ritardo, non su una soglia di record: dire ‘billing è venti minuti indietro’ lo capiscono tutti. Su CPU e message rate non allarmo: quello è Kafka che lavora.”
