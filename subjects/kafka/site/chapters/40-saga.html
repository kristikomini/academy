Sagas and distributed consistency

Long-running business processes across services, without a distributed lock. The follow-up question to microservices in every architecture interview: “and how do you keep them consistent?”

From the advert

Not in any advert. Implied by every posting that mentions microservices and event-driven architecture, because the moment a process spans two services the transaction is gone.

Why not just a distributed transaction

🧒 Simple

In one database, a transaction makes several changes happen together or not at all. Across services, there is no such thing you would actually want to use.

🎓 Professional

Two-phase commit exists and is avoided for good reasons: it holds locks across services for the duration, so the slowest participant sets everyone’s latency; it blocks if the coordinator fails at the wrong moment; and it requires every participant to support it, which Kafka and most modern services do not.

A saga replaces atomicity with a sequence of local transactions, each with a compensating action that semantically undoes it. There is no rollback — there is a refund. That distinction is the heart of the pattern: compensation is a business operation, not a technical one, and it is visible to the customer.

Choreographed or orchestrated

🧒 Simple

Either each service reacts to the previous one’s event, or one coordinator drives the steps in order.

🎓 Professional

Choreographed: payment listens for OrderPlaced, stock listens for PaymentTaken, and so on. No extra component, and the process exists nowhere — which past three steps means nobody can answer “where did this order get stuck?” without reading five services.

Orchestrated: a saga coordinator holds the state machine, sends commands and reacts to replies. The process is readable, testable and observable in one place, at the cost of a component that knows the whole flow. For anything with a defined business outcome — which a saga by definition has — orchestration is usually right, and saying so confidently is the answer interviewers are looking for (chapter 37).

⚠ Trap

Some steps cannot be compensated. You cannot un-send an email, un-ship a parcel, or un-tell a customer. Order the saga so irreversible steps come last, after everything that might fail has already succeeded. A saga that sends the confirmation email in step two and fails in step four has no way back.

Making one work

🧒 Simple

Every step must tolerate being retried, the state must be stored somewhere durable, and somebody must handle the case where compensation itself fails.

🎓 Professional

Requirements, in order of how often they are forgotten: every step and every compensation is idempotent (chapter 16), because retries are certain; saga state is persisted, not held in memory, so a crash resumes rather than abandons; every step has a timeout and a decision about what timing out means; and a failed compensation goes to a manual intervention queue, because at that point automation has run out and a human must look.

That last point is the mark of a realistic answer. Sagas do not make failure impossible; they make it bounded and visible. A design with no story for “compensation also failed” is incomplete, and interviewers notice when you volunteer it.

Golden rules
  • Two-phase commit holds locks across services and blocks on coordinator failure.
  • A saga is local transactions plus compensating actions. A refund, not a rollback.
  • Orchestration for a process with a defined outcome. Choreography hides it.
  • Put irreversible steps last. You cannot un-send an email.
  • Every step and every compensation is idempotent, and the state is persisted.
  • Have a manual queue for failed compensation. That is where automation ends.

“Come gestisce una transazione che attraversa tre microservizi?”

“Non con una transazione distribuita: il two-phase commit tiene lock tra servizi e si blocca se cade il coordinatore, e comunque Kafka non lo supporta. Uso una saga: una sequenza di transazioni locali, ognuna con un’azione compensativa che la annulla dal punto di vista del business — non un rollback, un rimborso. Con tre passi o più la faccio orchestrata, con una macchina a stati in un posto solo, perché in coreografia il processo non esiste da nessuna parte e nessuno sa dire dove si è fermato un ordine. Ogni passo e ogni compensazione devono essere idempotenti, lo stato della saga va persistito, e i passi irreversibili — una mail, una spedizione — li metto per ultimi. E se fallisce anche la compensazione, finisce in una coda di intervento manuale: lì l’automazione è finita.”
