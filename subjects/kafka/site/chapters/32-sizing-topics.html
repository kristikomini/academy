Sizing: partitions, throughput and how many is too many

Choosing a partition count you can live with, and the cost of getting it wrong in each direction. It is easy to increase and impossible to decrease, and increasing it breaks ordering — so it deserves a decision rather than a default.

From the advert

Not in any advert. And the first real design question on any new topic, with consequences that outlast the person who chose the number.

Working out the number

🧒 Simple

Start from how much you need to process and how much one consumer can handle. Divide, then leave room to grow.

🎓 Professional

The usable estimate is partitions = max(target throughput / per-consumer throughput, target throughput / per-partition write throughput), with headroom. In practice the consumer side dominates: measure what one instance of your consumer can actually process per second, then divide the peak you need to sustain.

Leave headroom deliberately, because the partition count is the ceiling on consumer parallelism (chapter 11) and you would rather not raise it later. Doubling your estimate is cheap at these scales; discovering the ceiling during a traffic peak is not.

What too many costs

🧒 Simple

Partitions are not free. Each one is files on disk, memory in the brokers and the clients, and work during a rebalance or a failover.

🎓 Professional

Costs scale with the total partition count across the cluster: open file handles and memory per replica on brokers, per-partition buffers in producers and fetch state in consumers, longer leader elections when a broker fails, and longer rebalances (chapter 13). KRaft (chapter 05) raised the practical ceiling considerably, but it is a ceiling, not an absence of cost.

Sensible shape: a few dozen partitions on a busy topic is ordinary; tens of thousands across a cluster wants a reason and a plan. And a topic with 50 partitions receiving three records a second is pure overhead — over-partitioning a low-volume topic is as much a mistake as under-partitioning a busy one, just a cheaper one.

⚠ Trap

You cannot reduce a topic’s partition count — there is no operation for it, and the only route is producing into a new topic and migrating every consumer. And increasing it rehashes keys, so per-key ordering across the change is lost permanently (chapter 02). Both directions are painful, which is exactly why the initial number deserves ten minutes of thought.

How many topics, and naming

🧒 Simple

The other sizing question: one topic per event type, or one topic carrying several? And what do you call them?

🎓 Professional

A topic per event type is the common default and gives independent retention, schemas and access control. Grouping several related types onto one topic is the right call when their relative order matters — an order’s placed, paid and shipped events belong together, keyed by order id, because splitting them across topics discards the ordering guarantee you needed (chapter 18).

Name topics with a convention decided once: something like <domain>.<entity>.<event> — sales.order.placed. Include the environment only if one cluster genuinely serves several, and prefer separate clusters. Names are effectively permanent, since renaming means migrating every producer and consumer.

Golden rules
  • Size from measured per-consumer throughput, then add headroom.
  • Partition count caps consumer parallelism. It is a ceiling you will meet.
  • You cannot reduce the count, and increasing it breaks per-key ordering.
  • Partitions cost file handles, memory, election and rebalance time.
  • Group event types on one topic when their relative order matters.
  • Decide a naming convention once. Topic names are effectively permanent.

“Quante partizioni per questo topic?”

“Parto da una misura, non da un numero: quanto riesce a elaborare un’istanza del consumer al secondo, e quale picco devo sostenere. Il rapporto mi dà il minimo, e ci lascio margine, perché il numero di partizioni è il tetto al parallelismo dei consumer. Margine però ragionevole: ogni partizione costa file aperti, memoria sui broker e sui client, e tempo durante ribilanciamenti ed elezioni. Il motivo per pensarci prima è che è una decisione poco reversibile: aumentare le partizioni riassegna le chiavi e rompe l’ordinamento per quelle esistenti, e diminuirle non si può proprio.”
