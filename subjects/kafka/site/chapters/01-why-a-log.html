Part 1 — The log, and why it is a log › Chapter 01

Why a log, and not a queue

One idea, and everything else in this course is a consequence of it. Kafka is an append-only log that readers track their own position in. Partitions, offsets, consumer groups, replay and every delivery guarantee follow from that sentence — they are not separate features to memorise.

From the advert

Not in any advert. And nothing else in Kafka makes sense without it. A candidate who describes Kafka as “a message queue” has already answered the interviewer’s real question.

The difference that decides everything

🧒 Simple

A queue is a basket of letters. You take one out and it is gone — nobody else can read it, and neither can you tomorrow.

Kafka is a diary that is only ever written at the end. Reading page 40 does not tear it out. Everyone reading keeps their own bookmark, so two people can read at different speeds — and anyone can move their bookmark back to page 1 and read the whole thing again.

🎓 Professional

In a traditional broker, delivery is destructive: the broker tracks which messages have been consumed and removes them. In Kafka the broker tracks almost nothing about consumers — it stores an ordered, immutable sequence of records and retains them for a configured time or size. Each consumer stores its own position.

Three consequences fall straight out. Reading is not destroying, so several independent consumers read the same data without coordinating. Consumers can be slow without back-pressuring the producer, because they are only falling behind a position. And replay is an ordinary operation: reset the position and reprocess, which is how you rebuild a downstream projection after finding a bug.

Why it is fast

🧒 Simple

Because it only ever adds to the end of a file, and never searches through the middle, the disk does the one thing disks are good at.

🎓 Professional

Appending to a file sequentially is dramatically faster than random access — on spinning disks by orders of magnitude, and still substantially on SSDs. Kafka leans on this: writes are appends, reads are sequential scans from an offset, and the operating system’s page cache does most of the caching, so a consumer reading recent data usually never touches the disk at all.

It also uses zero-copy (sendfile) to move bytes from the page cache to the network without passing through the application, and batches aggressively on both sides. The design choice worth naming: Kafka is fast because it does less — no per-message index, no per-consumer state, no in-place deletion.

What this buys, and what it costs

🧒 Simple

You get the ability to go back in time, and several readers of the same data. You give up per-message routing and per-message acknowledgement.

🎓 Professional

The model is excellent for event streams read by several independent consumers, for high throughput, and for anything where replay matters — rebuilding a cache, backfilling a new service, correcting a downstream bug by reprocessing history.

It is a poor fit for what a queue does well: routing individual messages by attribute, acknowledging or rejecting one message without affecting its neighbours, per-message priority, and delayed delivery. Kafka can be bent into those shapes and the result is uncomfortable. If what you need is “each job to exactly one worker, with a retry count and a dead-letter queue”, RabbitMQ is the honest answer — and saying so in an interview is a mark of judgement, not of ignorance.

⚠ Trap

“Kafka is a message queue” is the answer that ends the conversation. So is using it as one: a topic per consumer, one partition, every message acknowledged individually. That configuration pays Kafka’s operational cost for none of its benefits, and an interviewer who sees it in your description of a past project will ask why.

Golden rules
  • An append-only log, and each consumer owns its position. Everything follows from that.
  • Reading is not destroying. Many independent consumers, no coordination.
  • Replay is an ordinary operation, not a recovery procedure.
  • Fast because it does less — sequential appends, page cache, zero-copy, batching.
  • No per-message routing, priority or individual ack. Those are queue features.
  • If you need a queue, say so. Bending Kafka into one costs and buys nothing.

“Che differenza c’è tra Kafka e una coda tradizionale?”

This is the question, and the answer is one idea followed by its consequences: “Una coda consegna in modo distruttivo: il broker tiene traccia di cosa è stato consumato e lo rimuove. Kafka è un log append-only con retention: il broker non sa quasi nulla dei consumer, è ogni consumer a tenere il proprio offset. Da qui discende tutto — leggere non distrugge, quindi più consumer indipendenti leggono gli stessi dati senza coordinarsi; un consumer lento resta indietro senza bloccare il producer; e posso riportare indietro l’offset e rielaborare la storia, che è il motivo principale per cui si sceglie Kafka.”
