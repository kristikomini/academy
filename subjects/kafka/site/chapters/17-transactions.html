Kafka transactions and read-committed

The transactional producer, consume–transform–produce, and the boundary that exactly-once does not cross. The limit is the part worth knowing, because it is what decides whether transactions solve your problem or only look like they do.

From the advert

Not in any advert. It is the mechanism behind the exactly-once claim in chapter 15, and the place where that claim stops being true.

The transactional producer

🧒 Simple

A producer can group several sends into a transaction: either all of them become visible, or none of them do.

And it can include its own offset commits in that same transaction.

🎓 Professional

Set transactional.id, call initTransactions() once, then wrap work in beginTransaction() … commitTransaction() / abortTransaction(). Records from an aborted transaction are physically written to the log but marked aborted, so they are never delivered to a read-committed consumer.

The transactional.id is not decoration: it lets the broker fence a previous instance with the same id, so a zombie producer that was presumed dead cannot commit after a replacement has taken over. That fencing is what makes the guarantee hold across restarts, and it is a good detail to know.

Consume–transform–produce

🧒 Simple

The pattern this exists for: read from one topic, transform, write to another, and record how far you have read — all as one atomic step.

🎓 Professional

sendOffsetsToTransaction() puts the consumer’s offset commit into the producer’s transaction, so the output records and the position are committed together. Either the downstream records exist and the offset has moved, or neither happened.

Consumers must set isolation.level=read_committed to benefit; the default, read_uncommitted, delivers aborted records like any other. That is a common and embarrassing configuration miss: the producer side is set up carefully and the consumer never opts in, so the whole arrangement quietly does nothing.

⚠ Trap

Read-committed consumers cannot read past an open transaction in that partition — they must wait for it to resolve to preserve ordering. So a long-running or hung transaction stalls consumers on that partition, and transaction.timeout.ms is what bounds the damage. Keep transactions short, for the same reason as database transactions (Java chapter 25).

What it does not cover

🧒 Simple

The guarantee lives inside Kafka. As soon as your consumer writes to a database or calls another service, it stops applying.

🎓 Professional

Kafka transactions are atomic across Kafka partitions and the offset topic — nothing else. There is no distributed transaction spanning Kafka and Oracle, and there will not be one. If your handler writes to a database, the atomic unit you actually need is the database’s own transaction plus deduplication (chapter 16), or the outbox pattern (chapter 39).

They also cost: extra coordination round trips, a transaction marker per partition, and higher end-to-end latency because consumers wait for commits. Kafka Streams enables EOS with one setting (processing.guarantee=exactly_once_v2) and is where transactions are most often genuinely worth it, because the whole topology is Kafka-to-Kafka by construction.

Golden rules
  • A transaction spans Kafka writes and the offset commit, atomically.
  • transactional.id fences zombies. That is what makes it survive restarts.
  • Consumers must set read_committed, or the whole thing does nothing.
  • An open transaction stalls read-committed consumers on that partition.
  • The boundary is Kafka. A database write is outside it, always.
  • Streams with exactly_once_v2 is where this pays off most.

“Kafka supporta l’exactly-once. Quindi il problema dei duplicati è risolto?”

The question is a trap and the answer is the boundary: “Solo dentro Kafka. La transazione rende atomici i record prodotti e il commit degli offset, quindi il pattern consume-transform-produce è davvero exactly-once — a patto che i consumer a valle siano configurati read_committed, altrimenti leggono anche i record abortiti e non serve a niente. Ma appena il mio consumer scrive su Oracle o chiama un servizio esterno, quella garanzia non copre più nulla: non esiste una transazione distribuita tra Kafka e il database. Lì torno all’idempotenza, o al pattern outbox.”
