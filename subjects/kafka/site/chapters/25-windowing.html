Windowing, time and out-of-order events

Every real event stream is out of order. Systems that ignore that produce numbers which are quietly wrong — which is worse than producing none, because nobody knows to check.

From the advert

Not in any advert. And unavoidable the moment you aggregate: “orders per hour” requires deciding whose hour, and what to do about the record that arrives late.

Event time versus processing time

🧒 Simple

Two clocks. Event time is when the thing actually happened. Processing time is when your code got round to it.

A phone that was offline for an hour sends events with an old event time and a new processing time. Which one you count by changes the answer.

🎓 Professional

Streams uses event time by default, taken from the record timestamp — which is either set by the producer (CreateTime, the default) or by the broker on append (LogAppendTime, a per-topic setting). A custom TimestampExtractor lets you take it from a field in the payload, which is usually the most honest source.

Event time is almost always what the business means: “sales in September” means sales that happened in September, not sales your consumer processed in September. It also makes reprocessing deterministic — replay a topic and you get the same windows, which you emphatically do not with processing time. That determinism is the strongest argument for it and worth saying in an interview.

The window types

🧒 Simple

A window is a bucket of time you aggregate over: fixed hours, overlapping hours, or “until this user goes quiet for ten minutes”.

🎓 Professional

Tumbling: fixed, non-overlapping — every record in exactly one window. Hourly counts. Hopping: fixed size, advancing by a smaller step, so windows overlap and a record lands in several — “the last hour, updated every minute”. Sliding: windows defined by record proximity, for pairwise comparisons. Session: dynamic, closed by a gap of inactivity — the natural fit for user activity, since it defines the window from behaviour rather than by the clock.

Hopping windows multiply your output: an hour-long window advancing every minute means each record contributes to sixty windows, and both state size and downstream volume grow accordingly. That is worth checking before choosing one.

Late data and grace

🧒 Simple

When the window’s time has passed, records for it may still arrive. You decide how long to keep accepting them — that period is the grace.

After grace expires, late records are dropped.

🎓 Professional

ofSizeAndGrace(Duration.ofHours(1), Duration.ofMinutes(10)) keeps the window open for ten minutes past its end. Within grace, a late record updates the aggregate and emits a corrected result; after it, the record is discarded and the window’s state can be cleaned up.

So grace is a direct trade between completeness and latency — and, since every open window occupies state store memory (chapter 26), between completeness and memory. Long grace means later, more accurate results and a larger footprint. Emit a metric counting dropped-too-late records: if it is not zero, your grace is smaller than your real-world lateness, and your numbers are wrong in a way nothing else will reveal.

⚠ Trap

Streams advances its notion of time from the records it receives, not from the wall clock. So a partition that stops receiving data never advances, and its windows never close — results simply stop being emitted for it while everything looks healthy. On a low-traffic or unevenly-keyed topic this looks exactly like a bug in your aggregation, and it is not.

Golden rules
  • Event time is when it happened; processing time is when you saw it.
  • Event time makes reprocessing deterministic. That is the strongest argument for it.
  • Tumbling, hopping, sliding, session. Session windows come from behaviour, not the clock.
  • Hopping windows multiply output and state. Check before choosing one.
  • Grace trades completeness against latency and memory.
  • Count dropped-late records. Non-zero means your numbers are wrong.
  • Stream time advances from records, not the clock. A quiet partition never closes its windows.

“Come gestisce gli eventi che arrivano in ritardo?”

“Prima di tutto aggrego per event time, non per processing time: ‘vendite di settembre’ significa vendite avvenute a settembre, e in più mi rende il riprocessamento deterministico. Poi imposto una grace period sulla finestra: entro quella, un record in ritardo aggiorna l’aggregato ed emette un risultato corretto; dopo, viene scartato. È uno scambio esplicito tra completezza, latenza e memoria, perché ogni finestra aperta occupa spazio nello state store. E soprattutto metto una metrica sui record scartati perché troppo tardi: se non è zero, i miei numeri sono sbagliati e non me ne accorgerei in nessun altro modo.”
