The interview

The system-design round, RAL and contracts, and the questions you must ask them. The technical preparation is the other forty-five chapters; this one is the conversation they sit inside.

From the advert

“the whole advert”. Everything in this course arrives here.

The system-design round

🧒 Simple

You will be asked to design something with events on a whiteboard. They are watching how you approach it, not whether you produce the one right answer.

🎓 Professional

Follow a visible method. Ask about scale and consistency first — how many events per second, how stale can this reading be, does order matter per entity or globally — because those answers determine the design and asking shows you know that. Then commit to a shape, name the trade-off you are accepting, and say how you would know it was broken.

The moves that land, all from earlier chapters: choosing the key and saying what it makes ordered (02, 07); stating that delivery is at-least-once so consumers are idempotent (15, 16); raising the dual-write problem before they do (39); and answering “how would you know it was broken?” with consumer lag (14). Those four cover most of what a design round is actually assessing.

⚠ Trap

Do not reach for Kafka for everything. An interviewer describing a request–response integration is often checking whether you will propose a broker for it. “That is synchronous with a fixed contract — I would use HTTP” is the strong answer, and chapter 38 gives you the reason.

What to do when you do not know

🧒 Simple

Say so, then say what you would do to find out. That is a better answer than a confident guess.

🎓 Professional

“I have not used that — from what I understand it does X; is that how you use it?” keeps the conversation going and costs you nothing. A confident wrong answer costs a great deal, because it makes everything else you said suspect.

This matters more in this subject than in most, because the surface is large and nobody knows all of it. An interviewer who runs Kafka in production knows how much there is; a candidate claiming complete knowledge is signalling inexperience, not expertise. Naming your own boundary — “I understand the model and have run it locally; I have not operated a cluster” (chapter 00) — is what makes your other claims credible.

RAL, contract, and your questions

🧒 Simple

Know your number before the call, as an annual gross range. And have questions ready — having none reads as not being interested.

🎓 Professional

Give a RAL range, gross and annual, and say it is negotiable on the whole package. Ask which CCNL and which level, indeterminato or apprendistato, and how many mensilità — the Esprimo posting in this course’s corpus quotes 35–38k under CCNL Metalmeccanico, which is a useful reference point for a mid-level Milano role.

Your questions should be ones you actually want answered, and the good ones reveal how the team works: “Are you on Confluent, MSK or self-managed?”, “Who owns topic design — each team, or a platform team?”, “Do you have a schema registry and enforced compatibility?”, “How does a change reach production?” The answers tell you more about the job than the advert did — and asking them demonstrates the knowledge better than answering would.

Golden rules
  • Ask about scale, staleness and ordering first. Those answers decide the design.
  • Name the key, the guarantee, and how you would detect failure.
  • Raise the dual-write problem before they do.
  • Do not propose Kafka for request–response. Knowing when not to is the answer.
  • “I do not know, but…” beats a confident guess. Always.
  • RAL as a gross annual range; ask about CCNL, level and mensilità.
  • Ask who owns topic design. The answer tells you what the job really is.

“Ha domande per noi?”

Never say no, and make the questions do double duty — they reveal what you know: “Sì, tre. Siete su Confluent, su MSK o gestite voi il cluster? Chi possiede il design dei topic — ogni team il suo, o c’è un team di piattaforma che fa da guardiano? E avete uno schema registry con compatibilità imposta nella pipeline, o gli schemi si registrano al volo?” Each of those is a question only someone who has thought about the operational side asks, and the answers tell you whether the job is building on a platform or building the platform.
