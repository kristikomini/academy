Consumer lag: measuring it and fixing it

The one metric that tells you whether a Kafka system is healthy. What it means, the four things that make it rise, and why alerting on it is more useful than alerting on anything else.

From the advert

“monitoring systems (e.g., Prometheus, Grafana)”, from the aggregated Kafka-developer requirement lines.

What lag is

🧒 Simple

Lag is the distance between the newest record in a partition and the position your consumer has committed. In other words: how far behind you are, in records.

Steady lag is fine. Growing lag means you are falling behind and will not catch up on your own.

🎓 Professional

lag = log end offset − committed offset, per partition. It is the single most informative Kafka metric because it is an end-to-end statement: it captures producer rate, consumer throughput and failures in one number, without needing to know which of them changed.

What matters is the derivative, not the value. Lag of fifty thousand that is flat means a constant offset in a fast stream, which may be perfectly healthy. Lag of five hundred that climbs steadily means you have already lost, and the time to act is now rather than when it reaches a threshold.

The four reasons it rises

🧒 Simple

Either more is arriving than before, or you have got slower, or one partition is doing all the work, or your consumers are not running.

🎓 Professional

Producer rate rose — a legitimate load change; scale consumers, up to the partition count. Processing got slower — usually a downstream dependency, so check the handler’s own latency rather than Kafka. Lag is on one partition only — a hot key (chapter 07); more consumers will not help. Consumers are rebalancing or crash-looping (chapter 13) — the group is not consuming at all, and lag rising smoothly across every partition is the signature.

Reading which shape it is takes seconds and tells you which of the four you have: lag on one partition versus all of them separates a keying problem from a capacity or availability problem immediately. That diagnostic is worth more in an interview than any tuning knowledge.

Measuring and alerting

🧒 Simple

Export lag per group and per partition, and alert when it grows steadily — not when it crosses a fixed number.

🎓 Professional

The client exposes records-lag-max per consumer; for a cluster-wide view, an exporter such as Kafka Lag Exporter or Burrow publishes lag per group and partition to Prometheus without needing every application to report it. Grafana then gives you the two panels that matter: lag over time per group, and lag per partition within a group.

Alert on sustained growth, and on time-based lag where you can — “this consumer is twenty minutes behind” is meaningful to everyone, while “lag is 40,000” means nothing without knowing the rate. This is the Kafka-specific instance of the general rule in Java chapter 35: alert on symptoms users would notice, not on resource numbers.

⚠ Trap

Lag is computed from the committed offset, so a consumer that has stopped committing — auto-commit disabled and a commit path that throws — shows rising lag while actually processing fine. The reverse is worse: a consumer that commits before processing shows healthy lag while silently dropping records. Lag measures commits, not work done, and that gap is exactly why chapter 12 insists on committing after success.

Golden rules
  • Lag = log end offset − committed offset, per partition.
  • The trend matters, not the value. Flat-and-large is fine; small-and-climbing is not.
  • One partition lagging is a hot key. All of them is capacity or a rebalance loop.
  • Alert on sustained growth and on time behind, not on a fixed record count.
  • Lag measures commits, not work. Commit-before-processing shows healthy lag while losing data.

“Come monitora un consumer Kafka?”

“La metrica principale è il lag per gruppo e per partizione: è end-to-end, perché mette insieme la velocità del producer e quella del consumer in un numero solo. Guardo la tendenza, non il valore assoluto: un lag alto ma piatto può essere sano, un lag piccolo che cresce in modo costante no. E guardo la distribuzione: se il lag è su una partizione sola è una chiave calda e aggiungere consumer non serve; se cresce su tutte è capacità oppure il gruppo sta ribilanciando in loop. Esporto con un lag exporter verso Prometheus e allarmo sulla crescita sostenuta, meglio ancora sul tempo di ritardo, che è comprensibile a tutti.”
