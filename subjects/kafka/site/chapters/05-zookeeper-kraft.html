ZooKeeper, KRaft and the migration

Why ZooKeeper was there, why it is gone, and what you will actually meet in an existing cluster — because a company that adopted Kafka in 2019 is probably still running one and planning the move.

From the advert

“Familiarity with Kafka tools (such as Kafka Streams, Connect, and Zookeeper)”. Note that this line is already dated, which is itself worth understanding before you repeat it in an interview.

What ZooKeeper was for

🧒 Simple

Kafka needed somewhere to keep facts that every broker must agree on: which brokers exist, which topics exist, who leads each partition. ZooKeeper was that place.

🎓 Professional

ZooKeeper is a separate distributed coordination service holding cluster metadata, broker registration, topic configuration and controller election. It was a reasonable choice in 2011 — consensus is hard, and reusing a proven implementation beat writing another one.

The costs accumulated: a second distributed system to deploy, secure, monitor and tune; a metadata scalability ceiling, because propagating changes for very large numbers of partitions became slow; and split-brain-shaped failure modes when Kafka and ZooKeeper disagreed about who was alive.

KRaft

🧒 Simple

Kafka now keeps that information itself, in its own replicated log, using the same kind of agreement protocol. One system instead of two.

🎓 Professional

KRaft (KIP-500) moves metadata into an internal Raft-replicated log managed by a quorum of controller nodes. Metadata becomes an event log like everything else in Kafka, brokers follow it, and failover and start-up get substantially faster because a new controller replays a log rather than loading state from an external store.

The timeline matters for interviews: KRaft was production-ready in 3.3, ZooKeeper was deprecated in 3.5, and Kafka 4.0 removed ZooKeeper support entirely. So “we are on ZooKeeper” now implies a version ceiling, which is exactly why the migration is a live project in a lot of companies — and a good thing to ask about.

⚠ Trap

Do not say ZooKeeper is what tracks consumer offsets. It did, in versions before 0.9; since then offsets live in the internal __consumer_offsets topic (chapter 12). Saying the old thing is a precise signal that your knowledge comes from an old blog post rather than from a cluster.

What this means for you

🧒 Simple

As an application developer, almost nothing changes: your producers and consumers do not talk to ZooKeeper either way.

🎓 Professional

Client-side, the migration is invisible — clients have connected to brokers rather than to ZooKeeper since 0.9. What changes is operations: fewer moving parts, faster recovery, and an in-place migration path (dual-write mode, then cut over) that is well documented and still a genuine project.

The useful interview move is to treat the advert’s mention of ZooKeeper as information about them: “are you still on ZooKeeper, or have you moved to KRaft?” tells you how current their platform is and shows you know the difference — without making a point of it.

Golden rules
  • ZooKeeper held cluster metadata and elected the controller. Never consumer offsets, since 0.9.
  • KRaft puts metadata in Kafka’s own Raft log. One system, faster failover.
  • Kafka 4.0 removed ZooKeeper. Still running it implies a version ceiling.
  • Clients never talked to ZooKeeper anyway. The migration is an operations project.
  • Offsets live in __consumer_offsets. Saying otherwise dates you.

“A cosa serve ZooKeeper in Kafka?”

Answer in the past tense — that alone shows you are current: “Serviva: teneva i metadati del cluster, la registrazione dei broker e l’elezione del controller. Non ha mai tenuto gli offset dei consumer, che dalla 0.9 stanno nel topic interno __consumer_offsets. Da KRaft i metadati sono in un log Raft interno a Kafka, quindi niente secondo sistema da gestire e failover più rapido — e dalla 4.0 ZooKeeper non è più supportato. Voi siete già migrati a KRaft o ancora su ZooKeeper?”
