Events, commands and the naming that decides your coupling

OrderPlaced or SendInvoice — why the tense of a topic name is an architectural decision, and how a badly named topic quietly rebuilds the coupling Kafka was introduced to remove.

From the advert

Not in any advert. A five-minute idea with a decade-long effect, and one of the cheapest things to get right at the start.

The difference

🧒 Simple

An event says something happened: OrderPlaced. A command tells somebody to do something: SendInvoice.

An event has one publisher and any number of interested parties. A command has an intended recipient — which means the sender knows who that is.

🎓 Professional

Events are facts in the past tense, immutable, and cannot be rejected — it already happened. Commands are requests in the imperative, addressed to someone, and can be refused. That difference in addressing is the entire architectural consequence: a publisher of events knows nothing about consumers, while a sender of commands necessarily knows its recipient.

Both are legitimate. A command over Kafka is a reasonable way to get durability and back-pressure for a work queue. What is not legitimate is confusing the two, because the naming then lies about the coupling.

Naming, which is the decision

🧒 Simple

Name a topic in the past tense and you are publishing facts. Name it as an instruction and you have made a phone call with extra steps.

🎓 Professional

A topic named invoice.send is a command channel: the publisher has decided what should happen and who should do it, so it is coupled to the invoicing service’s existence and behaviour. A topic named sales.order.placed is a fact: invoicing subscribes because it has decided that is when to invoice, and that decision lives in the invoicing service where it belongs.

Keep the tense honest — <domain>.<entity>.<past-tense-verb> — and the architecture follows the naming. The failure mode is subtle: a command-shaped topic works perfectly and simply does not deliver the decoupling anyone thinks it does, so the discovery comes two years later when the invoicing rule must change and it turns out to live in the ordering service.

⚠ Trap

The worst version is a “command” whose sender waits for a reply on another topic. That is a synchronous call implemented with two topics: you have the latency of messaging, the coupling of RPC, correlation ids to manage and no timeout semantics. If you need a request and a response, use HTTP — and say so, because knowing when not to use Kafka is part of the answer.

How much to put in an event

🧒 Simple

An event can carry just an id, or the whole state that changed. The first is small and makes consumers call you back; the second is bigger and makes them independent.

🎓 Professional

Notification (“order 42 changed, go and look”) is tiny and reintroduces a synchronous callback — and with it temporal coupling and a load spike on the publisher whenever it emits. Event-carried state transfer puts the relevant state in the event, so consumers need no callback at all, at the cost of a larger payload and possibly stale data.

The usual right answer is state transfer with the fields consumers actually need — because it preserves the decoupling that was the point — plus a version number so consumers can discard out-of-order arrivals (chapter 18). What to avoid is putting the entire aggregate in every event out of caution: that couples every consumer to your full internal model, which is the schema coupling from chapter 37 at maximum strength.

Golden rules
  • Events are facts in the past; commands are instructions with a recipient.
  • A command-shaped topic name means the publisher knows its consumer.
  • <domain>.<entity>.<past-tense>, and the architecture follows.
  • Request–reply over two topics is RPC with worse ergonomics. Use HTTP.
  • Event-carried state transfer over notification, or you rebuild the callback.
  • Do not publish the whole aggregate. That is maximum schema coupling.

“Come chiamerebbe i topic in un sistema a eventi?”

“Al passato, con il dominio davanti: sales.order.placed. Non è una convenzione estetica, decide l’accoppiamento. Se chiamo un topic invoice.send sto mandando un comando: chi pubblica ha già deciso cosa deve succedere e chi deve farlo, quindi conosce il suo destinatario ed è accoppiato. Se pubblico il fatto ‘ordine creato’, la decisione di fatturare sta nel servizio di fatturazione, dove deve stare, e aggiungere un altro consumatore non richiede di toccare nulla. La cosa che eviterei è il comando con risposta su un secondo topic: è una chiamata sincrona con il doppio della complessità — se serve richiesta e risposta, uso HTTP.”
