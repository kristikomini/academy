acks, retries and the idempotent producer

Where “at-least-once” stops being a phrase and becomes a configuration. Why a retry silently duplicated your record before 0.11, what the idempotent producer fixed, and why it became the default.

From the advert

Not in any advert. This is the producer half of chapter 15’s delivery semantics, and the half people skip.

acks, from the producer’s side

🧒 Simple

acks is how much confirmation you wait for before considering the record sent: none, the leader, or all the in-sync copies.

Less waiting is faster and loses more when something fails.

🎓 Professional

Chapter 04 covered this from the broker’s side; the producer’s side is what it costs. acks=0 does not even wait for a network round trip, so throughput is highest and a broker failure loses whatever was in flight silently. acks=1 waits for the leader’s local write. acks=all waits for the full ISR, adding one replication round trip of latency.

The latency cost of acks=all is smaller than people expect, because it overlaps with batching (chapter 08) — you are waiting once per batch, not once per record. For anything with business meaning, acks=all plus min.insync.replicas=2 is the default to argue for, and acks=0 should be reserved for data you are genuinely willing to lose.

How a retry created a duplicate

🧒 Simple

The producer sends a record. The broker writes it and the acknowledgement is lost on the way back. The producer, having heard nothing, sends it again — and now there are two.

🎓 Professional

Before Kafka 0.11 this was unavoidable: a retry after a lost acknowledgement produced a genuine duplicate in the log, so retries > 0 meant at-least-once at the producer as well as at the consumer.

Worse, retries could reorder. With max.in.flight.requests.per.connection > 1, batch 2 could succeed while batch 1 was being retried, so records arrived out of order within a partition — quietly breaking the one ordering guarantee Kafka offers. The old advice was to set max-in-flight to 1, at a large throughput cost.

⚠ Trap

This is the answer to “does Kafka guarantee ordering?” that separates people who have read about it from people who have configured it. Per-partition ordering holds provided the producer does not reorder its own retries. Idempotence is what makes that safe while still allowing several in-flight requests.

The idempotent producer

🧒 Simple

The producer now stamps each record with an id and a sequence number. The broker remembers the last sequence it saw and quietly discards a repeat.

So a retry no longer creates a duplicate.

🎓 Professional

enable.idempotence=true gives the producer a producerId and a per-partition monotonic sequence number. The broker deduplicates by that pair and rejects out-of-order sequences, so retries are safe and up to five in-flight requests can be reordered safely by the broker rather than accepted out of order.

It is the default since Kafka 3.0 and it sets acks=all, effectively infinite retries and max.in.flight <= 5 for you — which is why explicitly setting acks=1 alongside it is a configuration conflict rather than a tuning choice. Note the scope precisely: it deduplicates retries by one producer session for one partition. It does not deduplicate your application sending the same logical event twice, which is chapter 39’s problem.

Golden rules
  • acks=all + min.insync.replicas=2 for anything with business meaning.
  • The latency cost is per batch, not per record. Smaller than people assume.
  • A lost acknowledgement plus a retry used to mean a duplicate.
  • Retries could also reorder, breaking the only ordering guarantee there is.
  • enable.idempotence is the default since 3.0 and sets acks, retries and in-flight for you.
  • It deduplicates retries, not your application logic. Different problem, chapter 39.

“Kafka garantisce l’ordinamento?”

Say “yes, within a partition” and then add the condition almost nobody adds: “Sì, ma solo dentro una partizione, e a una condizione: che il producer non riordini i propri retry. Prima dell’idempotenza, con più richieste in volo, un batch poteva andare a buon fine mentre quello precedente veniva ritentato, e i record finivano nel log fuori ordine — l’unica garanzia che Kafka offre, rotta in silenzio. Con enable.idempotence, che dalla 3.0 è il default, il broker deduplica per producer id e sequenza e rifiuta le sequenze fuori ordine, quindi la garanzia regge anche con retry e più richieste in volo.”
