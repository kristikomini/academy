At-most-once, at-least-once, exactly-once

The highest-value twenty minutes in this course. Almost every candidate can recite the three names; very few can say which one their last system actually had, or why. This chapter is that — where each one comes from, what it costs, and which you are running by default.

From the advert

Not in any advert. And the question every Kafka interview eventually reaches, because the answer reveals whether you have designed a consumer or only written one.

Where the three come from

🧒 Simple

Processing a record and recording that you processed it are two separate steps. Whichever you do first decides what happens if you die in between.

Commit first: a crash means the record is never processed — at-most-once. Process first: a crash means it is processed again — at-least-once.

🎓 Professional

That is the whole thing, and it is worth saying in exactly those terms because it shows the semantics are a consequence of ordering two operations rather than a feature you switch on.

At-most-once: commit the offset, then process. Fast, no duplicates, and loses records on any failure. Legitimate for high-volume telemetry where a gap is harmless. At-least-once: process, then commit. No loss, duplicates on failure. This is the sane default and what nearly every real system runs. Exactly-once: the effect of processing appears once, regardless of retries — achieved either by Kafka transactions within Kafka (chapter 17) or, far more commonly, by making the processing idempotent (chapter 16).

⚠ Trap

If you have not thought about it, you are running at-least-once with auto-commit, which is the worst of both: the timer can commit before you finish, so you get duplicates and occasional silent loss depending on where the crash lands. That is the actual default state of most Kafka consumers in production.

What exactly-once really means

🧒 Simple

Nothing can guarantee a message is delivered over a network exactly once. What can be guaranteed is that its effect happens once, however many times it arrives.

🎓 Professional

The distinction is delivery versus effect, and it is the one that makes the phrase honest. The two-generals problem means exactly-once delivery is impossible; exactly-once processing is achievable by making duplicates harmless or by making the write and the offset commit atomic.

Kafka’s EOS covers the second within a well-defined boundary: consume–transform–produce entirely inside Kafka, where the output records and the offset commit land in one transaction. The moment your consumer writes to Oracle, sends an email or calls an HTTP API, that boundary is crossed and you are back to designing for idempotency. Saying that boundary out loud is the strongest version of this answer.

Choosing, per consumer

🧒 Simple

It is a decision per consumer, not per system. Charging a card and updating a dashboard do not need the same guarantee.

🎓 Professional

Ask what a duplicate would cost and what a loss would cost. A duplicated metrics point is noise; a duplicated payment is a refund and a phone call. A lost metrics point is invisible; a lost order is a lost customer.

In practice: at-least-once plus an idempotent handler for almost everything; at-most-once only where loss is genuinely cheaper than the machinery; Kafka transactions when the whole pipeline is Kafka-to-Kafka and correctness matters more than the throughput cost. Being able to place a specific consumer in that framework, with reasons, is what the question is testing.

Golden rules
  • The order of processing and committing decides the semantics. That is the whole mechanism.
  • At-least-once is the sane default — process, then commit.
  • Auto-commit gives you duplicates and occasional loss. The worst of both.
  • Exactly-once delivery is impossible; exactly-once effect is not.
  • Kafka’s EOS is Kafka-to-Kafka. Writing to a database crosses the boundary.
  • Choose per consumer, by what a duplicate and a loss each cost.

“Che semantica di consegna usa il vostro consumer?”

“At-least-once, ed è una scelta. La semantica dipende dall’ordine di due operazioni: se committo l’offset prima di elaborare ho at-most-once e perdo record su un crash; se elaboro e poi committo ho at-least-once e in caso di crash rielaboro. Preferisco la seconda e rendo l’handler idempotente, perché un duplicato gestito costa molto meno di un record perso. L’exactly-once di Kafka lo userei solo per un flusso interamente Kafka-to-Kafka: appena scrivo su un database esterno quella garanzia non copre più nulla e torno comunque a doverla progettare io.”
