The job posting, decoded

Five adverts that write “Apache Kafka” and stop. This chapter says what that word is standing in for, why there is no standalone Kafka job in this market, and how to use a course where most chapters answer a question the advert never thought to ask.

From the advert

“the whole advert”. Unusually for this platform, the adverts here are the weakest part of the source material — and saying so is the point of this chapter.

There is no Kafka job

Phase 1 of this build collected five postings. Every Kafka line in them came from a Java or backend advert, mostly Milano or remote, several written in English.

🧒 Simple

Nobody in this region hires a “Kafka developer”. They hire a Java backend developer and list Kafka among the things that developer must know.

So this course is not preparing you for a different job. It is preparing you for one line of the job you were already applying for — a line that carries far more weight in an interview than its length suggests.

🎓 Professional

The postings behind this manifest: a Milano backend role listing “Java 17 (Spring Boot), OpenShift/Kubernetes, Apache Kafka/RabbitMQ, MongoDB e Oracle DB”; Esprimo in Milano wanting “architetture applicative con competenze su REST, SQL e Apache Kafka” at 35–38k RAL under CCNL Metalmeccanico; Sinergidea advertising “Sviluppatore Java e Kafka”; a remote “Java Backend Developer, Microservices & Kafka”; and a composite of Italian listings naming Streams, Connect, ZooKeeper, Schema Registry, Prometheus/Grafana and the Confluent certification.

Notice what is absent. None of them mentions partitions, offsets, consumer groups, delivery semantics or replication — the things the work actually turns on. That gap is why 29 of the 46 chapters here carry an extra rather than a req, the highest ratio of any subject on this platform, and why the coverage table on the home page is split the way it is.

What they are actually asking for

🧒 Simple

When an advert says Kafka, the interviewer usually wants to know one thing: do you understand that it is a log rather than a queue, and can you reason about what follows from that?

🎓 Professional

Almost everyone who lists Kafka has used a client library: sent a message, annotated a listener, watched it arrive. That is not what separates candidates. What separates them is whether they can answer the follow-up questions — what happens when a consumer is slow, what guarantees you actually have, what happens if you deploy a second instance, how you would reprocess yesterday.

So the highest-value chapters here are not the API ones. They are 12 (offsets), 13 (rebalancing), 15 (delivery semantics), 16 (idempotent consumers) and 39 (the outbox pattern) — and none of them is named in any advert.

What you can honestly claim afterwards

🧒 Simple

Finishing this course means you understand the model and have run a cluster locally. It does not mean you have operated Kafka in production, and saying so is not a weakness.

🎓 Professional

Kafka is unusually easy to list on a CV and unusually hard to defend, because the interviewer is often the person who runs it. An inflated claim collapses at the second question and takes the rest of your credibility with it (chapter 44).

The claim this course supports is precise and genuinely strong for a mid-level role: “I understand the model — partitions, offsets, delivery semantics — I have built producers and consumers against a local cluster, and I know why my consumers have to be idempotent.” That is more convincing than a vague claim of production experience, and it is true.

Golden rules
  • There is no standalone Kafka job here. It is one line of a Java advert.
  • The adverts name the tool and not the model. That gap is what this course fills.
  • 29 of 46 chapters are beyond the advert. The coverage table says which.
  • The valuable chapters are offsets, rebalancing, delivery semantics, idempotency and the outbox.
  • Claim the model and a local cluster. Not production experience you do not have.

“Vedo Kafka sul suo CV. Cosa ci ha fatto?”

Be exact, and let the precision do the work: “Ho lavorato sul modello più che in produzione, e preferisco essere chiaro: ho costruito producer e consumer contro un cluster locale, con Schema Registry, e ho studiato a fondo partizioni, offset e semantiche di consegna. Non ho gestito un cluster in produzione né fatto tuning di un broker. Quello che so fare è progettare un consumer idempotente e spiegare perché serve, dato che la consegna è at-least-once.” Naming the boundary yourself is what makes the rest believable — and for a mid-level backend role, this is a perfectly strong answer.
