Agile, Scrum and the ceremonies

What a team expects of you in each meeting, and the two habits that matter more than the framework — asking the question in refinement, and flagging slippage early.

From the advert

“Full-time participation in projects working collaboratively with development teams in Agile Scrum methodology”.

The four meetings

🧒 Simple

Stand-up: what you did, what you will do, what is blocking you. Refinement: understand the upcoming work well enough to estimate it. Planning: commit to a sprint. Retrospective: improve how the team works.

🎓 Professional

The stand-up is a synchronisation between peers, not a status report to a manager, so lead with blockers rather than with activity. Refinement is where you have the most leverage: the question that prevents a week of wrong work gets asked there, before anyone estimates.

In event-driven work specifically, the refinement questions worth asking are the ones this course has been about — who else consumes this event? what happens if it arrives twice? does the order matter? Asking those in refinement rather than discovering them in production is most of what a good backend developer contributes to a planning meeting.

Estimates, and being wrong out loud

🧒 Simple

Estimates are for planning a sprint, not for measuring you. The useful skill is saying early when one turns out to be wrong.

🎓 Professional

Points are relative size — complexity and uncertainty together — because people compare better than they predict durations. Velocity plans a team; it does not measure a person, and a team whose velocity is compared to another team’s has an organisational problem rather than an estimation one.

Two things read as experience: “I need a spike before I can estimate this” instead of a guess, and flagging slippage on day two rather than day nine. Nobody minds a task taking longer; they mind finding out too late to react.

⚠ Trap

Do not present yourself as an Agile purist. Most Italian software houses run something between Scrum and fixed-date project work — sprints with a commessa deadline underneath — and a candidate who insists on doctrine reads as someone who will be difficult about reality. Describe how you worked, then ask how they work.

Being easy to work with

🧒 Simple

Small pull requests, commit messages that explain why, prompt reviews, and asking for help after half an hour rather than after a day.

🎓 Professional

Keep pull requests small enough to review properly; write commit messages that say why, since the diff already says what; review others’ code promptly, because a PR waiting two days blocks a person; and separate “this is wrong” from “I would have done it differently” when you comment.

And a specific one for this subject: when you introduce an event, write down its contract where other teams can find it — the schema, the key, the ordering guarantee. An event is a public API with more consumers than an HTTP endpoint and usually less documentation, and being the person who documents it is a disproportionately visible contribution.

Golden rules
  • Stand-up is synchronisation. Lead with blockers.
  • Refinement is the leverage point. Ask who else consumes it, and what a duplicate costs.
  • “I need a spike” beats a guess, and flag slippage early.
  • Describe how you worked, then ask how they work. Doctrine reads badly.
  • Small PRs; commit messages say why.
  • An event is a public API. Document its schema, key and ordering guarantee.

“Come lavorate nel suo team? Usate Scrum?”

“Sprint di due settimane, stand-up, refinement a metà sprint e retrospettiva. La parte che trovo più utile è il refinement, perché è lì che si scoprono i malintesi prima che qualcuno stimi — e su un sistema a eventi le domande giuste sono sempre le stesse: chi altro consuma questo evento, cosa succede se arriva due volte, conta l’ordine. Le risposte le scrivo insieme allo schema, perché un evento è a tutti gli effetti un’API pubblica con più consumatori di un endpoint HTTP e di solito meno documentazione. Voi come siete organizzati?”
