Priority inversion, and the rover that rebooted

The best-documented real-time bug in history, on Mars, in 1997, diagnosed remotely and fixed by uplink. It is also the fastest way to show an interviewer that you think about time rather than throughput — because the whole story is three tasks and one mutex.

From the advert

Not in this advert. No posting names priority inversion. It is nevertheless the canonical follow-up to any answer involving mutexes, and it is the reason chapter 40 insists that a mutex and a binary semaphore are not interchangeable.

Three tasks and one mutex

🧒 Simple

Three jobs, in order of importance: urgent, ordinary, and unimportant.

The unimportant one picks up a tool. The urgent one needs that same tool, so it has to wait. So far, so reasonable — it will only be a moment.

But now the ordinary job wakes up. It does not need the tool, so nothing stops it, and it is more important than the unimportant one — so it runs, and keeps running. The unimportant job never gets the chance to put the tool down. And so the urgent job waits behind a job that is less important than it is, for as long as the ordinary job feels like running.

🎓 Professional

Formally: H (high), M (medium), L (low). H and L share a resource guarded by a mutex; M shares nothing.

  1. L runs and takes the mutex.
  2. H becomes ready, pre-empts L, tries to take the mutex, and blocks. This much is bounded priority inversion, and it is normal and unavoidable — H waits for the length of L's critical section.
  3. M becomes ready. M has higher priority than L and is not waiting on anything, so the scheduler runs M.
  4. L is now starved, so it cannot release the mutex, so H stays blocked — not for the length of a critical section, but for as long as M runs.

That is unbounded priority inversion. The blocking time no longer depends on the critical section at all; it depends on unrelated medium-priority work, so no worst-case analysis of H is possible. The system's priority scheme has quietly inverted, which is where the name comes from.

Mars Pathfinder, July 1997

🧒 Simple

A spacecraft landed on Mars and worked. Then, every so often, it reset itself and lost the data it had been collecting. Engineers on Earth reproduced it on an identical machine, found the cause, and sent up a fix that flipped one setting.

🎓 Professional

The lander ran VxWorks. An information bus, protected by a mutex, was written by a high-priority bus management task and by a low-priority meteorological task. A medium-priority communications task shared nothing with either.

When the meteorological task held the mutex and the communications task ran long, the bus management task missed its deadline. A watchdog observed that the bus task had not completed in its window and did the correct, conservative thing: it reset the spacecraft. Intermittently, because it needed all three tasks to line up.

Two details are what make this story worth telling in an interview, rather than just the mechanism:

They had tracing. The team could enable trace/instrumentation on the flight software and on an identical unit on the ground, which is how they caught something that happened rarely and left no other evidence. A bug you cannot observe is a bug you cannot fix — chapter 61.

The fix was a flag. VxWorks' mutex already supported priority inheritance; it had simply not been enabled for that mutex. They uplinked a change that turned it on. The defect was a configuration decision, not an algorithm.

The two protocols that fix it

🧒 Simple

The fix is to make the unimportant job temporarily important while it is holding something an important job wants. Then nothing in between can push it aside, it finishes quickly, puts the tool down, and goes back to being unimportant.

🎓 Professional

Priority inheritance. When H blocks on a mutex held by L, the kernel raises L to H's priority for as long as it holds the mutex, then restores it. M can no longer pre-empt L, so L finishes its critical section and releases. H's blocking time is bounded by the critical section again. This is what FreeRTOS's xSemaphoreCreateMutex() gives you, and it is precisely what a binary semaphore does not — a semaphore has no owner, so there is nobody to promote.

Priority ceiling. Every resource is given a ceiling equal to the highest priority of any task that can take it, and a task is raised to the ceiling the moment it acquires. Stronger: it prevents deadlock as well as inversion, and bounds blocking to one critical section per task. It is also more intrusive, needing the whole resource/priority map up front, which is why it is found in safety-critical designs (chapter 65) more than in general firmware.

Neither is free: inheritance adds work to every contended take and release, and neither removes the underlying need to keep critical sections short. Both are the reason “use a mutex for mutual exclusion and a semaphore for signalling” is a rule and not a stylistic preference.

⚠ Trap

Priority inheritance does not fix deadlock, and it does not apply to an ISR.

If two tasks take two mutexes in opposite orders, inheritance changes nothing — both are still waiting for each other, now at elevated priority. The fix for that is a global lock ordering, or not holding two locks at once.

And an interrupt handler has no priority the scheduler can raise, because it is not a task. This is one of the concrete reasons an ISR must never take a mutex (chapter 42): there is nothing to promote, so the inversion is unbounded by construction. Signal a task instead, and let the task take the lock.

Try it. Reproduce it deliberately. On a board with FreeRTOS, or in the FreeRTOS POSIX simulator on a PC — the bug is a scheduling property, so it shows up in both.

Create three tasks on one core:

  L  (priority 1)  take mutex; busy-loop 50 ms; give mutex; delay 200 ms
  M  (priority 2)  delay 20 ms; busy-loop 300 ms          (takes NO mutex)
  H  (priority 3)  delay 10 ms; take mutex; toggle a pin; give mutex

Toggle a GPIO pin on entry to and exit from H's critical section, and
watch it on a scope or logic analyser.

  1. With a BINARY SEMAPHORE standing in for the mutex, H's wait stretches
     to roughly L's 50 ms PLUS M's 300 ms. That extra 300 ms is the
     unbounded inversion, and it has nothing to do with the resource.

  2. Swap to xSemaphoreCreateMutex() -- priority inheritance on -- and the
     wait collapses back to about 50 ms, the length of L's critical
     section, no matter what M does.

Nothing else in the program changes. That difference is the whole chapter,
and it is why the two primitives are not interchangeable.
Golden rules
  • Bounded inversion is normal: a high-priority task waits for the length of a critical section.
  • Unbounded inversion is the bug: an unrelated medium-priority task starves the lock holder, so the wait no longer depends on the critical section.
  • Priority inheritance promotes the holder to the waiter's priority until it releases.
  • A mutex has an owner; a semaphore does not. That is why only a mutex can inherit, and why they are not interchangeable.
  • An ISR cannot participate — it has no priority to raise, so it must never take a mutex.
  • Inheritance does not prevent deadlock. Lock ordering does.
  • Pathfinder was fixed by enabling a flag, and found only because tracing was available.

“Ha mai sentito parlare di priority inversion? Mi faccia un esempio.”

“Sì. Tre task: alto, medio, basso. Il basso prende un mutex; l’alto arriva, vuole lo stesso mutex e si blocca — fin qui è normale, aspetta la durata della sezione critica. Il problema nasce quando si sveglia il medio, che non c’entra niente con quel mutex: ha priorità maggiore del basso, quindi gira lui, il basso non riesce a finire e non rilascia, e l’alto resta bloccato per tutto il tempo del medio. A quel punto il tempo di attesa non dipende più dalla sezione critica, quindi il worst case non è più calcolabile. La soluzione è l’ereditarietà della priorità: mentre tiene il mutex, il basso viene alzato alla priorità di chi aspetta, così il medio non lo scavalca. È anche il motivo per cui un mutex e un semaforo binario non sono intercambiabili: il mutex ha un proprietario, il semaforo no, e senza proprietario non c’è nessuno da promuovere. Il caso famoso è Mars Pathfinder nel ’97: si resettava da solo per questo, e l’hanno risolto abilitando l’ereditarietà su quel mutex — l’hanno trovato solo perché avevano il tracing attivabile da remoto.”
