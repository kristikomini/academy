The physical layer, and why RS-485

Before any protocol there is a voltage on a wire in a factory, next to a motor drive, two hundred metres from where it started. Everything Part 7 does above this layer assumes this layer works — and when it does not, the symptom looks exactly like a software bug.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Conoscenza approfondita dei protocolli di comunicazione seriale e Ethernet”. Approfondita is the word doing the work: anyone can call HAL_UART_Transmit, and the depth being asked for starts here, one layer below it.

Single-ended, and why it stops working

🧒 Simple

The simple way to send a bit is one wire plus a shared ground: high voltage means one, low means zero. It works on a desk.

In a factory it stops working, for two reasons. Electrical noise from motors adds itself to your wire, and the “ground” at the far end of a long cable is not actually at the same voltage as the ground where you started.

The fix is to stop measuring against ground. Send the signal on two wires at once, one the mirror of the other, and have the receiver look only at the difference between them. Noise hits both wires equally, so it cancels out of the difference exactly.

🎓 Professional

TTL/CMOS — what the microcontroller pin actually produces: 0 V and 3.3 V (or 5 V), referenced to the board's ground. Good for a few tens of centimetres, on the board or to a nearby module. This is what you have between the MCU and an RS-485 transceiver chip, and it is the level a USB-serial adapter expects.

RS-232 — single-ended, but with large bipolar swings (nominally ±12 V, receivers switch around ±3 V) and inverted logic. The wide swing buys noise margin; the shared ground reference still limits it to roughly 15 m and one device per link. Legacy, still present on older instruments and service ports.

RS-422 — differential, one driver and up to ten receivers, full duplex on two pairs. Point-to-multipoint broadcast.

RS-485 — differential, multi-drop (up to 32 unit loads on a segment as standard, more with low-load transceivers), half duplex on a single twisted pair, up to about 1200 m at low baud rates. A receiver decides on the differential voltage between A and B, with a threshold of about ±200 mV. This is why it is the industrial default and why Modbus RTU lives on it.

The property that matters is common-mode rejection. Interference couples into both conductors of a twisted pair almost identically, so it appears as a common-mode offset and the receiver, which only sees A minus B, ignores it. Transceivers specify a common-mode range — typically −7 V to +12 V — and that range is what tolerates the ground potential difference between two ends of a long run. Exceed it and the link fails regardless of how clean your signal is.

There is also a rate-distance trade: the usual rule of thumb is that baud rate times cable length is roughly constant, so 1200 m is a low-baud proposition and 10 Mbit/s is a short one.

Termination, biasing and the shape of the cable

🧒 Simple

An electrical signal travelling down a long cable behaves like a wave. When it reaches the end, if there is nothing there to absorb it, it bounces back and interferes with what comes after.

So you put a resistor at each far end to absorb it — at the two ends of the run only, not at every device. And the cable should be one line with devices along it, not a star, because every branch is another end for the signal to bounce off.

🎓 Professional

Termination. A resistor matching the cable's characteristic impedance — 120 Ω for standard RS-485 twisted pair — across A and B at each of the two physical ends of the bus, and nowhere else. Terminating every node loads the driver until nobody can reach threshold; terminating neither produces reflections that corrupt the fastest edges first, so the link works at 9600 and fails at 115200.

Topology. Daisy-chain, in and out at each device. Stubs from the trunk to a device should be very short — centimetres, not metres. A star wired in a panel because it was convenient is the classic field fault, and it is invisible in the software.

Fail-safe biasing. When no driver is enabled the bus is idle and floating, and a floating differential input can flip randomly — which a UART sees as a start bit followed by noise (chapter 46). Bias resistors pull A and B apart far enough to hold the idle state, and many modern transceivers include internal fail-safe for this. The symptom of missing bias is a device that receives garbage frames when nothing is being transmitted at all.

The ground wire. Differential does not mean groundless. The common-mode range must be respected, so a third conductor (or a cable shield bonded at one end only, to avoid a ground loop) is required for anything but a short run. Shield bonded at both ends creates a circulating current, which is itself a noise source.

Isolation. On a machine with drives and contactors, an isolated transceiver (transformer or capacitively coupled) is worth the cost: it breaks the ground loop entirely and protects the MCU from a transient that would otherwise destroy it. Ask the hardware engineer whether the board is isolated — it is one of the more useful questions you can ask at a bench (chapter 63).

⚠ Trap

Half duplex means a driver-enable pin, and releasing it at the wrong moment is a firmware bug that presents as a hardware fault. This is the single most common RS-485 mistake and it is worth being able to describe precisely.

One pair, so only one device may drive at a time. Your firmware asserts DE before the first bit and releases it after the last bit has physically left the shift register — that is the transmission complete flag (TC), not the transmit-buffer-empty flag (TXE), which fires as soon as the byte moves from the data register into the shifter and still has a whole character time to go.

Release on TXE and you truncate the final character, every time, by a predictable amount. At the master this appears as an intermittent CRC error, which looks like noise, which sends everybody to check the cable. The other half of the same problem is releasing too late: hold DE beyond the stop bit and you collide with the slave's reply, which on a polled bus means a timeout.

Many STM32 USARTs implement the driver-enable in hardware (the DEM bit, with configurable assertion and de-assertion guard times). Use it where it exists — hardware cannot be late the way an interrupt can.

Try it. The first two need nothing but a USB-serial adapter; the third is the one that changes how you debug.

1. Cheapest possible RS-485 setup: two USB-RS485 adapters (a few euro
   each), a length of twisted pair, and 120 ohm across A and B at each
   end. Run a Modbus master on one and a slave on the other (chapter 50).
   You now have a two-node industrial bus on your desk.

2. Now break it on purpose, one variable at a time, and watch the
   failure mode each time:

     - remove ONE terminator     -- often still works; margin is gone
     - remove BOTH               -- fails at high baud, fine at 9600
     - terminate a THIRD node    -- driver overloaded, nobody decodes
     - add a 2 m stub off the trunk -- reflections, intermittent CRC
     - swap A and B at one node  -- silence, and no error anywhere

   That last one is worth doing once. Reversed polarity produces no
   diagnostic at all, and it is a real field fault.

3. With a scope (chapter 62), probe A and B separately, then use the
   maths channel to show A minus B. Seeing the noise on both traces
   vanish from the difference is the entire argument for differential
   signalling in one picture.

4. If you have a board with an RS-485 transceiver: toggle a spare GPIO
   when you assert and release DE, and put it on the analyser next to
   the data. Move the release from TC to TXE and watch the last
   character get cut in half.
Golden rules
  • Differential signalling rejects noise because interference hits both conductors equally and cancels in the difference.
  • RS-485 is differential, multi-drop, half duplex, to about 1200 m at low baud; RS-232 is single-ended and about 15 m.
  • Common-mode range, typically −7 V to +12 V, is what tolerates ground potential difference. Exceed it and no signal quality helps.
  • 120 Ω at the two physical ends of the bus only — not at every node, and not nowhere.
  • Daisy-chain with short stubs. A star wired in a panel is the classic field fault and is invisible in software.
  • An idle unbiased bus floats, and a UART reads floating noise as frames arriving when nobody is transmitting.
  • Differential still needs a ground reference, and a shield bonded at both ends is a ground loop.
  • Release the driver enable on transmission-complete, never on transmit-buffer-empty — TXE truncates the last character and presents as intermittent CRC errors.
  • Baud rate times distance is roughly constant.
  • Reversed A and B gives silence with no diagnostic.

“Perché in campo si usa RS-485 e non una seriale normale?”

“Per due motivi che sono lo stesso motivo. Una seriale single-ended misura la tensione rispetto a massa, e in uno stabilimento la massa a duecento metri di distanza non è la stessa massa da cui sono partito, oltre al fatto che gli inverter iniettano rumore sul cavo. RS-485 è differenziale: mando il segnale su due fili in opposizione e il ricevitore guarda solo la differenza, quindi il rumore, che si accoppia uguale su entrambi, si cancella. In più è multi-drop, quindi metto trentadue nodi sulla stessa coppia, che è esattamente quello che serve a un bus Modbus. Le cose che controllo sempre in campo sono tre: la terminazione da 120 ohm ai due capi fisici del bus e basta — non su ogni nodo e non da nessuna parte; la topologia a catena con derivazioni cortissime, perché una stella fatta in quadro dà riflessioni che sembrano un bug software; e il polarization fail-safe, senza il quale a bus fermo la linea fluttua e la UART legge frame che nessuno ha mandato. Poi c’è la parte che è colpa mia e non del cavo: essendo half duplex devo pilotare il driver enable, e va rilasciato sul flag di trasmissione completata, non su quello di buffer vuoto, altrimenti tronco l’ultimo carattere e al master sembra rumore.”
