The product behind this advert: liquid cooling

The company in the anchor advert makes liquid-cooling components and systems for electrical and electronic devices. This chapter works out what a controller for one of those actually has to do — and turns it into the questions that show an interviewer you read who they are.

From the advert

Not a technical requirement, and the cheapest differentiator in the whole process. Every candidate arrives having revised C. Almost none arrive having worked out what the company's product does and what its firmware must therefore handle. This chapter is an exercise in doing that for one real posting; the method transfers to any other.

What the product is, and what it has to do

🧒 Simple

Power electronics — the big converters and drives that run motors and machines — produce a lot of heat in a small space. Air is often not enough, so a liquid is pumped past the hot parts, carries the heat away, and gives it up somewhere else through a radiator.

The controller's job is to keep that circuit doing its work: run the pump fast enough, keep the temperature in range, and notice immediately if the flow stops or the liquid leaks — because the equipment being cooled is worth far more than the cooler.

🎓 Professional

A cooling unit is a closed loop: a pump, a heat exchanger or radiator with fans, a reservoir, and plumbing to a cold plate on the device being cooled. The firmware's inputs and outputs, which is the useful thing to be able to list:

  • Temperatures — inlet and outlet at minimum, plus ambient. The difference between inlet and outlet, with flow, is the heat actually being removed, which is a far better health indicator than either temperature alone.
  • Flow, usually a pulse-output turbine or paddle sensor, so measurement is input capture and a frequency, and the absence of pulses is a stopped flow.
  • Pressure, often 4–20 mA, and a level switch in the reservoir.
  • Leak detection, typically a conductive strip or a float in a drip tray.
  • The pump — PWM-controlled, with a tachometer back (chapter 32) — and the fans, usually the same.
  • An interface to the host machine: dry-contact ready and alarm outputs at minimum, and increasingly Modbus RTU or Modbus TCP (chapter 58 is the design conversation).

And the control problem, which is more interesting than it first looks. The obvious approach — run the pump flat out — wastes energy, makes noise and wears the pump. The usual approach is to modulate to hold a target temperature, and the trap is that the process has large thermal inertia and transport delay: the water you heat at the cold plate arrives at the outlet sensor seconds later. A controller tuned without respecting that delay oscillates. This is the practical face of timing in a physical system, and “dead time” is the word to use for it.

There is also a constraint that surprises people and is worth mentioning in an interview: condensation. Cooling a surface below the dew point of the surrounding air puts water on electronics. A controller that simply minimises temperature can destroy the thing it is protecting, so the setpoint is bounded from below by ambient and humidity, not just by efficiency.

Designing for the failure, not the function

🧒 Simple

Keeping something cool when everything works is the easy half. The controller exists mostly for the other half: the pump seizes, a hose kinks, the liquid leaks, a sensor dies. What it does in those seconds decides whether an expensive machine survives.

🎓 Professional

The failure list, and for each one the decision that has to be made and written down:

  • No flow with the pump commanded on. Detected in a second or two by the absence of pulses. Must raise an alarm to the host immediately, because the host has to reduce its own load — the cooler cannot fix the problem, it can only report it in time. That is the key architectural insight of the whole product: the fastest, most valuable thing the controller does is tell the machine to back off.
  • Over-temperature, with two thresholds: a warning that lets the host throttle, and a critical level that demands shutdown. Hysteresis on both, so an alarm cannot chatter.
  • Leak or low level. Usually immediate shutdown of the pump, because pumping a leak empties the circuit and running a pump dry destroys it.
  • Sensor faults, from chapter 57: out of range, stuck, implausible rate, disagreeing pair. A controller that loses its outlet sensor should fall back to a defined safe behaviour — typically full pump, alarm raised — rather than to whatever the last number happened to be.
  • Loss of communication with the host, which must not stop the cooling. The machine can survive the controller being unable to talk; it cannot survive the controller stopping the pump because a cable came loose. The safe state for a cooler is almost always keep cooling, which is the opposite of the intuition people bring from other systems.
  • Power loss and restart. On restart, does the unit resume automatically? For a cooler, almost certainly yes — but it must come up in a defined state, with the alarm history preserved (chapter 34), because a unit that restarts silently after a fault hides the fault.

Every line of that list is a requirement, and writing them the way chapter 64a describes — one obligation, a number with a unit and a tolerance, a defined failure behaviour — is precisely the exercise this chapter exists to prompt.

Turning it into interview questions

🧒 Simple

At the end of an interview you are asked whether you have questions. Most people ask about the team or the hours. Asking something specific about their product shows you prepared, and it changes the conversation from an examination into a discussion between engineers.

🎓 Professional

Questions that are genuinely useful to you and that demonstrate preparation without pretending to experience you do not have:

  • “Does the cooling controller decide its own setpoint, or does the host machine command it? Where does that boundary sit?” — the who-is-master question, and it is a real architectural one.
  • “How fast does a flow fault have to reach the host, and how is that communicated — a dry contact, or over the bus?” — shows you understand the product's actual purpose is protection.
  • “How do you handle transport delay in the temperature loop? Is it a PID or something simpler?”
  • “Is the same firmware used across the range with configuration, or one build per model?” — the variant question.
  • “Can units be updated in the field, and over which interface?”
  • “Do you use Modbus with your customers, or their own protocol? Do you ever have to speak PROFINET?” (chapter 55)
  • “Is there a HIL bench, or is testing done on real units?” (chapter 64)

And the honest framing to use with all of them, which chapter 70 makes the general case for: you are not claiming to know their product, you are showing that you read their site, worked out what the firmware must handle, and arrived with the right questions. That is a completely different impression from a candidate who has revised pointers and nothing else, and it costs one evening.

⚠ Trap

Researching the company becomes pretending to know their product, and the interview turns into an examination you lose.

The failure looks like this: you read a product page, you say “so you use a PID on the outlet temperature with feed-forward from the load”, and the engineer — who has spent four years on exactly that loop — asks how you would handle the transport delay at low flow. Two questions later it is clear you were reciting, and everything else you said is now suspect.

The version that works uses the same preparation and the opposite grammar: state what you worked out, mark it as a guess, and ask. “From your site I gather the unit has to detect loss of flow quickly and tell the host to reduce load, because the cooler cannot fix the problem itself — is that the main safety function, or is over-temperature the one that matters more in practice?” That sentence proves you understood the product, invites them to correct you, and cannot collapse, because you claimed a deduction rather than knowledge.

It is the same discipline as the whole course: claim what you can defend, and be specific about the boundary. A junior who says “I have not worked on a cooling system, but here is what I think its firmware has to worry about, and here is what I would want to know” is doing the job of the interview correctly — and it is much rarer than technical knowledge.

Try it. This exercise is an evening of work and it is worth more per hour than any other in this course.

5. Write the five questions you would ask at the end of the
   interview. Not from the list above -- your own, from your page in

Golden rules
  • Read what the company makes, not just the advert. It is the cheapest differentiator available.
  • A cooling controller's main job is protection: telling the host to back off fast enough matters more than holding a temperature precisely.
  • Inlet minus outlet, with flow, is the real health indicator, not either temperature alone.
  • Absence of flow pulses is the fault signal, so it needs a timeout, not an infinite wait.
  • The process has thermal inertia and transport delay. A loop tuned without respecting dead time oscillates.
  • Do not cool below the dew point. Condensation destroys the equipment you are protecting.
  • Two thresholds with hysteresis: warn so the host can throttle, then shut down critically.
  • Leak or low level stops the pump; running a pump dry destroys it.
  • Loss of communication must not stop cooling. For a cooler the safe state is keep cooling.
  • Restart into a defined state and preserve the alarm history, so a fault cannot hide behind a silent reboot.
  • Every failure needs a decided, written behaviour — that list is the requirements document.
  • State your deductions as deductions and ask. Claiming their product collapses; reasoning about it does not.
