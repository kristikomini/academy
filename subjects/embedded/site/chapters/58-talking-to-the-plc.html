Talking to the PLC: who is master, and what happens when the link drops

The integration questions that decide the design, asked in the order that stops you rewriting it: who polls whom, what the register map says, which byte order, and — the one everybody defers and nobody should — what the machine does when your board stops answering.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Configurare l’interfaccia e la comunicazione tra schede elettroniche proprietarie e l’ecosistema dell’automazione industriale”. The verb is configurare, which sounds like a settings screen and is in fact the design conversation this chapter is about.

Who is master

🧒 Simple

Somebody has to ask the questions. Usually it is the PLC, because the PLC is in charge of the machine and wants the answer at a moment of its choosing.

That makes your board the one that answers. Which sounds easier, and mostly is — except that you can never volunteer information. If something urgent happens, you have to leave it somewhere and wait to be asked.

🎓 Professional

In practice the PLC is the Modbus master and your board is a slave, for three good reasons: the PLC owns the machine's timing, its scan cycle gives it a natural polling rhythm, and the automation engineer configuring it would rather add one more slave than be handed unsolicited traffic.

The design consequences of being the slave are the whole of the job:

You cannot push an event. An alarm has to sit in a register until the master polls it. So the polling interval is an upper bound on alarm latency, and it has to be negotiated rather than assumed. If 500 ms is too slow for a leak detection, that is a design input — either the poll rate goes up, or the alarm gets a dedicated hard-wired digital output, which is often the right answer for anything genuinely safety-related.

Latched status is usually correct. If a fault appears and clears between two polls, the master never sees it. A status bit that latches until explicitly acknowledged (the master writes a clear) is the standard pattern, and it is the same latch as the seal-in rung in the PLC's own world.

Commands need a handshake, not a level. The reliable shape is: master writes a command word plus an incrementing sequence number; the board executes and echoes that sequence number in a status register. The master knows the command landed exactly once even if the reply was lost and the request was retried (chapter 47). A bare command bit gets executed twice on a retry, or zero times if you clear it yourself and the master never saw it happen.

The register map is the contract

🧒 Simple

Both sides have to agree on what is stored where: register 12 is the water temperature, in tenths of a degree. If one side thinks it is whole degrees, the machine runs ten times too cold and nobody gets an error message.

So write it down, on paper, before either side writes code.

🎓 Professional

The register map document is the interface specification, and producing a good one is a disproportionate amount of the value you add. Every row needs: address, the convention that address is written in, name, type, unit, scaling, valid range, access, and the value on startup or fault. 

Addr  (40001-style)  Name              Type   Unit     Scale  Acc  On fault
----  -------------  ----------------  -----  -------  -----  ---  --------
  0        40001     status_word       u16    bitfld     1    R    0x0001 (fault)
  1        40002     fw_version        u16    BCD        1    R    unchanged
  2        40003     inlet_temp        i16    degC      0.1   R    0x8000 (invalid)
  3        40004     flow_rate         u16    l/min     0.1   R    0
  4-5      40005/6   pump_hours        u32    h          1    R    LOW WORD FIRST
 10        40011     setpoint_temp     i16    degC      0.1   R/W  retains last
 11        40012     cmd_word          u16    bitfld     1    R/W  cleared
 12        40013     cmd_seq           u16    counter    1    R/W  echoed in 40014

Four things in that table are the ones that actually go wrong:

The address convention. State it, and give a worked example. 40001 is address 0 (chapter 50), and half the integration bugs in the world live in this one line.

Word order for anything wider than 16 bits. Modbus registers are big-endian, but nothing in the specification says which of two registers holds the high half of a 32-bit value. Both orders ship, both are called Modbus, and Siemens and Schneider disagree by default. Write it in capitals as above, and test it with a value whose halves differ — 0x12345678, never 0x00000001.

Scaling instead of floats. Send tenths of a degree as an integer rather than an IEEE-754 float split across two registers. It avoids the word-order problem, it avoids the PLC side's float handling entirely, and it is what the automation engineer expects.

An explicit invalid value. A sensor that is disconnected must not read 0 °C, because 0 is a plausible temperature. Reserve a value (0x8000 for a signed 16-bit) or, better, carry a validity bit in the status word.

⚠ Trap

“What happens when the link drops?” is the question that separates a working prototype from a product, and it has to be answered on both sides.

Your board's side. If the master stops polling, that means something. After a configurable timeout — typically three missed poll intervals — your board must decide: keep the last commanded setpoint, or revert to a safe default? For a pump in a cooling loop, keeping it running is usually safer than stopping it. For a heater, the opposite. This is a product decision and it belongs in the register map document, not in somebody's head.

The PLC's side. If your board stops answering, the PLC must not carry on using the last value it read as though it were current. A temperature of 45 °C that is four minutes old looks exactly like a temperature of 45 °C. The standard fix is a heartbeat: a counter your board increments on every internal cycle, which the PLC reads and checks is changing. A frozen counter is detectable; a frozen value is not.

Ask, in the first design meeting: “cosa fa la macchina se la mia scheda smette di rispondere?” If the answer is a pause, you have just earned your place in the conversation, because you asked the question the commissioning engineer would otherwise have discovered on site.

Try it. No PLC needed. You can build and test the entire contract on a PC, which is also how you should test it before it ever reaches a machine.

1. Write the register map table FIRST, as a markdown file in the repo,
   before any code on either side. It is the artefact the other engineer
   reviews; the code is downstream of it.

2. Stand up a Modbus slave on your PC that implements it (pymodbus, as in
   chapter 50), and a master script that exercises it. Now test the things
   that only break in the field:

     - write a u32 whose halves differ (0x12345678) and read it back.
       If you get 0x56781234, your word order is the other one.
     - stop the master. Does the slave revert or hold? Is that what the
       document says?
     - stop the SLAVE. Does the master notice, or does it keep using the
       last value? Watch the heartbeat counter stop.
     - send the same command twice with the same sequence number. It must
       execute once.
     - send a read for a register that does not exist. You must get
       exception 02, not silence.

3. Only then connect real hardware. Every one of those five tests is
   something that is hard to provoke on a machine and trivial to provoke
   on a laptop.
Golden rules
  • The PLC is normally the master, so your board can never push an event — the poll interval is your alarm latency.
  • Anything genuinely safety-related gets a hard-wired output, not a register.
  • Latch status bits until acknowledged, or a fault between two polls never happened.
  • Commands need a sequence number echoed back, so a retry executes exactly once.
  • The register map document is the contract. Address convention, units, scaling, access and fault value, per row.
  • State the word order of 32-bit values in capitals, and test with 0x12345678.
  • Send scaled integers, not floats.
  • Reserve an explicit invalid value. A disconnected sensor must not read a plausible number.
  • Decide the link-loss behaviour on both sides, and use a heartbeat counter — a frozen counter is detectable, a frozen value is not.

“Come imposterebbe la comunicazione fra una vostra scheda e il PLC del cliente?”

“Partirei da tre domande prima di scrivere una riga: chi fa il master — di solito il PLC, perché è lui che possiede i tempi della macchina — ogni quanto interroga, e cosa deve fare la macchina se la mia scheda smette di rispondere. Quella terza è quella che di solito si rimanda, e poi salta fuori in collaudo. Poi scriverei la mappa registri come documento, prima del codice: indirizzo, con la convenzione dichiarata perché 40001 è l’indirizzo 0, unità di misura, fattore di scala, accesso e valore in caso di guasto. I valori li mando come interi scalati, decimi di grado, non come float: evito il problema dell’ordine delle word e al PLC fa comodo. Per i comandi uso un numero di sequenza che rimando indietro nello stato, così se il master ritrasmette il comando viene eseguito una volta sola. E metto sempre un contatore di heartbeat che incremento io: se la mia scheda si pianta, un valore fermo è indistinguibile da un valore giusto, mentre un contatore fermo il PLC lo vede subito. Per le sicurezze vere non userei il bus: un’uscita digitale cablata.”
