MISRA C, static analysis and functional safety

A coding standard that forbids perfectly legal C, the tools that enforce it, and the standards underneath — IEC 61508, ISO 26262, and what a SIL or an ASIL actually obliges a team to do. Knowing what MISRA is for matters far more than memorising rules.

From the advert

Not in the anchor advert, and present across the regional safety-critical and machinery listings collected beside it, increasingly under the Machinery Regulation. The automotive posting in the corpus implies it: propulsion work is where the higher ASILs live, and chapter 64a is the process this chapter supplies the coding half of.

Why forbid legal C

🧒 Simple

C lets you write things whose meaning is not fully defined, or which mean different things on different compilers, or which are simply very easy to get wrong. The compiler accepts them without complaint.

MISRA is a list of “do not use this part of the language” rules, written by the motor industry and now used far beyond it. The point is not that those constructs never work. It is that when a mistake could hurt somebody, you give up some freedom in exchange for code whose behaviour can be argued about with confidence.

🎓 Professional

MISRA C:2012 (with amendments) is the current edition, and its rules come in three obligations — the same grammar as specification English: mandatory (no deviation permitted), required (deviation possible with a documented, approved rationale) and advisory (recommended). There are also directives, which are about process and cannot be checked purely by a tool.

The rules that actually change how you write, and which are worth knowing by their content rather than their number:

  • No dynamic memory after initialisation — which is already the house rule in chapter 11.
  • No recursion, because stack usage must be statically provable (chapter 12).
  • Every if and loop body in braces, and every switch ends with a default. Both exist because of specific, famous defects.
  • One return, no goto (advisory in 2012 and often deviated from, since early return can be clearer than deep nesting).
  • No implicit conversions between signedness or width, which is the family of rules people find most annoying and which catches the most real bugs — chapter 3 is the reason.
  • Fixed-width types (uint16_t, not int) everywhere the size matters.
  • No undefined or unspecified behaviour, including no relying on evaluation order (chapter 13).
  • Pointer arithmetic constrained, and pointer-to-integer conversions restricted — which is exactly where memory-mapped I/O needs a documented deviation, because a register access is a pointer-to-integer conversion by definition.

That last point generalises: a deviation is a normal part of using MISRA, not a failure. Register access, a vendor HAL, an RTOS, a compiler intrinsic — each needs a recorded deviation with a rationale and an approver. A project claiming zero deviations either has no hardware access or is not being honest, and saying that in an interview shows you have worked with the standard rather than read about it.

The tools, and what each kind can find

🧒 Simple

Nobody checks these rules by eye. Programs read your source and report violations — and the better ones also find real bugs the rules were written to prevent: a variable used before it is set, an array index that can go past the end, a value that can be divided by zero.

The cheapest of these tools is the compiler you already have, with its warnings turned all the way up.

🎓 Professional
  • The compiler. -Wall -Wextra, plus -Wconversion, -Wshadow and -Wpointer-arith, with -Werror in CI. This is free, it is already installed, and it catches a substantial fraction of what people buy tools for. A project that is not warning-clean cannot usefully add anything else, because the signal is already buried.
  • Free static analysers — cppcheck, clang-tidy and the Clang static analyser. They do real data-flow analysis and have partial MISRA add-ons. Worth adding to CI on any project.
  • Commercial tools — PC-lint Plus, Polyspace, Coverity, Helix QAC. Certified for use in safety processes, with full MISRA coverage and, in the case of abstract interpretation, the ability to prove the absence of certain run-time errors rather than merely look for them. That certification, and the report it produces, is much of what is being paid for.
  • Dynamic tools on the host build — AddressSanitizer, UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer, Valgrind — which find things no static tool can, and which run for free in the off-target tests of chapter 64. Static and dynamic analysis are complements, not alternatives, and being able to say why is a good interview answer.

The operational rule that decides whether any of this works: turn it on at the start, and keep the count at zero. A tool introduced to a mature codebase reports four thousand findings, everybody agrees to fix them gradually, and the number never falls — and a report nobody reads is worse than no report, because it looks like diligence. On an existing project the workable approach is to baseline the current findings, fail the build only on new ones, and burn the baseline down deliberately.

Functional safety, in enough detail to talk about it

🧒 Simple

If a failure of your software could injure somebody, the law and the customer stop caring about your opinion and start asking for evidence: what could go wrong, how likely is it, what have you done about it, and how do you know?

The standards are the agreed answer to those four questions. They do not make software safe; they make the argument about safety systematic and reviewable.

🎓 Professional
  • IEC 61508 — the general functional-safety standard, with SIL 1 to SIL 4. The parent of most of the others.
  • ISO 26262 — automotive, with ASIL A to D, derived from severity, exposure and controllability. Propulsion work commonly sits at ASIL B or above.
  • ISO 13849 / IEC 62061 — machinery, with performance levels PL a–e. This is the set a machine builder in this region meets most often.
  • IEC 60730 for appliances, IEC 62304 for medical devices, DO-178C for aviation.

What an integrity level actually obliges, stripped of ceremony: a hazard analysis that says what can go wrong and how bad it is; requirements traced to the architecture and to tests in both directions (chapter 64a); a qualified toolchain, because a compiler bug is a safety issue and the compiler version is part of the evidence; coding standards enforced, which is MISRA; structural coverage evidence, up to MC/DC at high levels; diagnostics in the product itself — RAM and flash self-tests, a watchdog, plausibility checks between redundant sensors, a defined safe state entered on fault; and configuration management with reviews, which is chapter 66 made mandatory.

Nothing on that list is exotic. Every item is something a well-run non-safety project does informally; the standard makes it explicit, traceable and auditable. That framing is the honest one to bring to an interview, especially as a junior who has not worked on a certified product: you are not claiming experience, you are showing you know what the work consists of.

⚠ Trap

Compliance becomes the goal, and the code gets less safe while the paperwork improves.

The mechanism is banal. A tool flags a construct; the quickest way to clear the finding is a cast that silences the check rather than the fix that addresses it. An implicit conversion warning is removed by casting to the expected type, which discards exactly the information the rule existed to preserve. A deviation is granted because the deadline is Friday, with a rationale of “this is fine”. Six months later the codebase reports zero violations and contains casts nobody can justify, and the report says everything is in order.

Two questions keep it honest. For every suppression: does this cast make the behaviour defined, or does it only make the tool quiet? They are completely different acts, and the difference is visible in one line of review. And for every deviation: would you write this rationale down and sign it? A deviation is a small engineering argument — what the rule protects, why that risk is absent here, what else covers it — and one that cannot be written in three sentences is usually a defect being licensed.

The larger version of the same trap is worth stating because it is the answer to “what do you think of MISRA?”: a standard cannot make a design safe. Perfectly MISRA-compliant code can miss a hazard entirely, and no rule about braces catches a sensor read that is never plausibility-checked or a safe state that was never defined. The rules reduce a class of language-level mistakes so that review and analysis can spend their attention on the design, which is where the real hazards live.

Try it. All of this runs on a PC, on code you already have.

1. Turn the warnings up on an existing project:

     -Wall -Wextra -Wconversion -Wshadow -Wpointer-arith

   Count the findings. Then fix ten of them and look at what they
   were: a good share will be genuine sign or width bugs of the
   chapter 3 family, not style.

2. Run cppcheck with its MISRA add-on over the same project:

     cppcheck --enable=all --addon=misra src/

   Read twenty findings and sort them into three piles: real bug,
   real rule violation that is fine here, and noise. That sorting IS
   the skill -- the tool cannot do it.

3. Write one deviation properly. Take your register access -- a
   pointer-to-integer conversion, which MISRA restricts -- and write
   three sentences: what the rule protects against, why that risk does
   not apply here, and what controls it instead. Keep it; that is the
   artefact a safety process wants.

4. Demonstrate the trap to yourself. Find a -Wconversion warning and
   "fix" it with a cast, then work out whether the cast changed the
   behaviour or only the message. On at least one of them it will have
   hidden a real truncation.

5. Add the sanitisers to the host tests from chapter 64:

     -fsanitize=address,undefined

   Then run the parser's property test again. Static analysis and
   sanitisers find different things, and seeing that once settles the
   "which tool do we need" argument.

6. Finally, write the hazard list for something you have built -- the
   fan controller, the Modbus node. Five lines: what can go wrong, how
   bad, what detects it, what the safe state is. Nobody expects a
   junior to have certified a product; being able to produce that
   table is what the question is really testing.
Golden rules
  • MISRA restricts legal C so behaviour can be argued about with confidence, not because the constructs never work.
  • Mandatory, required, advisory are three different obligations, and directives are process rather than code.
  • Deviations are normal. Register access needs one, and a project claiming zero is not being honest.
  • A deviation is an argument: what the rule protects, why the risk is absent here, what covers it instead — in three sentences, signed.
  • Start with the compiler. -Wall -Wextra -Wconversion with -Werror in CI is free and catches much of it.
  • Static and dynamic analysis are complements; sanitisers on the host build find what no static tool can.
  • Turn tools on at the start and keep the count at zero, or baseline and fail only on new findings.
  • A report nobody reads is worse than no report, because it looks like diligence.
  • Ask whether a cast makes the behaviour defined or only makes the tool quiet.
  • Integrity levels oblige hazard analysis, traceability, a qualified toolchain, coverage evidence, in-product diagnostics and a defined safe state.
  • A standard cannot make a design safe. It clears language-level noise so review can look at the hazards.

“Cosa ne pensa di MISRA? Ha lavorato con norme di sicurezza funzionale?”

“Su un prodotto certificato non ho lavorato, e lo dico subito; però so di cosa si tratta e come si lavora. MISRA per me non è una lista di divieti stilistici: è rinunciare a un pezzo di libertà del linguaggio in cambio di codice di cui si può argomentare il comportamento — niente allocazione dinamica dopo l’inizializzazione, niente ricorsione perché lo stack deve essere dimostrabile, tipi a larghezza fissa, niente conversioni implicite fra segni e larghezze, che è la famiglia di regole che dà più fastidio e che prende più bug veri. E le deviazioni sono normali: l’accesso ai registri è una conversione da puntatore a intero, quindi una deviazione documentata serve per forza — un progetto che dichiara zero deviazioni o non tocca hardware o non è sincero. La deviazione per me è un piccolo ragionamento scritto: da cosa protegge la regola, perché qui quel rischio non c’è, cosa lo copre al suo posto. Sui tool parto sempre dal compilatore, -Wall -Wextra -Wconversion con -Werror in CI, perché è gratis ed è già installato, e aggiungo cppcheck e i sanitizer sui test host, che trovano cose che l’analisi statica non vede. La trappola che ho visto descritta e che mi sembra reale è che la conformità diventi il fine: si mette un cast per far stare zitto il tool invece di correggere, e dopo sei mesi il report è verde e il codice è peggiore. La domanda che tengo è: questo cast rende il comportamento definito o fa solo tacere lo strumento?”
