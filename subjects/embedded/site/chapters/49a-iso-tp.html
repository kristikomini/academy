ISO-TP: more than eight bytes over CAN

A CAN frame carries eight bytes. A diagnostic answer is routinely two hundred. ISO 15765-2 is the transport layer that bridges the two — four frame types, one flow-control handshake, and a set of timing rules that are the reason a reflash works on the bench and stalls in the car.

It is here because of a second posting collected later: an Embedded Integration Engineer role in Automotive, whose requirement line reads “Esperienza nell’utilizzo di protocolli di comunicazione, quali CAN e UDS”. UDS does not sit on CAN directly; it sits on this. A candidate who says “CAN and UDS” without being able to name the layer between them has learned two acronyms rather than a stack, and that is exactly what the follow-up question is for.

Chapter 49 is the bus underneath this. Chapter 49b is what rides on top of it.

Four frame types, and why there are exactly four

🧒 Simple

Imagine you can only send postcards, and each postcard holds seven characters. To send a long message you need three things: a way to say “this whole message fits on one card”, a way to say “this is the first card of a long message, and here is how long the message is”, and a way to number the cards that follow so the reader notices if one goes missing.

ISO-TP is those three, plus a fourth: a card the reader sends back saying “go ahead — and slow down, one card every five milliseconds, I cannot read faster than that”. That fourth one is the whole reason the protocol works on a small device.

🎓 Professional

ISO 15765-2, universally called ISO-TP. It is a segmentation and reassembly layer over raw CAN, and it spends the first byte of a frame on a protocol control information byte, whose high nibble is the frame type:

  • 0 — Single Frame (SF). Low nibble is the length, 1 to 7. The whole message is in this one frame; there is no handshake at all. On CAN FD a length above 7 uses an escape: PCI byte 0x00 followed by a one-byte length.
  • 1 — First Frame (FF). Low nibble plus the next byte give a 12-bit total length, so up to 4095 bytes; six data bytes travel with it. Lengths beyond that use the escape form — FF_DL nibble and byte both zero, then a 32-bit length.
  • 2 — Consecutive Frame (CF). Low nibble is a 4-bit sequence number, seven data bytes. The first CF after a FF is 1, not 0, and it wraps 15 → 0 → 1. The receiver checks it, and a gap is an abort, not a retry — there is no retransmission in ISO-TP.
  • 3 — Flow Control (FC). Sent by the receiver, once, after the First Frame. Three fields: FS (0 = ContinueToSend, 1 = Wait, 2 = Overflow — abort, I have no buffer for a message that long), BS the block size, and STmin the minimum separation time.

BS is how many Consecutive Frames the sender may transmit before stopping for another Flow Control. BS = 0 means “send them all, never stop”, which is what a PC tool typically asks for and what a microcontroller with one 4 KB buffer typically should not.

STmin is the gap the sender must leave between Consecutive Frames: 0x00–0x7F is 0 to 127 milliseconds, and 0xF1–0xF9 is 100 to 900 microseconds. Two different units in one byte, with a reserved hole between them, is the kind of encoding that produces a thousand-fold error in one direction — and it is a favourite interview detail for exactly that reason.

Unused bytes are padded, conventionally with 0x00, 0xAA or 0xCC. Whether an ECU pads to a full 8 bytes, and whether it rejects a request that is not padded, is a per-project decision that the specification allows and does not settle.

Addressing: which identifier carries which conversation

🧒 Simple

CAN frames are not addressed to anybody — every node hears every frame and decides whether it cares. So a conversation between one tool and one control unit has to be made out of identifiers: one number that means “this is a question for you”, and another that means “this is my answer”.

There is also a special number that means “this is a question for everybody”. It is how a tool finds out which control units are in the car without being told first.

🎓 Professional

Three schemes exist, and a project picks one:

  • Normal addressing. One CAN identifier per direction, and the full eight bytes are available to the transport layer. The 11-bit convention from ISO 15765-4 is the one everybody recognises: 0x7DF is the functional request identifier, heard by every ECU; 0x7E0–0x7E7 are physical requests to one specific ECU; and its answer comes back on the request identifier plus 8, so 0x7E0 is answered on 0x7E8.
  • Extended addressing. The first data byte carries a target address, leaving seven bytes per frame for the transport layer and six for a Single Frame payload. Used when many logical nodes share one identifier.
  • Normal fixed / mixed addressing on 29-bit identifiers, where source and target are fields inside the identifier itself: 0x18DA then target then source for a physical request, 0x18DB33F1 for a functional one — F1 being the conventional address of the tester.

Two rules follow, and both bite. Functional addressing may only carry a Single Frame — a segmented conversation needs one partner, and “everybody” is not one partner. And a receiver runs one reassembly buffer per connection: a second First Frame arriving on the same connection while one is open does not queue, it replaces. Interleaving two segmented requests to the same ECU on the same identifier does not work, and the failure looks like data corruption rather than like a protocol violation.

The timers, which is what actually goes wrong

🧒 Simple

Because nothing is ever re-sent, both sides need to agree on how long to wait before deciding the other one has died. There are two waits that matter: the sender waiting for permission to continue, and the receiver waiting for the next numbered card. Both give up after about a second.

The trap is that a device can be fast enough on a quiet desk and too slow in a car, where the same bus is also carrying everything else. Nothing warns you. The transfer simply stops halfway.

🎓 Professional

The named timers, because the names appear in tool logs and in defect reports:

  • N_As / N_Ar — time to get a frame onto the wire, sender and receiver side. This expires when arbitration never lets you transmit: a busy bus, or a diagnostic identifier assigned a low priority.
  • N_Bs — the sender waiting for a Flow Control after its First Frame. Typically 1000 ms. Expiry means the ECU never answered, or answered on an identifier you are not listening to.
  • N_Cr — the receiver waiting for the next Consecutive Frame, also typically 1000 ms. Expiry aborts the reassembly and discards everything received so far.
  • N_Cs — the sender's own budget between Consecutive Frames, which must stay below the STmin the receiver asked for.

A Flow Control with FS = Wait restarts N_Bs, which is how an ECU busy erasing a flash sector buys time legitimately. There is a limit on how many Waits a sender will accept, and it is configurable — which means a bootloader that leans on it is depending on the tester's configuration, not on the standard.

Worth internalising: ISO-TP has no retransmission and no checksum of its own. It trusts CAN's CRC for integrity and its own sequence numbers for completeness, and when either fails it aborts upward rather than repairing. Every reliability argument about a diagnostic transfer ends at the CAN controller's error handling, which is chapter 49.

⚠ Trap

Your ECU asks for STmin = 0 because it was easy, and the reflash fails in the vehicle with no error anybody can localise.

On the bench there are two nodes and a tester that transmits perfectly. In the car the same diagnostic identifier competes with a few hundred cyclic messages, the ECU is also servicing its application, and the receive interrupt now lands behind other work. Frames are not lost by CAN — they are lost by your receive path, because the mailbox was overwritten before the task drained it. The transfer aborts on a sequence-number gap, and the log says “unexpected SN”, which describes the symptom perfectly and points nowhere.

Three habits. Set STmin from what your receive path can actually sustain, not from what makes the bench number look good — and measure that under load, not idle. Set BS to the number of frames your buffer holds, so flow control does the job it exists for instead of being a formality. And count aborts and expose the counter the way you expose the CAN error counters: a transport layer that fails silently upward is one where every defect report arrives as “the flash sometimes doesn't work”.

The mirror-image mistake is on the tester side: honouring STmin with a millisecond-resolution sleep, so a requested 1 ms becomes 2 ms, a 500-frame transfer takes twice as long as budgeted, and somebody concludes the ECU is slow.

Try it. This one needs no car and no ECU. Linux has ISO-TP in the kernel, and a socket that does segmentation for you is the fastest way to see the frames.

1. A virtual bus costs nothing:

     sudo modprobe vcan
     sudo ip link add dev vcan0 type vcan
     sudo ip link set up vcan0
     candump -tz vcan0 &

2. Send 3 bytes, then 30, with can-utils (isotpsend/isotprecv are in
   the same package as candump):

     echo "22 F1 90" | isotpsend -s 7E0 -d 7E8 vcan0

   Watch candump: ONE frame, PCI 03. Now make the payload 30 bytes and
   watch it become a First Frame, a Flow Control coming back, and four
   Consecutive Frames numbered 1,2,3,4. That single screenshot is the
   whole chapter.

3. Change the receiver's flow control and watch the sender obey:

     isotprecv -s 7E8 -d 7E0 -b 2 -m 20 vcan0

   Block size 2 and STmin 0x20 (32 ms). Now count the Flow Control
   frames -- one every two CFs -- and measure the gaps with candump's
   timestamps. You have just seen the receiver pace the sender.

4. Break it on purpose. Use `cansend` to inject a Consecutive Frame
   with the wrong sequence number in the middle of a transfer, and
   watch the receiver abort rather than ask for a repeat. Nothing in
   ISO-TP recovers; that is the design.

5. Then read one implementation end to end -- isotp-c is a few hundred
   lines. The state machine is small enough to hold in your head, and
   having read it once is the difference between reciting the frame
   types and being able to say where a transfer stalled.
Golden rules
  • UDS does not run on CAN. It runs on ISO-TP, which runs on CAN. Naming the middle layer is the difference between two acronyms and a stack.
  • Four frame types: Single, First, Consecutive, Flow Control — and the Flow Control is sent by the receiver.
  • The first Consecutive Frame is number 1, and the sequence number wraps through 15 to 0.
  • BS = 0 means “send everything without stopping”. Ask for what your buffer can hold, not for what is fastest.
  • STmin below 0x80 is milliseconds; 0xF1 to 0xF9 is 100 to 900 microseconds. Two units in one byte.
  • Functional addressing carries Single Frames only. A segmented conversation needs exactly one partner.
  • One reassembly buffer per connection. A second First Frame replaces the first; interleaving corrupts.
  • There is no retransmission and no checksum of its own. A gap aborts the transfer upward.
  • N_Bs and N_Cr are the two timeouts you will actually meet, both around a second, and a Flow Control with Wait restarts N_Bs.
  • Pace the sender from your measured worst case under bus load, not from the bench, and count aborts where somebody can see them.

“Ha detto CAN e UDS. Cosa c’è in mezzo?”

“C’è ISO-TP, la ISO 15765-2, e secondo me è la parte più interessante delle tre. CAN mi dà otto byte per frame, una risposta diagnostica ne può occupare duecento, e ISO-TP è il livello di trasporto che li segmenta e li riassembla. Ha quattro tipi di frame: Single Frame quando ci sta tutto, First Frame che dichiara la lunghezza totale su dodici bit, Consecutive Frame numerati — il primo è l’uno, non lo zero, e il contatore gira su quattro bit — e il Flow Control, che però lo manda chi riceve: dice ‘vai avanti’, quanti frame posso mandargli prima di fermarmi di nuovo, e lo STmin, cioè la distanza minima fra un frame e l’altro. Quello è il punto che sul banco non si vede: se configuro STmin a zero perché in laboratorio funziona, poi in vettura il bus è carico, la mia catena di ricezione non sta dietro, e il trasferimento si interrompe con un buco nella numerazione. ISO-TP non ritrasmette niente e non ha un CRC suo: se manca un frame aborta e basta. Quindi STmin e block size li dimensiono su quello che regge davvero la mia ricezione sotto carico, e tengo un contatore degli abort esposto, come faccio con i contatori di errore del CAN.”
