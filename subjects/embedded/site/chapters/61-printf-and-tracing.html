Printf, tracing and the cost of looking

Every instrument disturbs what it measures, and printf disturbs it more than anything else you will reach for. What it actually costs, why that cost hides exactly the bugs you are hunting, and the cheaper instruments — SWO, RTT, a ring buffer, and one spare pin.

From the advert

Not in any advert, and present in every project. It is the first instinct everybody brings from PC programming, and the one that hides real-time bugs most effectively — which makes it worth a chapter rather than a footnote. Chapter 43 is the measurement theory; this is the plumbing.

What a printf costs

🧒 Simple

On a PC, printing a line is free enough to ignore. On a microcontroller sending a 40-character line down a serial port at 115200 baud takes about three and a half milliseconds — and if the code waits for it, that is three and a half milliseconds of doing nothing else.

In a system where things are supposed to happen every millisecond, one debug line has changed the behaviour more than most bugs would. You are no longer measuring your program; you are measuring a different program.

🎓 Professional

The three costs, in order of how often they matter:

  • Time on the wire. Ten bits per character at 115200 baud is 87 µs per character; 40 characters is 3.5 ms. At 168 MHz that is over half a million instructions. A blocking printf inside a 1 ms control loop does not slow it down, it destroys it.
  • Formatting. printf parses a format string at run time, and the float-capable version pulls in conversion code that can cost 10–20 KB of flash (chapter 36). It also uses a surprising amount of stack, which on a small task is how you meet chapter 12.
  • Re-entrancy. The standard library's printf is generally not re-entrant. Called from both the main loop and an ISR, output interleaves into garbage at best; with a shared buffer, it corrupts. And printf in an ISR is doubly wrong, because it makes the interrupt long (chapter 26).

So the first fix is not to stop printing, it is to stop blocking: format into a ring buffer and let a DMA or an interrupt drain it to the UART (chapter 27). The producer costs microseconds instead of milliseconds, and the ordering is preserved. The second fix is to drop rather than block when the buffer is full, and to count the drops — a logger that stalls the system under load is a logger that changes the system's behaviour exactly when the log matters, whereas “47 messages lost” is honest and cheap.

Two further wins, both nearly free. Timestamp every line from a free-running counter, because a log without time cannot show you a timing bug. And log binary events rather than text where volume matters: a one-byte event id plus a timestamp is four bytes and a few cycles, against forty bytes and a formatting pass, and a five-line script on the PC side turns the ids back into words.

Cheaper instruments

🧒 Simple

There are ways to get information out of a running chip that cost almost nothing: a dedicated trace pin the debugger reads, a shared memory buffer the debugger picks up while the program keeps running, or simply toggling a spare pin and watching it with an instrument.

🎓 Professional
  • SWO / ITM. One extra pin, driven by the core's trace unit. Writing a byte to an ITM stimulus register is a single store when the buffer is free — a few cycles rather than milliseconds — and the debugger prints it. The catch is that it needs the trace clock configured to match the core clock, and that a wrong clock setting produces silence or garbage, which is why people give up on it in ten minutes.
  • RTT (SEGGER's, with J-Link). A ring buffer in RAM with a known signature; the probe finds it by scanning memory and reads it while the core runs, because the debug port is a bus master. Costs a memcpy on the target, needs no extra pin, and is the best general-purpose answer when you have a J-Link.
  • A GPIO pin and a logic analyser. Set on entry, clear on exit: two instructions, about 12 ns, and you get exact timing of an ISR or a task with essentially zero disturbance. For any question of the form “how long, how often, in what order”, this is the correct instrument, and it is the one experienced people reach for first.
  • An event ring in no-init RAM, dumped after a reset (chapter 60). The only instrument that works when the failure is a crash in the field.
  • CYCCNT from the DWT: a free-running cycle counter you read before and after. Not an output channel at all, but the right way to answer “how long does this take” to the cycle.

A comparison worth remembering as one line: printf costs milliseconds, RTT and ITM cost microseconds, a pin costs nanoseconds. Choose by how small the thing you are measuring is.

Logging that ships

🧒 Simple

Debug printing usually gets deleted before the product ships, which means the day a customer has a problem, the device says nothing at all. A better plan is a logging system quiet enough to leave switched on.

🎓 Professional

What “quiet enough to ship” means in practice:

  • Levels, filtered at compile time as well as at run time. A macro that compiles to nothing at the lowest level costs no flash and no cycles; a run-time level lets a technician raise verbosity on site without a new binary.
  • Per-module tags, so one subsystem can be made verbose without drowning in the rest.
  • A bounded output path that drops and counts rather than blocking, on a low-priority path, and never from an ISR — an ISR enqueues an event id, and the printing happens later at a level where blocking is survivable.
  • A dedicated diagnostic channel where one exists. In automotive that is UDS DIDs and DTCs rather than a serial console; in a plant it is a block of Modbus registers. A log nobody can retrieve without opening the cabinet is not a diagnostic.
  • Counters beside the log. Error counts, drop counts, resets, worst-case loop time — numbers survive better than prose, cost nothing to keep, and are what a technician reads first (the same argument as the CAN error counters in chapter 49).
⚠ Trap

The bug disappears when you add the printf, and comes back when you remove it — so the printf gets left in “because it works now”.

This is the classic heisenbug, and it is a diagnosis, not a nuisance. The printf changed the timing, and the fact that timing changes the outcome tells you the defect is one of a small family: a race between an ISR and the main context; a missing volatile, where the delay happens to give the hardware time to catch up; a buffer that only overruns when the consumer is fast; or a peripheral that needs a settling time the datasheet mentions and nobody read.

What to do instead of leaving it in: replace the printf with a delay of the same length. If the bug stays away, it is timing, and you now know roughly how much slack the system needed — which points at the real fix. If the bug comes back, the printf was doing something else, most likely serialising two code paths through a shared buffer, which means you have found a concurrency bug rather than a timing one.

Shipping the printf is a genuine hazard, not a style complaint: the timing that hides the bug is an accident, so it changes with a compiler version, an optimisation flag, a different baud rate or a busier bus — and the defect reappears in the field, in a build nobody associates with the change. The rule worth holding: a delay that fixes a bug is a bug you have not found yet.

Try it. A Nucleo, and for the pin exercise the logic analyser from chapter 29.

1. Measure the cost. Toggle a pin around a blocking printf of 40
   characters at 115200 baud and look at it on the analyser. You
   should see roughly 3.5 ms. Then do the same around an ITM or RTT
   write and compare -- microseconds.

2. Build the non-blocking logger: format into a ring buffer, drain it
   with UART DMA or a TX-empty interrupt, drop and COUNT when full.
   Then hammer it from a fast loop and confirm the system keeps its
   timing while the drop counter rises. That is the difference between
   a logger and a hazard.

3. Timestamp every line from a free-running timer (chapter 32,
   exercise 5) or from DWT CYCCNT, and print the delta. A log without
   time cannot show a timing bug, and you will feel the difference
   immediately.

4. Instrument an ISR with a pin: set on entry, clear on exit. Measure
   its duration and its period on the analyser. Then add a printf
   inside the same ISR and watch the period fall apart. Two minutes,
   and it is this whole chapter.

5. Get SWO working once, deliberately -- configure the trace clock to
   match the core clock, redirect putchar to ITM_SendChar, and read it
   in the IDE. It fails silently if the clock is wrong, which is why
   most people never use it; getting it right once makes it available
   forever.

6. Then make the heisenbug on purpose: a flag written by an ISR and
   read by the main loop, without volatile, at -O2. Watch a printf
   "fix" it. Replace the printf with an equal delay and watch it stay
   fixed. Then fix it properly with volatile and remove both.
Golden rules
  • A 40-character line at 115200 baud is about 3.5 ms, which is half a million instructions at 168 MHz.
  • Never block on output. Format into a ring buffer and drain it by DMA or interrupt.
  • Drop and count rather than stall. A logger that blocks under load changes the system exactly when the log matters.
  • Never printf from an ISR. Enqueue an event id and print it later.
  • printf is not re-entrant, and the float version can cost 20 KB of flash and a lot of stack.
  • Timestamp everything. A log without time cannot show a timing bug.
  • printf costs milliseconds, ITM and RTT cost microseconds, a pin costs nanoseconds. Pick by the size of what you are measuring.
  • For “how long, how often, in what order”, use a pin and a logic analyser.
  • A bug that a printf fixes is a timing or concurrency bug, and that is a diagnosis worth acting on.
  • A delay that fixes a bug is a bug you have not found yet. Never ship it.
  • Ship the logging, with levels, tags, drop counts and a way to read it over the device's normal interface.

“Come fa il debug di un problema di tempi, e perché non con le printf?”

“Perché la printf è lo strumento che disturba di più proprio quello che voglio misurare. Quaranta caratteri a 115200 sono circa tre millisecondi e mezzo bloccanti: dentro un loop di controllo da un millisecondo non lo rallento, lo distruggo — e quello che misuro non è più il mio programma. Quindi se devo loggare, formatto in un ring buffer e lo svuoto in DMA, e quando il buffer è pieno scarto e conto gli scarti invece di bloccare; e dentro un’ISR non stampo mai, al massimo accodo un id di evento. Ma per le domande di tempo — quanto dura, ogni quanto, in che ordine — lo strumento giusto è un pin: set all’ingresso dell’ISR, clear all’uscita, due istruzioni, una decina di nanosecondi, e guardo tutto sull’analizzatore logico. La regola che mi porto dietro è questa: printf costa millisecondi, ITM o RTT costano microsecondi, un pin costa nanosecondi. E se aggiungendo una printf il bug sparisce, per me non è una soluzione ma una diagnosi: vuol dire che è un problema di tempi o di concorrenza — spesso un volatile mancante. Allora provo a sostituire la printf con un ritardo uguale: se resta a posto so quanto slack serviva e dove guardare. Un ritardo che risolve un bug è un bug che non ho ancora trovato, e non lo spedisco.”
