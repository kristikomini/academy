UART

Start bit, data, parity, stop. The oldest interface on the board and still the first one you reach for when nothing else works — plus the three ways to receive, only one of which survives a fast link.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Implementare e testare protocolli di comunicazione seriale e di rete” and “Conoscenza approfondita dei protocolli di comunicazione seriale e Ethernet”. Everything in chapter 50 runs on top of this.

The character on the wire

🧒 Simple

Two wires, one each way, and no clock. The line sits high when idle. To send a byte, the sender pulls it low for one bit-time — that is the start bit, and it tells the receiver when to begin — then sends the eight data bits, then lets it go high again.

Because there is no shared clock, both ends must already agree on how long a bit lasts. That agreement is the baud rate, and if they disagree, nothing works.

🎓 Professional

Asynchronous, no clock line. A character is: one start bit (a falling edge, which is the synchronisation event), 5–9 data bits (almost always 8), an optional parity bit, and 1, 1.5 or 2 stop bits. So 8N1 — the usual setting — is 10 bit-times per byte, and 8E1 or 8N2 is 11, which is the number Modbus's frame timing is built on.

The receiver resynchronises on every start bit and samples each bit near its centre, typically with 16× or 8× oversampling and a three-sample majority vote to reject noise. That per-character resync is what limits the tolerable clock error: roughly ±2–3 % total between the two ends. It is also why an internal RC oscillator is often not good enough over temperature and an external crystal is (chapter 25).

Baud rates are nominal, and the peripheral divides the clock down to approximate them. The reference manual tabulates the resulting error per baud rate per clock frequency — check it. This is the single most common cause of “the UART sends garbage”, and the characteristic symptom is that it works at 9600 and fails at 115200 with the same code.

The error flags matter more than beginners expect, and each names a different fault: framing error (the stop bit was not high — wrong baud, or the line was cut mid-character), parity error (a bit flipped), noise error (the oversampling votes disagreed — a signal-integrity problem), overrun (a byte arrived before you read the previous one — entirely your fault). Reading them and logging which one occurred turns an unhelpful bug report into a diagnosis.

Polling, interrupt, DMA

🧒 Simple

Three ways to collect the bytes: keep checking whether one arrived, be told each time one arrives, or have separate hardware put them in a buffer and only be told at the end.

The third is the one that keeps working when the bytes come fast.

🎓 Professional

Polling. Loop on the receive-not-empty flag. Fine for bring-up and for a superloop with nothing else to do. It cannot coexist with deadlines, because the loop either blocks or misses bytes.

Interrupt per byte. The ISR reads one byte into a ring buffer and returns; a task drains it. This is the standard answer and it is correct up to moderate rates. The cost is fixed per byte: at 921600 baud that is about 90,000 interrupts per second, each with entry, exit and its share of latency — by which point the CPU is largely servicing the UART.

DMA. The DMA controller writes arriving bytes straight into memory with no CPU involvement, and you get one interrupt per transfer instead of per byte (chapter 27). This is what makes high rates and idle CPU compatible.

The difficulty with DMA on receive is that you usually do not know the length in advance, and a plain DMA-complete interrupt only fires when the buffer is full. The idiom that solves it, and the one worth being able to describe:

Circular DMA plus idle-line detection. Configure the DMA in circular mode into a ring buffer, and enable the UART's IDLE interrupt, which fires when the line has been quiet for one character time after data. On that interrupt, read the DMA's remaining-count register, subtract to find how many bytes landed, and hand that slice to the parser. Enable the half-transfer and transfer-complete interrupts as well and the same handler covers a message that wraps the buffer. One interrupt per message, hardware framing, no per-byte cost — and it is exactly what Modbus RTU's 3.5-character silence rule wants.

⚠ Trap

An overrun error is not a communication fault, it is a design fault, and on most STM32 parts it stops reception until you clear it. A byte arrived while the previous one was still sitting in the data register, the peripheral set ORE, and depending on configuration it then discards everything until the flag is cleared. The symptom is a link that works and then silently dies, usually under load or the first time somebody attaches a debugger and halts the core.

Two consequences. Always read and clear the error flags in the handler, not just the data register — and read the reference manual's sequence for your part, because on several families clearing requires reading the status register and then the data register in that order. And remember that halting at a breakpoint does not stop the UART (chapter 60): the peripheral keeps receiving while the core is stopped, so stepping through a receive path produces overruns that would never occur in a free-running system.

Try it. A USB-serial adapter is a few euro and is the single most useful thing on an embedded desk after the board itself.

1. On a PC alone, with no hardware, prove the baud-mismatch symptom to
   yourself using two virtual ports (socat on Linux/macOS, com0com on
   Windows) -- or just read the reference manual's baud-error table for
   your part at 115200 and at 9600 and compare the percentages.

2. With a USB-serial adapter, short its TX to its RX. You now have a
   loopback: anything you send comes back. Send at 9600, receive at
   19200, and look at what arrives. That garbage is what every "the UART
   does not work" report looks like.

3. On a board, implement receive three ways in turn and measure CPU load
   with a GPIO pin toggled in the idle task:

     polling            -- works, CPU pinned
     interrupt per byte -- works, load rises linearly with baud
     circular DMA+IDLE  -- one interrupt per message, load flat

   Push the baud rate up until the interrupt version starts dropping
   bytes. Note where. That number is the honest answer to "when do you
   need DMA?" and it is yours.

4. Deliberately provoke an overrun: set a breakpoint in the receive path
   and let the other end keep sending. Watch ORE set, and watch reception
   stop until you clear it.
Golden rules
  • No clock line: both ends must already agree the bit time, and the start bit is the only synchronisation event.
  • 8N1 is 10 bit-times per byte; 8E1 and 8N2 are 11, which is what Modbus frame timing is built on.
  • Tolerable total clock error is about 2 to 3 percent, which is why an internal RC oscillator is often not enough over temperature.
  • Check the reference manual's baud-error table. Works at 9600, fails at 115200 is a clock problem, not a UART problem.
  • Framing, parity, noise and overrun errors each name a different fault. Log which one.
  • Overrun is your fault, not the line's, and on most parts it halts reception until cleared.
  • Interrupt per byte stops scaling somewhere around a hundred thousand interrupts a second.
  • Circular DMA plus idle-line detection gives one interrupt per message and does the framing in hardware.
  • Halting at a breakpoint does not stop the UART.

“Dipende dalla velocità, e il criterio ce l’ho chiaro. In polling solo in fase di bring-up. Un interrupt per byte che scrive in un ring buffer, con un task che lo svuota, è la risposta standard e va benissimo fino a velocità medie — però il costo è fisso per byte, e a 921600 sono circa novantamila interrupt al secondo, a quel punto la CPU fa soprattutto il servizio della UART. Sopra, DMA circolare più rilevamento di linea idle: il DMA scrive in memoria da solo, l’interrupt IDLE scatta quando la linea tace, e la lunghezza la ricavo dal contatore residuo del DMA. Un interrupt per messaggio, e il framing me lo fa l’hardware — che è esattamente quello che serve per il silenzio di 3,5 caratteri del Modbus RTU. Una cosa che leggo e azzero sempre sono i flag di errore, non solo il dato: l’overrun in particolare non è un problema di linea, è colpa mia, e su molti STM32 blocca la ricezione finché non lo pulisco — il sintomo è un link che funziona e poi muore in silenzio sotto carico.”
