Queues, semaphores and mutexes

Three primitives that look interchangeable in the API reference and are not. One signals, one counts, one owns — and choosing wrongly is the most common RTOS design error there is.

From the advert

Not named in this advert, which says “sistemi operativi Real-Time (RTOS)” and stops. These are what that phrase turns out to mean once two pieces of your code need to talk to each other, and the follow-up question is always chapter 41.

One signals, one counts, one owns

🧒 Simple

A queue is a letterbox: you put something in, somebody else takes it out.

A semaphore is a bell: you ring it, somebody who was waiting wakes up. Nothing is carried across, only the fact that something happened.

A mutex is a key to one room. Whoever takes the key is the only one inside, and the same person must put it back.

🎓 Professional

Queue. Transfers data, by copy, between tasks or from an ISR to a task. Fixed capacity and fixed item size, decided at creation, which is what makes it safe without a heap (chapter 11). Copying is deliberate: the sender may reuse or destroy its buffer immediately, and there is no lifetime question. For large items, queue a pointer to a block from a pool instead — and then ownership becomes your problem again, which is the trade.

Binary semaphore. Signals that something happened. No owner, no data, and it does not count beyond one — two gives before a take is indistinguishable from one. The canonical use is an ISR waking a task.

Counting semaphore. Counts events or manages a pool of N identical resources. Each give increments, each take decrements and blocks at zero. This is the one to use when you have three identical UART channels and any of them will do.

Mutex. Mutual exclusion, with an owner. Only the taker may give it back, and that ownership is what makes priority inheritance possible — the kernel knows whom to promote. A recursive mutex additionally lets the same task take it again without deadlocking itself, which is convenient and usually a sign the call graph wants simplifying.

Task notification deserves a mention because it is the cheap modern answer: a 32-bit value and a state held inside the task's own control block, with no separate object to allocate. In FreeRTOS it is significantly faster and smaller than a binary semaphore for the ISR-wakes-one-specific-task case, which is most cases. The limitation is in the name — it targets one task, so it cannot be a broadcast or a many-to-one queue.

Choosing, in one question each

🧒 Simple

Ask: am I sending something, or just saying it happened, or protecting something? Those three questions pick the three tools, and they almost never give an ambiguous answer.

🎓 Professional

Am I moving data? Queue. If the answer is yes, stop here — a semaphore plus a shared global is a queue you wrote badly, with a race between the signal and the write.

Am I only announcing an event? Binary semaphore, or better a task notification. The classic shape: the UART ISR receives a frame, notifies the protocol task, and returns (chapter 42).

Am I protecting a shared resource? Mutex, always, and never a binary semaphore — even though a binary semaphore works for this in the sense that the code runs. It works right up until a medium-priority task turns a bounded wait into an unbounded one, and then it fails intermittently, under load, in the field.

Am I managing N identical things? Counting semaphore.

Two habits that are worth more than the choice itself. Always use a timeout rather than waiting forever: a task blocked for ever is invisible, whereas a task that times out can log, recover or trip the watchdog. And keep the critical section short — every millisecond a mutex is held is a millisecond added to somebody's worst case (chapter 37).

⚠ Trap

Two mutexes taken in two different orders is a deadlock, and no primitive will save you. Task A takes X then wants Y; task B takes Y then wants X; both wait for ever. Priority inheritance does nothing here — both are still waiting, now at elevated priority.

The fix is structural: define a global lock ordering and take locks only in that order, or better, never hold two at once. If you find yourself needing both, that is usually a sign the resources belong behind a single owning task that everybody sends messages to instead.

The related mistake is subtler: giving a mutex from a different task than the one that took it. It is not a locking scheme, it is a signalling scheme wearing a mutex's clothes, and a well-behaved kernel will refuse or assert. If that is what you meant, you wanted a semaphore.

Try it. On a board, or in the FreeRTOS POSIX simulator on a PC — these are scheduling properties, so they reproduce in both.

1. Build the wrong version on purpose. Two tasks incrementing one shared
   counter a million times each, with NO lock. Print the total. It will
   not be two million, and the amount by which it is wrong changes every
   run -- which is exactly why this class of bug is hard to trust a test
   about.

2. Add a mutex around the increment. Now it is two million, every time,
   and slower. That difference is what synchronisation costs.

3. Replace the mutex with a BINARY SEMAPHORE. The count is still correct.
   Everything looks fine. Now go and do the three-task experiment in
   chapter 41 and watch it fail -- the correctness was never the thing
   the mutex was buying you.

4. Deadlock it deliberately: two mutexes, two tasks, opposite orders,
   with a short delay between the two takes so the interleaving is
   reliable. Both tasks stop. Note that nothing reports an error -- the
   system simply goes quiet, which is what a deadlock looks like from
   outside and why a watchdog is not optional.
Golden rules
  • Queue to move data, semaphore to signal an event, mutex to protect a resource.
  • A queue copies by value, which is what removes the lifetime question and lets it work without a heap.
  • A semaphore plus a shared global is a badly written queue, with a race between the signal and the write.
  • Only a mutex has an owner, and only an owner can be promoted by priority inheritance.
  • Never protect a resource with a binary semaphore. It works until it fails intermittently, under load, in the field.
  • A task notification is the cheap modern replacement for the ISR-wakes-one-task semaphore.
  • Always take with a timeout. A task blocked for ever is invisible.
  • Two locks in two orders is a deadlock, and no primitive fixes it. Order them, or restructure around one owning task.

“Quando userebbe un mutex e quando un semaforo?”

“Mi faccio una domanda sola: sto spostando un dato, sto segnalando un evento, o sto proteggendo una risorsa? Se sposto un dato uso una coda, che copia per valore — così chi manda può riusare subito il suo buffer e non ho problemi di lifetime. Se segnalo e basta, un semaforo binario, o meglio ancora una task notification, che in FreeRTOS è più leggera e copre il caso tipico dell’ISR che sveglia un task preciso. Se proteggo una risorsa condivisa, mutex, sempre — mai un semaforo binario, anche se funziona. Funziona finché non arriva un task a priorità media e l’attesa da limitata diventa illimitata: il mutex ha un proprietario, quindi il kernel sa chi promuovere, il semaforo no. Due abitudini che tengo fisse: prendo sempre con un timeout, perché un task bloccato per sempre è invisibile mentre uno che va in timeout può loggare o far scattare il watchdog; e tengo le sezioni critiche corte, perché ogni millisecondo in cui tengo il mutex finisce nel caso peggiore di qualcun altro.”
