Sensors, actuators and the signals between them

The electrical vocabulary of a factory: 24 V logic, PNP and NPN, 4–20 mA and its live zero, thermocouples and RTDs, and the inductive kickback that resets your board. Firmware does not design this, and firmware that does not understand it writes drivers for signals that are not what it thinks.

24 V logic, PNP and NPN

🧒 Simple

On a circuit board, a digital signal is 3.3 V. In a factory, it is 24 V — because a longer cable picks up interference, and a bigger voltage is harder to disturb.

There is also a choice nobody outside industry expects: a sensor can signal “yes” either by connecting the wire to 24 V, or by connecting it to zero. Both are common, they are called PNP and NPN, and wiring one where the other is expected means the input never works — or works permanently.

🎓 Professional
  • PNP (sourcing) — the sensor supplies 24 V to the input when active, and the input has a pull-down. This is the European default and what you will meet most often here.
  • NPN (sinking) — the sensor pulls the input down to 0 V when active, and the input has a pull-up. Common in Asian equipment.
  • The standard that defines the thresholds is IEC 61131-2, which specifies input types by voltage and current: broadly, below about 5 V is off, above about 15 V is on, and the band between is undefined. A design that reads “undefined” as a level rather than as a fault is a design that behaves differently on a long cable.

Between that 24 V world and your 3.3 V pin there is always conditioning: a divider with clamping, or more usually an opto-isolator, which also breaks the ground connection — important when the sensor's ground and the board's ground are metres apart and not at the same potential. The firmware consequences are the ones from chapter 63: the stage usually inverts, it adds delay (an opto with a slow output can take hundreds of microseconds, which matters if you are timing an encoder), and it means the level at your pin is not the level in the field. Put that translation in exactly one named function.

Two habits worth building in. Debounce and filter every field input on a periodic tick (chapter 31), because contacts bounce and cables pick up noise from contactors switching. And read back what you drive where it matters: an output with a readback or a current sense tells you the coil is actually energised, which is the difference between “I commanded the valve” and “the valve moved”.

Analogue: 4–20 mA, thermocouples, RTDs

🧒 Simple

Measurements travel down factory cables as a current, not a voltage, because current is not weakened by a long wire. The scale runs from 4 to 20 milliamps, and the fact that it starts at 4 rather than 0 is deliberate: a broken wire gives 0, which is outside the scale, so the receiver can tell the difference between “the value is zero” and “the sensor is gone”.

Temperature is measured with two common devices, and neither gives you degrees directly: one gives a very small voltage that depends on the temperature difference between its two ends, and the other is simply a resistor whose value changes with temperature.

🎓 Professional
  • 4–20 mA, read across a precision shunt (typically 165 Ω for a 0–3.3 V span) and then through the ADC chain of chapter 33. The live zero is the design lesson worth carrying everywhere: below about 3.6 mA is a broken wire or a failed transmitter, above about 21 mA is a short, and both are faults, not readings. Some transmitters also use a NAMUR convention to signal a self-detected fault by driving deliberately out of range.
  • 0–10 V exists too, is cheaper, and is worse over distance because the voltage drops along the cable and ground offsets add directly to the measurement.
  • Thermocouples produce tens of microvolts per degree from the difference between the measuring junction and the connection terminals, so you must measure the terminal temperature separately and add it back: cold junction compensation. Forgetting it gives a reading that is wrong by room temperature and drifts with the weather — a classic and very confusing first bug. The type letter (K, J, T) selects a different non-linear curve, implemented as a polynomial or a lookup table.
  • RTDs (Pt100, Pt1000) are far more linear and more accurate. The catch is that you are measuring a resistance through a cable whose own resistance is in series with it, which is why three- and four-wire connections exist — they cancel the lead resistance. A Pt100 read with two wires over twenty metres reads several degrees high, consistently, and the mistake is invisible until somebody checks against a reference thermometer.
  • Dedicated front-end chips — MAX31865 for RTDs, MAX31856 for thermocouples — do the excitation, amplification, linearisation and fault detection and hand you a number over SPI. For a junior building a product, using one is usually the right engineering choice and is not an admission of weakness.

Driving things, and what they do back to you

🧒 Simple

A microcontroller pin can light an LED. It cannot run a valve, a relay or a motor — those need far more current, and they push back. When you switch off anything with a coil in it, the coil fights the change and produces a large voltage spike.

That spike is what resets boards, corrupts communications and produces faults that only happen “sometimes, when the machine is working”.

🎓 Professional
  • Between the pin and the load there is always a driver: a transistor, a MOSFET, a dedicated high-side switch, or a relay. Intelligent drivers are worth asking for — they report open load, overcurrent and over-temperature back to firmware, which turns “commanded” into “confirmed”.
  • Inductive kickback is handled by a flyback diode across a DC coil, or an RC snubber and often a varistor on AC. It is the hardware team's job, and it is the firmware engineer's job to recognise the symptom: resets, corrupted UART bytes or ADC glitches that correlate exactly with a relay switching (chapter 62 shows it on a rail in one capture).
  • Solid-state versus mechanical relays. Mechanical relays have finite contact life — hundreds of thousands of operations — which makes rapid cycling a design error rather than a nuisance. If your control law chatters a relay, the relay is the component that dies, and adding hysteresis in firmware is what stops it.
  • Valves, motors and heaters have physics. A valve takes hundreds of milliseconds to travel; a large motor must not be restarted immediately after stopping; a heater has thermal inertia that dominates any control loop. Minimum on and off times, and interlocks against impossible combinations, belong in firmware because firmware is the only place that knows the sequence.
  • Safety is not firmware's to claim. An emergency stop is hardwired to remove power through certified safety relays, and it is independent of your software by design (chapter 65). You must know it exists, know your software cannot override it, and know that after an E-stop your state machine restarts from a defined, safe state rather than resuming.
⚠ Trap

A missing sensor reads as a valid value, and the machine acts on it.

A disconnected 0–10 V input on a floating pin reads whatever the pin drifts to, and 0 V is a legitimate value in the range. A thermocouple whose wire has broken reads a number, not an error. A two-wire Pt100 with a corroded terminal reads high but plausible. In every case the controller receives a number that passes any sanity test written in terms of “is it inside the range”, and acts on it — which means a cooling system can run the pump on the strength of a temperature that no longer exists.

The discipline is to make the absence of a signal detectable, and then to detect it. That is exactly why 4–20 mA has a live zero, and the same instinct generalises into four checks that should exist for every field input in every industrial product:

  • Out-of-range detection with explicit fault codes — below 3.6 mA is broken wire, not minimum value.
  • Rate-of-change checks: a liquid temperature that moves 40 degrees in one sample is a sensor fault, because water has thermal mass.
  • Cross-checks between sensors that must agree — an inlet and outlet temperature that diverge impossibly, or a pump commanded on with no flow measured.
  • Stuck-value detection: a reading that has not changed by even one count in ten minutes on a live process is suspicious, and it is the only way to catch a sensor that has frozen rather than failed.

And each detected fault needs a defined behaviour chosen deliberately — hold last value, go to a safe default, shut down, or run degraded — because “keep using the number” is what happens when nobody decides. That decision is a requirement, it should be written down, and being the person who asks for it is what configurare l’interfaccia actually means.

Try it. A few euros of components on a breadboard cover most of this, and a 24 V supply is the only unusual item.

1. Build a 4-20 mA receiver: a 165 ohm precision resistor to ground,
   into an ADC input. Use a bench supply and a resistor decade, or a
   cheap current-loop simulator, to sweep 4 to 20 mA and calibrate.
   Then write the scaling with explicit fault bands: below 3.6 mA is
   BROKEN_WIRE, above 21 mA is SHORT, and neither is a number.

2. Read a Pt1000 with two wires and then with four, over a long
   cable, and compare. Seeing the two-wire error appear as a
   consistent offset -- and disappear with four wires -- makes lead
   resistance real rather than theoretical.

3. Make the cold-junction mistake on purpose with a thermocouple
   module: read the raw voltage, convert without compensation, and
   watch the answer be wrong by the room temperature. Then add the
   board temperature back and watch it come right.

4. Opto-isolate an input. Feed 24 V through an opto to a GPIO, and
   look at the switching edge on a logic analyser: note the delay and
   the inversion. Then write the one named function in the hardware
   layer that turns "pin low" into "field signal active".

5. Watch the kickback. Switch a 24 V relay coil WITHOUT a flyback
   diode while a scope is on the 3.3 V rail -- from a safe distance,
   and not on a board you love. Then add the diode and look again.
   That comparison explains a whole category of "it only happens when
   the machine runs" faults.

6. Then implement the four plausibility checks on one real input:
   range, rate of change, cross-check and stuck value. Decide what
   each fault does. That code is exactly what an industrial product
   ships with, and almost no portfolio project has it.
Golden rules
  • Field logic is 24 V, and PNP sourcing versus NPN sinking must be matched or the input never works.
  • The conditioning stage usually inverts and always delays. Put the translation in one named function.
  • Debounce and filter every field input on a periodic tick.
  • 4–20 mA has a live zero on purpose: below 3.6 mA is a broken wire, above 21 mA a short, and both are faults rather than readings.
  • 0–10 V is cheaper and worse over distance, because drop and ground offset add to the measurement.
  • Thermocouples need cold junction compensation, or the reading is wrong by room temperature.
  • Pt100 needs three or four wires over any distance, or lead resistance reads as temperature.
  • A dedicated front-end chip is usually the right choice, not a weakness.
  • Never drive a coil from a pin, and expect the hardware to carry a flyback diode or snubber.
  • Relay chatter is a design error. Add hysteresis and minimum on and off times.
  • Read back what you drive where it matters, so commanded becomes confirmed.
  • Range, rate of change, cross-check, stuck value — four checks per input, each with a decided behaviour on fault.
  • The emergency stop is hardwired and independent of your software, and your state machine restarts safely after it.

“Come gestisce un sensore che si guasta?”

“Parto dal presupposto che un sensore guasto quasi sempre non dà un errore, dà un numero plausibile: un ingresso 0-10 V staccato legge quello che capita, una termocoppia con il filo rotto legge un valore, una Pt100 a due fili con un morsetto ossidato legge alto ma credibile. Per questo il 4-20 mA ha lo zero vivo: sotto 3,6 mA è filo rotto, sopra 21 è corto, e nessuno dei due è una misura — è un guasto con il suo codice. La stessa logica la applico a tutti gli ingressi con quattro controlli: fuori range; velocità di variazione, perché un liquido che fa quaranta gradi in un campione è il sensore, non il processo; incrocio fra sensori che devono essere coerenti, tipo pompa comandata e portata a zero; e valore bloccato, perché un sensore congelato non lo prende nessun controllo di range. E poi la parte che secondo me è il vero lavoro: decidere cosa fare per ogni guasto — tengo l’ultimo valore, vado su un default sicuro, fermo l’impianto o vado in degradato. Se nessuno lo decide, il comportamento di default è ‘continuo a usare il numero sbagliato’. Quello lo scrivo come requisito e lo chiedo, perché è esattamente quello che vuol dire configurare l’interfaccia.”
