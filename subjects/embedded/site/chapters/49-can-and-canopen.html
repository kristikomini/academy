CAN and CANopen

A bus with no master, where priority is decided by the message identifier and collisions are resolved without losing a single bit. Then CANopen on top of it: the object dictionary, PDO, SDO and NMT — four acronyms that describe how an industrial device is expected to behave.

From the advert

Not named in this advert, but CAN and CANopen appear across the Modena machinery, vehicle and motion listings collected alongside it, and they are the usual answer when a machine has intelligent actuators rather than dumb ones. LogiFlow chapter 32d introduces the frame from the PC side; here you configure the controller.

Arbitration: the idea that makes CAN different

🧒 Simple

On most buses, if two devices talk at once the message is ruined and both have to try again.

CAN is built so that cannot happen. Every message starts with a number, its identifier. As the devices send that number bit by bit, each one also listens. A zero on the wire always wins over a one — so a device that sent a one and hears a zero knows somebody more important is talking, and stops immediately.

The important part: the winner does not notice anything, and its message is not delayed or repeated. The loser simply tries again afterwards. No time is wasted at all.

🎓 Professional

A differential pair, like RS-485, with 120 Ω termination at both ends — but the two bus states are asymmetric. Dominant (logical 0) actively drives the pair apart; recessive (logical 1) lets it relax. So a dominant bit always overwrites a recessive one, and the bus is a wired-AND.

Arbitration follows directly: every transmitter monitors the bus while sending the identifier, and a node that transmits recessive but reads dominant has lost and withdraws immediately. This is non-destructive — the highest-priority message continues undisturbed and arrives with no retransmission and no delay. A lower numeric identifier is higher priority, and identifier assignment is therefore a real-time design decision, not an administrative one.

Frames are small and message-oriented rather than address-oriented: an 11-bit identifier (standard) or 29-bit (extended), 0 to 8 data bytes, CRC, and an ACK slot in which every receiving node asserts a dominant bit. Nobody is addressed; nodes filter on identifier and take what concerns them. CAN FD extends the payload to 64 bytes and allows a faster bit rate during the data phase.

The feature that matters most in practice is hardware error handling. Every controller keeps a transmit and a receive error counter. Errors increase them, successful frames decrease them, and at 128 a node goes error passive; at 256 it goes bus off and stops transmitting entirely until it is recovered. That is a deliberate design: a node with a fault removes itself from the bus rather than jamming it. Read those counters and expose them — they are the best diagnostic any field bus gives you, and a rising TEC points at wiring or termination long before anything stops working.

Bit timing is the configuration people get wrong: the bit is divided into segments and the sample point is placed at roughly 75–87.5 % of the way through. Every node on the bus must agree on bit rate and, in practice, have a compatible sample point. A mismatched sample point gives a bus that works on the bench and errors on a long cable, because propagation delay is what the segment placement is compensating for.

CANopen: what the bytes mean

🧒 Simple

CAN moves eight bytes and says nothing about what they mean. CANopen is the agreement on top: a standard catalogue of numbered settings and values that every device has, and standard ways to read them, write them, and start or stop the device.

🎓 Professional

The central concept is the object dictionary: every parameter and process value a device has, addressed by a 16-bit index and an 8-bit sub-index. Standard ranges are fixed by the specification — 0x1000–0x1FFF is communication parameters, 0x6000–0x9FFF is the device profile — so a drive from one maker looks much like a drive from another. The device ships with an EDS file describing its dictionary, which configuration tools read.

Four mechanisms sit on top, and being able to say which is which is the useful knowledge:

  • PDO — Process Data Object. Real-time process values, up to 8 bytes, no protocol overhead and no confirmation, sent cyclically or on change. This is where a motor position or a temperature travels. Which dictionary entries a PDO carries is configured by mapping, which is itself done over SDO.
  • SDO — Service Data Object. Confirmed read/write access to any dictionary entry, with a request and a response, able to transfer objects larger than 8 bytes by segmenting. Used for configuration, not for a control loop.
  • NMT — Network Management. The state machine every node has: Initialising, Pre-operational, Operational, Stopped. PDOs flow only in Operational, which is the answer to the commonest CANopen complaint, namely that a device is on the bus and sends nothing.
  • Heartbeat (and the older node guarding). Each node periodically announces its NMT state, and the absence of a heartbeat is how the master detects a missing device — the same reasoning as the heartbeat counter in chapter 58, standardised.

There is also SYNC, a broadcast that makes nodes sample inputs or apply outputs simultaneously, and EMCY, a high-priority emergency message carrying an error code. Node IDs run 1–127 and the default identifiers are derived from them, which is why two devices left at the factory default node ID conflict immediately.

⚠ Trap

A single node with the wrong bit rate takes down the entire bus, and the symptom points everywhere except at it.

A node configured at 125 kbit/s on a 250 kbit/s bus does not simply fail to understand traffic. It misreads valid frames as malformed and transmits error frames — which, being dominant bits, actively destroy everyone else's messages. Every node's error counters rise, several go bus-off, and the bus appears entirely broken. The node that is wrong is frequently the one that looks healthiest.

Two habits. First, when a CAN bus misbehaves, read every node's TEC and REC before touching anything: the counters tell you far more than the traffic does. Second, on a bench, bring nodes up one at a time. It takes ten minutes and converts an unbounded search into a bounded one.

The same asymmetry catches people testing with two nodes: a lone transmitter with nothing else on the bus will go error-passive and then bus-off, because nobody is acknowledging — the ACK slot needs at least one other listening node. A CAN node cannot successfully talk to itself, and “my board transmits and immediately goes bus-off” is almost always this, not a fault.

Try it. Two USB-CAN adapters are about thirty euro the pair, and CAN is much easier to learn with real traffic than from a diagram.

1. Two nodes, twisted pair, 120 ohm at each end. On Linux, SocketCAN
   gives you the tools for free:

     ip link set can0 up type can bitrate 250000
     candump can0
     cansend can0 123#DEADBEEF

2. Watch arbitration happen. Have both nodes transmit continuously,
   one with identifier 0x100 and one with 0x101, and look at the order
   on the analyser. The lower identifier wins every contested slot, and
   -- the part worth internalising -- it is never delayed or repeated.

3. Set ONE node to the wrong bit rate and watch the whole bus collapse.
   Then read both nodes' error counters and see which one is actually
   at fault. Do this once; it is the failure you will meet in the field.

4. Disconnect one node so only a transmitter remains. Watch the error
   counter climb and the node go bus-off, because nothing is filling
   the ACK slot. Knowing this stops you debugging a non-problem.

5. For CANopen: CANopenNode is an open-source stack you can read and
   run. Bring a node to Operational with an NMT start message and watch
   the PDOs begin -- then set it back to Pre-operational and watch them
   stop. That transition is the answer to most "it sends nothing"
   reports.
Golden rules
  • Dominant beats recessive, so the bus is a wired-AND and arbitration is non-destructive.
  • The winner is never delayed or retransmitted, and a lower identifier is higher priority — so identifier assignment is a real-time design decision.
  • Nodes are not addressed. Frames carry an identifier and receivers filter on it.
  • Error counters, error-passive at 128 and bus-off at 256, are the best diagnostic any field bus offers. Expose them.
  • A faulty node removes itself rather than jamming the bus. That is deliberate.
  • Every node must agree on bit rate and sample point, and a sample-point mismatch works on the bench and fails on a long cable.
  • One node at the wrong bit rate destroys the whole bus, and the counters find it faster than the traffic does.
  • A lone transmitter goes bus-off because nobody fills the ACK slot.
  • CANopen adds an object dictionary: PDO for process data, SDO for configuration, NMT for state, heartbeat for liveness.
  • PDOs only flow in the Operational state, which explains most “it sends nothing” reports.

“Ha lavorato con CAN? Mi spieghi come funziona l’arbitraggio.”

“CAN non ha un master: tutti possono parlare, e il conflitto si risolve bit a bit sull’identificatore. Il bus è un AND cablato — lo stato dominante, lo zero, sovrascrive il recessivo — e ogni trasmettitore rilegge quello che manda: se manda un uno e legge uno zero ha perso e si ritira subito. La cosa elegante è che non è distruttivo: chi vince non si accorge di niente, il suo messaggio arriva senza ritardo e senza ritrasmissione, e chi ha perso riprova dopo. Quindi l’identificatore più basso ha priorità più alta, e assegnare gli ID è una scelta di progetto real-time, non una convenzione. La cosa che uso di più in diagnostica sono i contatori di errore: a 128 il nodo va error passive, a 256 va bus off e si stacca da solo invece di disturbare. Se un bus CAN fa i capricci, la prima cosa che guardo sono i contatori di tutti i nodi, perché un solo nodo con il bit rate sbagliato manda error frame e distrugge i messaggi di tutti gli altri — e sembra guasto tutto tranne lui. Su CANopen la cosa pratica da sapere è che i PDO viaggiano solo nello stato Operational: metà dei ‘il dispositivo non manda niente’ sono un NMT start mai inviato.”
