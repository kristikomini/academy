Tasks, the scheduler and priorities

Each task is written as though it owns the CPU and runs forever. The scheduler maintains that illusion, and the price of the illusion is that a priority you chose in five seconds decides which deadline gets missed.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Sviluppare e ottimizzare firmware in C/C++ su microcontrollori e sistemi operativi Real-Time (RTOS)”. It is the concrete half of chapter 37: that one defines the deadline, this one is the mechanism you use to meet it.

One CPU, several programs that do not know it

🧒 Simple

A task is an ordinary function with a loop in it that never ends. You write it as though it is the only thing running: read the sensor, wait a bit, read it again, forever.

There is only one processor, so only one task really runs at a time. The scheduler swaps between them fast enough, and at the right moments, that each one can be written as though nothing else exists. That is the whole reason an RTOS is worth having: you stop writing one enormous machine that does everything a bit at a time.

🎓 Professional

A task is a function plus its own stack plus a control block holding its saved registers, priority and state. It never returns — the loop is infinite by contract, and a task that falls off the end of its function is undefined behaviour in most kernels (FreeRTOS calls a configurable hook and then usually traps).

Its states, and the transitions between them are most of what you reason about:

  • Running — on the CPU. Exactly one task, always.
  • Ready — could run, but something higher-priority is running.
  • Blocked — waiting for something with a timeout: a queue item, a semaphore, a delay. Costs no CPU at all, which is the distinction that matters.
  • Suspended — taken out of scheduling explicitly, waiting for nothing. Rare, and usually a sign the design wants a different primitive.

A context switch saves the running task's registers to its stack, updates the control block, and restores another's. On a Cortex-M the kernel does this inside the PendSV handler, set to the lowest interrupt priority deliberately, so that a switch can never pre-empt a real device interrupt — and SysTick provides the periodic tick that expires delays and timeouts (chapter 26). Those two exceptions are the entire scheduler mechanism on this architecture, which is worth knowing because it explains the priority rules in chapter 42.

The scheduler is pre-emptive and priority-based: at every moment, the highest-priority ready task runs. It switches the instant a higher-priority task becomes ready — typically inside the interrupt that made it ready — not at the next tick. Among tasks of equal priority, the kernel time-slices round-robin if configured to, and otherwise a running task keeps the CPU until it blocks.

Choosing priorities, which is the actual design

🧒 Simple

Priority is not importance. It is urgency — how soon this has to happen after it becomes possible. Logging to a memory card is very important and not at all urgent; toggling a motor output is not important at all and must happen now.

🎓 Professional

The single most common beginner error is assigning priority by importance. The safety check feels like the most important thing in the system, so it gets the top priority, and then it spends its life running a 40 ms comparison that starves the motor commutation. Priority is about deadline tightness, nothing else.

The defensible default is rate-monotonic: for periodic tasks whose deadline equals their period, assign priority by rate — shorter period, higher priority. It is provably optimal among fixed-priority schemes, and being able to name it is worth more in an interview than any particular assignment you made.

Practical rules that follow from it:

Few levels, not many. Three or four bands — control, communication, housekeeping — is easier to reason about than fifteen distinct numbers, and it makes the intent readable. Define them as named constants, never as bare integers at the call site.

Every task must block. A task that never blocks at its own priority starves everything below it permanently. This is the number-one cause of “the RTOS is broken” reports, and it is not the RTOS.

Use vTaskDelayUntil for periodic work, not vTaskDelay. vTaskDelay(10) waits 10 ms from now, so the period becomes 10 ms plus however long the body took, and it drifts. vTaskDelayUntil wakes at a fixed absolute time, which is what “every 10 ms” actually means.

The idle task is not wasted time. It runs at priority 0, frees memory from deleted tasks, and is where you put the sleep instruction and the CPU-load measurement. A system whose idle task never runs has no headroom, and you want to know that number.

⚠ Trap

Every task needs its own stack, sized for its worst case, and getting it wrong does not produce an error message. It produces a variable in an unrelated task changing by itself.

Each stack must hold that task's deepest call chain, its locals, anything the compiler spills, and — because interrupts are stacked onto whichever stack is current — the deepest legal interrupt nesting chain on top. A printf or a floating-point library call in a task that you sized for 128 words will quietly walk off the end and into whatever is next in RAM.

Do not guess. Turn on the kernel's stack painting (FreeRTOS fills stacks with a known pattern and uxTaskGetStackHighWaterMark reports the closest approach), run the system through its worst paths, and read the watermarks. Size to the measured worst case plus a real margin, and re-check after adding any library. Chapter 12 is the long version.

Try it. On a board, or in the FreeRTOS POSIX simulator on a PC — scheduling behaviour reproduces in both.

1. Three tasks, priorities 1, 2 and 3, each toggling its own GPIO pin.
   Put them on a logic analyser. You are now watching the scheduler,
   which is otherwise entirely invisible.

2. Make the priority-2 task busy-wait for 500 ms instead of blocking:

     for (volatile uint32_t i = 0; i < 5000000; i++) { }

   Priority 1 stops completely. Priority 3 is unaffected. That single
   picture is what "starvation" means, and it is the most common cause
   of an RTOS bug report.

3. Replace the busy-wait with vTaskDelay(500). Priority 1 comes back,
   because BLOCKED costs no CPU while READY-but-not-running does.

4. Now measure the drift. Task body takes ~3 ms; use vTaskDelay(10) and
   timestamp every wake for a minute. The period is 13 ms, not 10, and
   it wanders. Switch to vTaskDelayUntil and it is 10 ms, flat.

5. Finally, read uxTaskGetStackHighWaterMark for each task after the
   run. Then add one printf to a task and read it again. That drop is
   the reason stack sizes are measured rather than guessed.
Golden rules
  • A task is a function plus its own stack plus a control block, and its loop never returns.
  • Blocked costs no CPU; ready-but-not-running does not run. That distinction is most of RTOS reasoning.
  • The highest-priority ready task runs, and it switches the instant one becomes ready — not at the next tick.
  • Priority is urgency, not importance. Assigning by importance is the classic beginner error.
  • Rate-monotonic is the defensible default: shorter period, higher priority.
  • Few named priority bands beat many bare numbers.
  • Every task must block somewhere, or it starves everything below it permanently.
  • vTaskDelayUntil for periodic work — vTaskDelay drifts by the length of the body.
  • Size every stack from a measured high-water mark, not a guess, and re-measure after adding a library.
  • On Cortex-M the scheduler is PendSV plus SysTick, with PendSV at the lowest priority so a switch never pre-empts a device interrupt.

“Come assegna le priorità ai task?”

“Parto da un principio: la priorità è urgenza, non importanza. L’errore tipico è dare la priorità massima al controllo di sicurezza perché ‘è la cosa più importante’, e poi quello si mangia la CPU con un confronto da 40 millisecondi e affama la commutazione del motore. Come default uso rate-monotonic: per i task periodici con scadenza uguale al periodo, periodo più corto significa priorità più alta — è dimostrabilmente ottimale fra gli schemi a priorità fissa. In pratica tengo tre o quattro fasce con nomi — controllo, comunicazione, servizio — non quindici numeri sparsi. Due regole che non rompo: ogni task deve bloccarsi da qualche parte, altrimenti affama per sempre tutto quello che sta sotto, ed è la causa numero uno dei ‘non funziona l’RTOS’; e per il lavoro periodico uso vTaskDelayUntil e non vTaskDelay, perché il secondo aspetta da adesso e il periodo diventa dieci millisecondi più la durata del corpo, e deriva. Gli stack non li stimo: faccio girare il sistema sui percorsi peggiori e leggo gli high water mark.”
