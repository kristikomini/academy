The debugger: SWD, breakpoints, and why they lie

Two wires that can stop a processor mid-instruction and read its memory. What that buys you, what it costs, and the category of bug it cannot see — because halting a core changes the thing you were trying to observe.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Collaborare con il team hardware per il debug e la validazione dei sistemi” — a line that appeared in the adverts this course was built from, and one of only two that names debugging directly. It is the software half of that sentence; chapter 62 is the half you do standing next to the hardware engineer.

What SWD actually is

🧒 Simple

Inside the chip, next to the processor, there is a small piece of hardware whose only job is to let an outside tool look in. It has its own two wires to the outside world: one for data, one for a clock.

Through those two wires a tool can read and write any memory address, stop the processor, restart it, and run it one instruction at a time — without any help from your program at all. That last part matters: it works even when your program has crashed completely.

🎓 Professional

SWD — Serial Wire Debug — is ARM's two-pin replacement for JTAG: SWDIO and SWCLK, plus ground and a reference to the target voltage, and optionally NRST and SWO. It reaches the debug access port, which is a bus master in its own right on the bus matrix — which is why a debugger can read RAM while the core is running, and why that read competes with your code for bus bandwidth.

The hardware inside the core, all of it architecturally defined and therefore the same on every vendor's Cortex-M:

  • FPB, the breakpoint unit. Hardware breakpoints are a small, fixed number — typically 6 on an M4, 2 on an M0 — because each one is a comparator in silicon. Exceed them and the IDE either refuses or silently falls back to software breakpoints, which work by patching a BKPT instruction into the code and therefore only work in RAM, not in flash.
  • DWT, the watchpoint and trace unit: break on a data address being read or written (typically four of them), plus the free-running CYCCNT cycle counter, which is the cheapest accurate timing instrument you have (chapter 43).
  • ITM, which feeds SWO tracing.

The tools: ST-LINK on every Nucleo and Discovery board, and detachable for a custom board; J-Link, faster, with better flash algorithms and RTT; and OpenOCD plus GDB underneath most of them, which is what you reach for when you want scripting or a build server. Knowing that the IDE is a front end for GDB matters because the GDB command line does things no IDE button does, and because a CI job cannot click.

Two options worth knowing before you need them. Connect under reset holds NRST low while attaching, which is the only way onto a board whose firmware reconfigures the SWD pins or enters a low-power mode immediately (chapter 31). And attach without reset connects to a running system without disturbing it, which is how you inspect a machine that has been misbehaving for three hours without losing its state.

Using it well

🧒 Simple

Most people use a debugger in one way: put a breakpoint somewhere, look at variables, step forward. That is the least powerful thing it does.

The stronger moves are: stop when a value changes rather than when a line is reached; look at the peripheral registers rather than at your variables; and look at the list of function calls that got you here, which usually answers the question on its own.

🎓 Professional
  • Watchpoints are the underused feature. “This variable becomes 0xFF and I cannot find who does it” is a five-minute problem with a data watchpoint and an afternoon without one. It is also the fastest way to catch a stack overflow eating a global.
  • The peripheral register view is the ground truth. Every claim in Parts 4 and 5 of this course — the clock is not enabled, the alternate function is wrong, the configuration did not take — is confirmed or refuted there in seconds. Registers cannot be wrong about what the hardware believes.
  • The call stack, and the fault path. After a hard fault the stacked frame gives you the faulting PC (chapter 22); a decent IDE will unwind it for you if you point it at the right stack.
  • Read memory while running. Because the debug port is a bus master, most tools can show a live variable without halting — enormously better than a breakpoint for anything periodic.
  • Scripted sessions. A GDB script that attaches, dumps a fault record from no-init RAM, and detaches turns a field board into a one-command diagnostic, and it can run from a build server.

And the habit that separates a fast engineer from a slow one: form a hypothesis before setting a breakpoint. A debugger answers questions; it does not generate them. Stepping through code hoping to notice something is the slowest debugging technique there is, and it is the default for most people. State what you expect to be true, pick the one observation that would disprove it, and go get exactly that.

The category it cannot see

🧒 Simple

A debugger works by stopping time for the processor. But the world outside the chip does not stop: a motor keeps turning, bytes keep arriving on a serial line, a timer keeps counting.

So the moment you stop, the situation you were investigating is gone. For anything to do with timing, the act of looking destroys the thing being looked at.

🎓 Professional

Concretely, what breaks when you halt:

  • Peripherals keep running by default. A UART overruns, a CAN controller's error counters climb to bus-off, DMA completes into a buffer you are watching, and a motor keeps spinning with its last PWM value — which is a safety issue, not an inconvenience. There are debug freeze bits for timers and the watchdog, and you should set them; there is nothing equivalent for the physical world.
  • The watchdog resets you unless frozen, so long inspections end in a reboot (chapter 28).
  • Heisenbugs. Any race, any missed-deadline bug, any interrupt-priority inversion changes character or disappears when single-stepped. If a bug goes away under the debugger, that is information: it is timing-related, and you should switch instruments to tracing or a pin and a scope.
  • Low-power modes do not behave. SWD keeps the core powered, so current measurements are meaningless and some Stop-mode paths simply do not execute as they would in production.
  • Optimised code lies visually. At -O2 variables live in registers and are “optimised out”, and the program counter jumps around because statements were reordered. That is not a broken debugger; it is the compiler doing its job, and -Og exists as the compromise.
⚠ Trap

The bug only happens in the field, and every attempt to attach a debugger makes it go away — so the investigation stalls on the instrument rather than on the problem.

This is the central limitation of interactive debugging in embedded work, and the way out is to stop trying to catch the bug live and instead make the system record its own evidence. The pattern, which is worth building once and carrying between projects:

  • A no-init RAM region (a linker section that startup does not clear, chapter 36) holding a small crash record: the stacked PC and LR, CFSR, the reset cause, uptime, and a few application-defined slots such as the current state-machine state.
  • A fault handler and a watchdog-reset path that fill it in, then reset.
  • A ring buffer of the last N events — not text, just small numbered records with a timestamp — also in no-init RAM.
  • A way to read all of it back over the normal interface after the restart: a diagnostic command on the field bus, or a ReadDataByIdentifier in automotive.

That costs a day to build and turns “it crashed at the customer’s site on Tuesday” into an address and a sequence of events, with no debugger present and nobody driving anywhere. It is also the single most convincing thing a junior can describe having built, because it shows you understand that the debugger is one instrument among several rather than the definition of debugging.

Try it. A Nucleo has the debugger built in, so all of this is free.

1. Set a data watchpoint, not a breakpoint. Declare a global, corrupt
   it from two different places, and let the debugger tell you which
   one did it. Most people have never done this once; it is the
   highest-value five minutes in this chapter.

2. Find the hardware breakpoint limit on your part. Add breakpoints
   one at a time until the IDE complains or starts behaving oddly.
   Knowing the number (6 on most M4s, 2 on an M0) explains a lot of
   confusing IDE behaviour.

3. Open the peripheral register view and re-do one bug from chapter
   25 deliberately: comment out a clock enable, then find it in the
   register view rather than in the source. Thirty seconds versus
   thirty minutes.

4. Observe the Heisenbug. Write a tight loop that increments a
   counter, and an ISR at 10 kHz that increments another. Break,
   step, continue -- and watch the relationship between the two
   counters become nonsense. That is why timing bugs need a different
   instrument.

5. Build the crash recorder. A no-init section, a fault handler that
   stores PC, LR, CFSR and the reset cause, and a UART command that
   prints the last record. Then crash the board on purpose with a
   null pointer, reset it, and read the record back. Keep this code
   forever; paste it into every project.

6. Then do it from the command line: openocd plus arm-none-eabi-gdb,
   a script that attaches, dumps that memory region and exits. Now a
   field board is a one-command diagnostic and CI could run it.
Golden rules
  • SWD is two wires to a debug port that is itself a bus master, which is why it can read RAM while the core runs.
  • Hardware breakpoints are a small fixed number, and software breakpoints only work in RAM.
  • Watchpoints answer “who wrote this variable?” in minutes. Use them.
  • The peripheral register view is ground truth. Check it before rereading your source.
  • Form a hypothesis before setting a breakpoint. A debugger answers questions; stepping hopefully is the slowest method there is.
  • Halting does not stop the world. Motors turn, UARTs overrun, CAN goes bus-off.
  • Set the debug freeze bits for timers and the watchdog, and remember there is no freeze bit for a machine.
  • If the bug disappears under the debugger, it is a timing bug. Change instrument rather than technique.
  • Current measurements with SWD attached are meaningless.
  • “Optimised out” is the compiler working, not the debugger failing. Use -Og.
  • Make the system record its own evidence — crash record and event ring in no-init RAM, readable over the normal interface. That is what works in the field.

“Un bug si presenta solo in campo e col debugger sparisce. Come procede?”

“Intanto quello per me è già un’informazione: se sparisce quando lo guardo, è un problema di tempi, quindi cambio strumento invece di insistere. Fermare il core non ferma il mondo — il motore continua a girare, la UART va in overrun, il CAN arriva a bus off — quindi col debugger interattivo quel tipo di difetto non lo prendo. Quello che faccio è far registrare al sistema le proprie prove: una regione di RAM non inizializzata, che lo startup non azzera, con dentro un record di crash — PC e LR impilati, CFSR, causa del reset, uptime, lo stato della macchina a stati — e un ring buffer degli ultimi eventi, non testo ma record piccoli con timestamp. Il fault handler e il percorso di reset da watchdog lo riempiono, e poi lo rileggo dopo il riavvio con un comando diagnostico sulla stessa interfaccia che il dispositivo ha già: un blocco Modbus, o un ReadDataByIdentifier in automotive. Costa una giornata e trasforma ‘è crashato dal cliente martedì’ in un indirizzo e una sequenza di eventi, senza mandare nessuno sul posto. Il debugger lo uso eccome, ma soprattutto per i watchpoint — chi mi sta scrivendo questa variabile — e per la vista dei registri delle periferiche, che è la verità: se un registro rileggo zero, il problema è il clock, non il mio codice.”
