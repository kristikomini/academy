UDS: the diagnostic language of the ECU

ISO 14229. One request byte, one response byte that is the request plus 0x40, and a negative response that tells you precisely why you were refused. Sessions, security access, the response-pending byte that is not an error, and the ten-message sequence that reflashes a control unit.

The shape of a request, and the answer that is not an error

🧒 Simple

UDS is a small language for asking a control unit questions: what is your part number, what is that sensor reading, clear your fault memory, restart, take this new software. Every question starts with one byte saying which question.

A good answer starts with that same byte plus 0x40, so a request 0x22 comes back as 0x62 and you can tell at a glance that it worked. A refusal always looks the same: 0x7F, then the question you asked, then one byte of reason — not allowed right now, not in this mode, I do not know that identifier, you have not authenticated.

And there is one special refusal that is not a refusal at all: 0x78 means “I heard you, this takes a while, keep waiting”.

🎓 Professional

The service identifier is the first byte of the payload ISO-TP carries. A positive response is SID + 0x40. A negative response is always three bytes: 0x7F, the SID you sent, and a negative response code. The ones worth knowing by name, because they are what a log shows you:

  • 0x11 serviceNotSupported and 0x12 subFunctionNotSupported — this ECU does not implement it at all.
  • 0x13 incorrectMessageLengthOrInvalidFormat — nearly always your framing, not their firmware.
  • 0x22 conditionsNotCorrect — the vehicle is not in a state where this is allowed. Engine running, speed above zero, voltage too low.
  • 0x24 requestSequenceError — you skipped a step, most often in a flash sequence.
  • 0x31 requestOutOfRange — the identifier or address does not exist here.
  • 0x33 securityAccessDenied, 0x35 invalidKey, 0x36 exceedNumberOfAttempts, 0x37 requiredTimeDelayNotExpired — the four faces of service 0x27.
  • 0x7E and 0x7F — supported, but not in the session you are currently in. The fix is a session change, not a different service.
  • 0x78 requestCorrectlyReceived-ResponsePending. Not an error.

0x78 exists because of the two timing parameters: P2, the normal time the tester waits for an answer (50 ms by convention), and P2*, the extended one (often 5000 ms). An ECU that cannot answer inside P2 — because it is erasing flash or running a routine — sends 0x7F xx 78, and the tester's timer becomes P2*. The ECU may repeat it. A tester that treats 0x78 as a failure reports a broken ECU, and a tester that accepts it forever hangs instead of timing out; both are common bugs, and knowing which one you are looking at is the point of recognising the byte.

Bit 7 of a sub-function byte is the suppressPosRspMsgIndicationBit: set it and the ECU stays silent on success and answers only on failure. That is why 3E 80 — TesterPresent with suppression — generates no traffic at all, and why a tool log that appears to show an unanswered request often shows a perfectly correct one.

Sessions, security access, and the state you are actually in

🧒 Simple

A control unit will not let you do dangerous things just because you asked politely. It has modes: a normal one where you can read almost anything, an extended one for adjustments, and a programming one where the application is not even running and new software can be written.

Moving into a privileged mode needs a handshake: the unit gives you a random number, you send back the number transformed by a secret only the manufacturer's tool knows, and if it matches you are allowed in. Get it wrong a few times and it makes you wait — on purpose.

And the mode expires. If you stop talking for five seconds, the unit quietly goes back to normal, which is why tools send a small “still here” message on a timer.

🎓 Professional

Service 0x10, DiagnosticSessionControl, with sub-functions 01 default, 02 programming, 03 extended. Almost everything interesting is gated behind a non-default session, and the positive response carries the ECU's P2 and P2* values — so a tester that hardcodes 50 ms instead of reading them is guessing.

Service 0x27, SecurityAccess, is a seed-and-key challenge on odd/even sub-functions: an odd number requests a seed, the next even number sends the key. 27 01 then 27 02; a second level would be 27 03 / 27 04. A seed of all zeros means “already unlocked”, not “broken random number generator”. Wrong keys must be counted and answered with 0x36, then a mandatory delay enforced with 0x37 — the delay is the security control, since the algorithm itself is usually a shared secret in a supplier-provided DLL rather than real cryptography. On newer programmes 0x29, Authentication, replaces it with certificates, and knowing that 0x27 is the legacy mechanism is worth a sentence in an interview.

The S3 timer. Roughly five seconds of silence returns the ECU to the default session, which drops your security access with it. Service 0x3E, TesterPresent, sent every two seconds or so with the suppress bit set, keeps it alive. Nearly every “it worked and then stopped working in the middle” report is an S3 expiry, and it is invisible in a log precisely because the keep-alive that should be there produces no frames.

The everyday services, the ones you will implement rather than merely invoke:

  • 0x22 ReadDataByIdentifier and 0x2E WriteDataByIdentifier, addressing a 16-bit DID. F190 is the VIN, F18C the serial number, F1xx generally identification; the rest are project-defined and live in a database file, not in the standard. This pair is 80 % of the diagnostic traffic in a working vehicle.
  • 0x19 ReadDTCInformation, the fault memory. A DTC is three bytes plus a status byte whose bits distinguish failed right now from confirmed and stored from pending — and the difference between “testFailed” and “confirmedDTC” is the difference between a fault and a warranty claim. 0x14 clears it.
  • 0x2F InputOutputControlByIdentifier — force an actuator, then hand control back. The “hand it back” sub-function is not optional.
  • 0x31 RoutineControl — start, stop, request results. Self-tests, flash erase, checksum verification.
  • 0x11 ECUReset, 0x28 CommunicationControl (silence the normal application messages), 0x85 ControlDTCSetting (stop recording faults you are about to cause yourself).

Two neighbours worth separating: OBD-II is not UDS. The legally mandated emissions diagnostics (SAE J1979, modes 0x01–0x0A, PIDs, the generic scan tool) share the bus and the transport layer but are a different service set. And DoIP (ISO 13400) carries the same UDS on Ethernet, which is how a modern programme reflashes a gigabyte of software in minutes rather than hours — the services are unchanged, only the transport differs.

The flash sequence, which is where the job actually is

🧒 Simple

Putting new software into a control unit is not one command. It is a fixed recipe: quieten the unit down, prove you are allowed, switch to the mode where the application is not running, erase, then send the new software in blocks that are numbered so nothing can silently go missing, then check it, then restart.

Every step can refuse, and the refusal says which step you got wrong. That is the friendliest thing about the protocol.

🎓 Professional

The canonical order — details vary per manufacturer, the shape does not: 

10 03            extended session
85 02            stop storing DTCs (you are about to cause them)
28 03 01         disable normal application communication
27 01 / 27 02    seed, key
10 02            programming session  (application stops; bootloader runs)
34 ...           RequestDownload: address, size, encoding
36 01 ...        TransferData block 1
36 02 ...        block 2 ... counter wraps 0xFF -> 0x00
37               RequestTransferExit
31 01 FF01       routine: check programming dependencies / checksum
11 01            ECUReset -- hard reset, application runs again

Points that carry the understanding. The block sequence counter in 0x36 is one byte and wraps, so it identifies position modulo 256, not absolutely. 0x34's positive response tells you the maximum block length the ECU will accept, and a tester that ignores it and sends its own preferred size gets 0x31 — this is the single most common integration defect in a flash bring-up. The erase is 0x31 RoutineControl, not a service of its own, and it is the step most likely to exceed P2 and therefore to produce a string of 0x78 responses.

The reprogramming happens in a bootloader, not in your application, which is the fact that ties this chapter to chapter 34: after 10 02 the application is gone, the code answering you lives in a protected sector, and a power cut between the erase and a valid checksum must leave a device that still boots into that bootloader. A design where it does not is a device that comes back on a truck.

⚠ Trap

Everything works on the bench, and in the vehicle the same sequence fails halfway with conditionsNotCorrect — or worse, it does not fail at all and you cannot reproduce the field report.

Three causes, all of them about state rather than about protocol:

  • The S3 timer. Your bench script had no gaps; the real process has an operator confirming something. Five seconds of silence, session back to default, security lost, and the next service is refused with 0x7F ... 33. The fix is TesterPresent on a timer, and it belongs in the transport layer of your tool, not sprinkled through the test script.
  • Preconditions you do not control. 0x22 conditionsNotCorrect is the ECU saying the vehicle state forbids it — ignition, road speed, engine state, battery voltage. On a bench supply at 13.5 V none of these are ever wrong, which is exactly why the bench proves less than people think it does.
  • Someone else is talking. A second tester, a gateway, or a periodic routine in the factory line shares the ECU's single diagnostic connection. Whoever sends the last session change wins, and a tool that never re-checks the session it is in will be confidently wrong.

The discipline that prevents all three: log every request and response with a timestamp, including the negative ones, and never discard a 0x7F. The NRC byte is a designed diagnostic channel — almost uniquely among the protocols in this course, it tells you why — and a tool that reports “failed” while throwing that byte away has discarded the only evidence that mattered.

Try it. No vehicle needed, and you should not experiment on one. Build both sides on the virtual CAN bus from the previous chapter.

1. Write a toy ECU in about 150 lines of C, on the ISO-TP socket:

     - answer 22 F1 90 with 62 F1 90 plus 17 bytes of fake VIN
     - answer 22 anything-else with 7F 22 31 (requestOutOfRange)
     - accept 10 03, refuse 2E unless the session is 03 with 7F 2E 7F
     - implement 27 01 / 27 02 with key = seed XOR 0x5A5A5A5A,
       count failures, answer 36 after three and start a delay timer

   Then drive it with can-utils. You now own both halves, which is the
   only way to see WHY each rule exists.

2. Make it slow on purpose. Have one service sleep 400 ms while
   sending 7F xx 78 every 100 ms, and write a client that handles it
   correctly -- P2 until the first 78, then P2* -- and one that does
   not. The second is the bug you will be asked to diagnose one day.

3. Let the S3 timer expire. Sit silent for six seconds mid-sequence,
   watch your ECU drop to the default session, and watch the next
   request come back 7F xx 33. Then add 3E 80 on a two-second timer
   and watch the problem disappear. That is the single most useful
   thing in this chapter.

4. Sequence the flash without doing any flashing: implement 34, 36,
   37 and 31 01 FF01 over a RAM buffer, enforce the block sequence
   counter and the maximum block length from your own 34 response,
   and confirm your client reads that length instead of assuming one.

5. Read the free part of a real specification -- any supplier's
   diagnostic requirement document, or an open-source stack such as
   the UDS layer in an AUTOSAR-style project -- and find the DID
   table. That table, not the standard, is what a project actually
   argues about.
Golden rules
  • Positive response is the service identifier plus 0x40; a negative one is always 7F, the SID, and a reason byte.
  • 0x78 is not an error. It moves the tester from P2 to P2*, and both sides get this wrong in opposite directions.
  • Read P2 and P2* from the session response instead of hardcoding 50 ms.
  • Almost everything useful is gated behind a non-default session, and 0x7E or 0x7F as an NRC means “right service, wrong session”.
  • Security access is odd-seed, even-key, with a mandatory delay after repeated failures — the delay is the actual security, not the algorithm.
  • The S3 timer drops your session after about five seconds of silence. Send TesterPresent with the suppress bit, from the transport layer, on a timer.
  • The suppressPosRspMsgIndicationBit is bit 7 of the sub-function. Silence can mean success.
  • Obey the maximum block length from the 0x34 response, and respect the one-byte block sequence counter that wraps.
  • Reprogramming runs in a bootloader, and a power cut mid-flash must still leave a device that boots into it.
  • Never discard a negative response code. It is the one protocol in this course that tells you why, and a tool that logs only success has thrown the evidence away.
  • OBD-II is not UDS, and DoIP is UDS on Ethernet. Same bus, different service set; same services, different transport.
