Technical English

The advert asks for written and spoken English. In this job that means four concrete things: the reference manual, the errata sheet, the message you write to a vendor's support desk, and the call with a supplier who is not Italian either. All four are narrower than general English, and all four are trainable in weeks rather than years.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Buona conoscenza della lingua inglese, scritta e parlata” — a line that appeared in the adverts this course was built from. The second advert in the corpus raises it to “conoscenza fluente della lingua inglese”, which in a large engineering-services company usually means something real: the customer may be German or French, and the working language of the project is English.

Read the two phrasings as different requirements. Buona means you can read the manual and write a clear ticket. Fluente means you can hold a technical conversation without the other person adapting for you, and it will be tested — usually by switching languages in the middle of the interview (chapter 71).

Reading: the manual, the datasheet, the errata

🧒 Simple

Almost everything you need to know about a chip is written in English, once, by its maker, in a document of a thousand pages. Nobody reads it end to end. You learn to find the page you need and to read that page very carefully.

The good news is that these documents use a small, repetitive vocabulary and very plain grammar. They are written to be translated into machinery, not to be enjoyed — so the English in them is far easier than a newspaper's.

🎓 Professional

Four document types, each with its own conventions:

  • The datasheet — what the part is: pinout, electrical characteristics, absolute maximum ratings, timing. Numbers with min, typ and max columns, and the only column you may design against is the one that is guaranteed. Typical is not a promise.
  • The reference manual — what the peripherals do: register descriptions, bit fields, and sequences. This is where you live.
  • The application note — how to do one specific thing well, usually with the parts the reference manual leaves implicit.
  • The errata sheet — where the chip does not behave as the manual says. Its structure is always Description, Conditions, Impact, Workaround, and the habit of reading it before a long debugging session rather than after one is a genuine professional marker.

The grammar of specification English is worth learning deliberately, because it is legally precise rather than decorative: shall is an obligation, must usually a physical necessity, should a recommendation you may deviate from with a reason, may a permission, and shall not a prohibition. The same vocabulary appears in requirements documents (chapter 64a) and in standards such as MISRA, where mandatory, required and advisory are three different obligations (chapter 65).

Phrases that carry a warning and are easy to skim past: “the user must ensure that…” (nothing enforces this and it will fail silently), “undefined behaviour”, “this bit must be kept at its reset value”, “the sequence is mandatory”, “access to this register while … is ongoing is not allowed”, and the deceptively mild “it is recommended to…”, which in a reference manual very often means “everybody who ignored this lost a week”.

Writing: the ticket that gets answered

🧒 Simple

Sooner or later you will have to ask a stranger in another country for help: a vendor's support desk, a forum, a colleague in another office. Whether you get a useful answer depends far more on how you structure the message than on how good your English is.

The structure is always the same: what I am trying to do, what I did, what I expected, what happened, and everything about my setup. Short sentences. No apologies, no story.

🎓 Professional

A support request that gets a real answer instead of a form reply: 

Subject: STM32F407 - SPI2 RX overrun when DMA is enabled (RM0090 rev 19)

Setup:   STM32F407VG rev Z, CubeIDE 1.15, HAL 1.28.1, 168 MHz.
         SPI2 slave, 8-bit, CPOL=0 CPHA=0, DMA1 stream 3 channel 0.

Goal:    Receive 256-byte frames at 10 MHz using DMA.

What I did:
  1. Configure SPI2 and DMA as above (code below, 30 lines).
  2. Start HAL_SPI_Receive_DMA() once, restart it in the callback.

Expected: continuous reception, no data lost between frames.
Actual:   OVR flag set on ~1 frame in 50. Data after the overrun
          is shifted by one byte until SPI is reset.

Already tried: increased DMA priority; disabled all other
interrupts; verified on a scope that the master gaps frames by
40 us.

Question: is the restart in the callback too late at this clock,
and is the circular-buffer approach in AN4031 the intended fix?

Every element there is doing work: the exact part revision and document revision, because answers differ between them; a separation of expected from actual, which is the single most useful habit in technical writing anywhere; the list of things already tried, which stops the first three replies being suggestions you have already exhausted; and a specific question at the end, so the reader knows what “done” looks like.

Register and protocol vocabulary you should be able to use without hesitating: to assert and to de-assert a signal, rising and falling edge, to latch, to toggle, to poll, to sample, set and clear a bit, read-modify-write, to be pulled up or down, floating, glitch, jitter, duty cycle, throughput against latency, upstream and downstream, a workaround, root cause, to reproduce, intermittent, to narrow down, to roll back, to ship.

And the false friends that mark an Italian speaker instantly, all of them worth un-learning consciously: eventually means alla fine, not eventualmente (say possibly or if needed); actually means in realtà, not attualmente (say currently); to control means comandare, not controllare (say to check); to pretend is fingere, not pretendere (say to expect or to require); sensible is ragionevole, not sensibile (say sensitive); to realize is rendersi conto, not realizzare (say to implement); to assist is aiutare, not assistere a (say to attend); to attend is partecipare, not attendere (say to wait); and an argument in code is a parameter, while in a meeting it is a fight — the topic you meant is a subject or a point.

Speaking: the call, the stand-up, the interview

🧒 Simple

Speaking is the part people are afraid of, and the fear is usually about sounding wrong rather than about being understood. In a technical call almost nobody is a native speaker, everybody accepts simple grammar, and the only real failures are silence and pretending to have understood.

Two sentences remove most of the risk: “Sorry, could you repeat that?” and “Let me repeat it back to make sure I understood.” Both make you look careful, not weak.

🎓 Professional

Situations, in the order you will meet them:

  • The daily stand-up, if the project has one. Thirty seconds, three sentences: what you finished, what you are on, what is blocking you. Rehearse it once and it stops being a language problem forever.
  • The supplier or vendor call. Send an agenda beforehand, in writing, with the numbers in it — then the hard information has already arrived and the call only has to confirm it. Take notes and send a two-line summary afterwards: “To confirm what we agreed…”. That habit is valued far beyond its cost, and it removes the pressure to catch every word live.
  • Spelling and numbers over a bad line. Learn to say hex aloud — zero-x-seven-E-eight, or seven-echo-eight using the NATO alphabet — and to say one-two-five kilobits per second rather than a hundred twenty-five. Also: a decimal point, not a comma; one point five volts. Getting a number wrong on a call costs a day of somebody's work.
  • The interview in English, which is usually ten minutes in the middle of an Italian conversation. It is a test of composure, not of vocabulary: describe your project, describe one bug and how you found it, ask them a question back.

Pronunciation worth fixing once, because these words come up constantly and the Italian defaults are hard to parse: cache (like cash, not cachet), queue (like Q), routine and router, suite (like sweet), bus, bias, ground, bought against board, issue, schedule, architecture, asynchronous, deterministic, hierarchy, parameter against perimeter. And the two habits that carry more than any of them: slow down — speed is not fluency — and stress the right word, because English carries meaning in stress where Italian carries it in word order.

⚠ Trap

“I read English perfectly, so my English is fine” — and then the interview switches language for ten minutes and the technical answer collapses.

Reading is passive and enormously easier, and a course like this one is read in English all day without ever exercising production. The gap between the two is invisible from the inside: you feel fluent while reading and discover otherwise the first time you have to produce a sentence under time pressure about something you understand perfectly in Italian.

The fix is narrow and takes weeks, not years, because the domain is small. Produce something in English every day, however short: commit messages, a paragraph of notes at the end of the day, the comment above a function. Speak out loud, alone, for two minutes — explain what volatile does, explain why your last bug happened — and record it once a week; the improvement is audible within a month, and the exercise is nearly identical to the interview. Prepare the three set pieces: your background, your project, one bug you solved end to end. Those three cover most of what any English portion of an interview will ask.

The other half of the trap is on the CV. Claiming C1 because you watch series in English is checkable in ninety seconds and costs you the room; claiming B2 written, B1 spoken, improving is honest, defensible, and in a junior role costs almost nothing — chapter 70 makes the same argument about claiming embedded experience. If they need better English than you have, you want to know that on the day rather than in month three.

Try it. None of this needs a course, a teacher or a certificate, and all of it is directly the job.

1. Read one section of a real reference manual, aloud. Take the SPI
   or the UART chapter of RM0090 (STM32F4) -- ten pages -- and read a
   register description out loud. It forces pronunciation of exactly
   the vocabulary you will use, and you learn the register at the
   same time.

2. Read one errata sheet, end to end. They are short. Pick your board's
   MCU, and find one item whose Workaround you could actually
   implement. Being able to say "I read the errata before I debug" is
   a sentence with real weight in an interview.

3. Write the support ticket from this chapter for a bug you already
   solved -- setup, goal, steps, expected, actual, already tried, one
   question. Then cut it by a third. Learning to write short is most
   of learning to write in a second language.

4. Post it somewhere real, once. The ST community forum, a GitHub
   issue on a driver you use, a mailing list. The first message is the
   only hard one, and a public, polite, well-structured technical
   question is also a portfolio item.

5. Two minutes out loud, weekly, recorded. Week 1: "what my project
   does". Week 2: "the worst bug I had and how I found it". Week 3:
   "what volatile is for". Listen back once. Do not transcribe, do not
   correct grammar -- only fix the words you could not find, and learn
   those.

6. Switch your own working notes and commit messages to English
   permanently. It costs nothing, it produces daily practice without
   any extra time, and by the interview it is no longer a performance.
Golden rules
  • Technical English is a small language. The manual's vocabulary is narrow and repetitive — it is trainable in weeks.
  • Shall, should and may are three different obligations, in a datasheet and in a requirement alike.
  • Design against the guaranteed column, never the typical one.
  • Read the errata before the debugging session, not after it.
  • “The user must ensure” means nothing enforces it and it will fail silently.
  • Separate expected from actual in every report you write. It is the highest-value habit in technical writing.
  • State versions, revisions and what you already tried, and end with one specific question.
  • Un-learn the false friends: eventually, actually, control, pretend, sensible, realize, assist, attend.
  • Never pretend to have understood on a call. Ask for a repeat, then repeat it back.
  • Say hex and numbers digit by digit, and confirm them in writing afterwards.
  • Reading is passive; production is the gap. Write something and speak two minutes every day, and prepare the three set pieces.
  • Claim the level you can defend — it is testable in ninety seconds.

Scritto me la cavo bene: quando apro un ticket a un supporto tecnico uso sempre la stessa struttura — setup con le revisioni esatte del chip e del documento, cosa volevo fare, cosa mi aspettavo, cosa è successo davvero, cosa ho già provato, e una domanda precisa alla fine. Parlato sono più lento, e non faccio finta: se non capisco chiedo di ripetere e poi riassumo per conferma, e dopo una call mando due righe di riepilogo scritto. Su numeri e esadecimali li scandisco cifra per cifra, perché un numero sbagliato al telefono costa una giornata a qualcuno. Sul CV ho scritto il livello che posso difendere, non uno in più. E se vuole andiamo avanti in inglese adesso, così se lo verifica direttamente invece di fidarsi di una sigla.”
