Timing, jitter, and how you measure it

The advert says ottimizzare. You cannot optimise what you have not measured, and in firmware the cheapest instrument — one spare pin and a scope — beats every estimate and most profilers.

From the advert

This chapter answers the second verb in “Sviluppare e ottimizzare firmware in C/C++”. That word is why the role exists: somebody will hand you code that works and ask you to make it meet a deadline it currently misses.

A pin and a scope

🧒 Simple

Set a spare pin high when a piece of code starts and low when it ends. Now look at that pin with an instrument. The width of the pulse is exactly how long the code took, in nanoseconds, with no guessing and almost no effect on the thing you are measuring.

It sounds too simple to be the professional answer. It is the professional answer.

🎓 Professional

A GPIO write is one store instruction — a handful of nanoseconds — so the instrument's own cost is negligible against anything you would want to measure. Use the atomic set/reset register rather than a read-modify-write on the output register, and drop to registers rather than the HAL here specifically, because a HAL call has its own parameter checking in the measurement path (chapter 30): 

#define PROBE_HI()  (GPIOB->BSRR = (1u << 7))
#define PROBE_LO()  (GPIOB->BSRR = (1u << (7 + 16)))

void TIM2_IRQHandler(void) {
    PROBE_HI();
    ...
    PROBE_LO();
}

What the trace tells you that a number cannot:

  • Pulse width — execution time. Set the scope to infinite persistence or use its measurement statistics, run for minutes, and read the maximum, not the mean (chapter 37).
  • Gap between pulses — the period, and its variation is the jitter.
  • Two probes — latency between an event and the response to it. Toggle one pin in the ISR and another at the top of the woken task, and you have measured the context-switch path, which is the number chapter 42 is really about.

Put a probe pin on the schematic before the board is made. Asking the hardware engineer for two spare test points early costs nothing and is one of the more useful things you can ask for (chapter 63).

Counting cycles when you have no scope

🧒 Simple

The processor can also count its own clock ticks. Read the counter before and after, subtract, and you have the duration in cycles — no instrument needed, though now the measurement itself costs something.

🎓 Professional

On Cortex-M3 and above the DWT cycle counter is a free-running 32-bit counter at the core clock. It needs enabling once, and then it is the finest-grained timer you have: 

CoreDebug->DEMCR |= CoreDebug_DEMCR_TRCENA_Msk;
DWT->CYCCNT = 0;
DWT->CTRL  |= DWT_CTRL_CYCCNTENA_Msk;

uint32_t t0 = DWT->CYCCNT;
thing_to_measure();
uint32_t dt = DWT->CYCCNT - t0;     /* unsigned: correct across wrap */

Note the subtraction is unsigned, so it stays correct when the counter wraps — the reason timer arithmetic uses unsigned types (chapter 03). At 168 MHz it wraps about every 25 seconds, so it measures short things.

Do not use it to store a histogram in a fast path without thinking: the read is cheap but the storage is not. The usual pattern is to keep only a running maximum, which is one compare and one conditional store, and which is also the only statistic that matters.

SWO and ITM trace is the level above: the core streams timestamped events out of a dedicated pin with very low overhead, and a debugger or an analyser reconstructs a timeline (chapter 61). An RTOS-aware trace tool built on it shows you every context switch, which task was running when, and where a deadline was missed — and it is the only tool that makes a scheduling bug visible directly rather than by inference.

⚠ Trap

Measuring changes what you measure, and printf changes it enormously.

A blocking printf over UART at 115200 baud takes about 87 µs per character. A forty-character line is 3.5 ms. Putting that inside the interrupt whose latency you were investigating does not slow the measurement down slightly — it makes the interrupt the dominant term in its own measurement, and it will usually make the original symptom disappear, which is the worst possible outcome because it looks like a fix.

The related and subtler one: halting the core at a breakpoint does not halt the world. Peripherals keep running, the UART keeps receiving into an overrun, DMA keeps transferring, and the plant keeps moving (chapter 60). Timing problems are the class of bug least amenable to a debugger and most amenable to a pin.

And beware of measuring the wrong thing correctly: a mean of 40 µs over a million samples is a true statement that tells you nothing if one sample in a million took 9 ms. Record the maximum, or a histogram, or both. The tail is the product.

Try it. Steps 1 and 2 need a board; step 3 is the honest fallback if you have no instrument yet.

1. Instrument your ISR with PROBE_HI/PROBE_LO and put a logic analyser
   on it. Run for two minutes with the system under realistic load.
   Read the MAXIMUM pulse width, not the average. That number is the
   contribution your handler makes to every lower-priority interrupt's
   worst case, and almost nobody you interview against will have
   measured theirs.

2. Now find the thing that dominates. Add a second probe around your
   longest critical section -- every __disable_irq() / taskENTER_CRITICAL
   region -- and compare the widths. In most systems the critical
   section, not the handler, is the largest single term.

3. No instrument? Use the DWT counter and keep a running maximum in a
   global. Print it once a second from a LOW-priority task, never from
   the path you are measuring:

     static volatile uint32_t worst;
     uint32_t t0 = DWT->CYCCNT;
     ...
     uint32_t dt = DWT->CYCCNT - t0;
     if (dt > worst) worst = dt;

4. Then do the optimisation, and measure again. If you cannot show the
   before and after numbers, you have not optimised anything -- you have
   changed something.
Golden rules
  • A spare pin and an instrument beat every estimate, and the probe's own cost is one store.
  • Use the atomic set/reset register for the probe, and registers rather than the HAL in a measurement path.
  • Read the maximum, over a long run under load. The mean is nearly meaningless.
  • Two probes measure latency — one in the ISR, one in the woken task.
  • The DWT cycle counter is the finest timer you have, and unsigned subtraction stays correct across its wrap.
  • Keep a running maximum, not a histogram, in a fast path.
  • printf in the path you are measuring becomes the measurement, and often hides the symptom.
  • Halting the core does not halt the peripherals, the DMA or the plant.
  • Ask for probe test points before the board is made.
  • No before-and-after numbers means you changed something, not optimised it.

“Come misurerebbe se una routine rispetta i tempi?”

“Nel modo più stupido e più affidabile: alzo un pin all’inizio e lo abbasso alla fine, e guardo la larghezza dell’impulso con un analizzatore logico o un oscilloscopio. Una scrittura su BSRR è una singola istruzione, quindi la sonda non perturba la misura. Poi guardo il massimo su una corsa lunga e sotto carico realistico, non la media: la media di quaranta microsecondi non mi dice niente se uno su un milione ne fa nove millisecondi, e in real-time quello che conta è la coda. Con due sonde misuro anche la latenza: una la alzo nell’ISR, l’altra all’inizio del task che ho svegliato, e ho il costo del cambio di contesto. Se non ho lo strumento uso il contatore di cicli DWT e mi tengo solo il massimo in una variabile, che poi stampo da un task a bassa priorità — mai dal percorso che sto misurando, perché una printf bloccante a 115200 fa ottantasette microsecondi a carattere e diventa lei il termine dominante, oltre a far sparire il sintomo, che è il risultato peggiore perché sembra una soluzione. Una cosa che chiedo sempre all’hardwarista: due test point liberi sulla scheda, prima che vada in produzione.”
