TCP/IP in 64 KB: lwIP

A complete TCP/IP stack on a part with no MMU, no processes and a few tens of kilobytes to spare. What lwIP is, the three APIs it offers and why the choice matters, the pbuf model that makes zero-copy possible, and what actually runs out first.

From the advert

Not named in the advert, which says Ethernet and means a stack. lwIP is what is running inside the overwhelming majority of these products: it is what CubeMX generates, what FreeRTOS integrates with, and what nearly every STM32 Ethernet example is built on. Chapter 51 got frames into memory; this is everything above them.

What lwIP is, and the three APIs

🧒 Simple

A TCP/IP stack is the software that turns raw frames into something useful: finding out who has which address, splitting data into packets, putting them back in order, asking for the missing ones again, and making it all look like a connection.

On a PC that work is inside the operating system. On a microcontroller there is no operating system to put it in, so it is a library you compile into your program — and it has to be small enough to fit beside everything else.

🎓 Professional

lwIP — lightweight IP — gives you IPv4 and IPv6, ARP, ICMP, UDP, TCP, DHCP, DNS, and optional applications such as an HTTP server, SNTP and MQTT, in something like 40 KB of flash and tens of kilobytes of RAM depending on configuration. It offers three APIs, and picking the wrong one is a decision people regret for the life of the project:

  • The raw / callback API. No threads, no copies, no blocking: you register callbacks and lwIP calls them from its own context. The smallest and fastest option, and the only one usable bare-metal in a superloop. The cost is that your application logic turns inside out into a state machine (chapter 7), and that everything must run in the lwIP context — blocking inside a callback stalls the whole stack.
  • The netconn API. Sequential and blocking, needs an RTOS, and hides most of the callback machinery while keeping lwIP's buffer model.
  • The socket API. BSD sockets, familiar to anybody who has written network code on a PC, needs an RTOS, and copies data across the boundary. Easiest to write and the most expensive in RAM and cycles.

The rule that matters more than any of them: lwIP is not thread-safe except through the netconn and socket APIs. Calling raw-API functions from another task is the single most common way to produce a stack that works for hours and then corrupts itself. When you must, lwIP provides tcpip_callback to run your function in the stack's own context, and the LWIP_TCPIP_CORE_LOCKING option makes the locking explicit. A project with a mysterious, rare Ethernet hang has usually violated this rule somewhere.

pbufs, and why zero-copy is the whole point

🧒 Simple

Copying data costs time and memory, and a small chip has little of either. So instead of copying a packet from place to place as it moves up the layers, the stack passes around a small description of where the data already is.

A single message can also be spread across several separate pieces of memory, chained together — which is efficient, and which means your code cannot assume the data is one contiguous block.

🎓 Professional

A pbuf is lwIP's packet buffer: a small struct with a pointer, a length, a total chain length, a reference count and a next pointer. Three kinds matter in practice:

  • PBUF_POOL — from a fixed pool, the usual choice for received packets. Fixed-size, so a large frame becomes a chain of them.
  • PBUF_RAM — a single allocation, contiguous, used for outgoing data you build yourself.
  • PBUF_REF and PBUF_ROM — a pointer to data the stack does not own, which is how you send a constant from flash without copying it at all.

Three consequences to design around, all of which are where real bugs come from:

  • Never assume a pbuf is contiguous. Walk the chain, or use pbuf_copy_partial into your own buffer when you genuinely need one block. A protocol parser that reads a header by casting a struct onto p->payload works for small packets and fails the day a message spans two pbufs (chapter 9 has the alignment half of the same argument).
  • Reference counting is ownership. pbuf_free when you are done, pbuf_ref if you keep it. A leaked pbuf is a pool that empties over hours, giving a device that works all day and stops overnight — and the leak is usually on an error path nobody tested.
  • Return them promptly. Holding pbufs while the application thinks starves the receive ring from chapter 51.

On the TCP side, the two functions worth understanding properly are tcp_write — which queues data, possibly without copying, so the buffer you passed must stay valid until the data is acknowledged unless you asked for a copy — and tcp_recved, which tells the stack you have consumed data and reopens the receive window. Forgetting tcp_recved gives a connection that transfers a few kilobytes and then stalls forever, which is a genuinely confusing symptom the first time.

What runs out first

🧒 Simple

A stack on a small chip does not fail by being slow. It fails by running out of memory, and when it does the symptoms look like a network problem rather than a memory problem.

🎓 Professional

Everything in lwIP is statically sized in lwipopts.h, and the numbers people leave at their defaults are the ones that bite:

  • PBUF_POOL_SIZE and MEM_SIZE — the receive pool and the general heap. Too small and packets are dropped under burst traffic, which TCP hides by retransmitting and UDP does not hide at all.
  • MEMP_NUM_TCP_PCB — how many simultaneous connections exist. A device that accepts a sixth connection when five were configured simply refuses it, and the customer reports that the machine “stops answering after a while”. Connections in TIME_WAIT count too, which is why a client that reconnects rapidly exhausts a server sized by counting only active links.
  • TCP_SND_BUF and TCP_WND — throughput is bounded by window divided by round-trip time, so a 2 KB window on a 1 ms network caps you around 2 MB/s regardless of the link speed. This is the answer to “why is our 100 Mbit device slow” nine times out of ten.

Two habits make all of it visible instead of mysterious. Turn on LWIP_STATS and print the counters — dropped packets, memory errors, pool exhaustion, per protocol. They are free, they are already there, and they turn a vague complaint into a number. And decide what the application does when the network is gone: an industrial device must keep controlling its machine while its Ethernet is unplugged, so no control path may ever block on a socket (chapter 58 makes the same point about a field bus). If pulling the cable disturbs the machine, the architecture is wrong regardless of how good the stack is.

⚠ Trap

Everything works, and after nine days the device stops answering — because a pbuf leaks on a path that only executes when something goes wrong.

The shape is always the same. The happy path frees its pbufs correctly, because it is the path you ran ten thousand times. The error path — a malformed packet, a connection reset mid-message, a length field that does not match, an allocation that failed — returns early and forgets. Each occurrence costs one buffer from a fixed pool. On a quiet network that is once a day, so the device runs for a week and then refuses connections, and the log says nothing because nothing crashed.

Three defences worth building in from the start. Print the lwIP stats periodically in development — a pool whose free count trends downwards over hours is a leak, visible long before the failure. Give every callback exactly one exit point that frees, or use a small wrapper that frees on return, so the error path cannot forget what the happy path remembers. And test the error paths deliberately: send truncated packets, reset connections mid-transfer, open more connections than configured, and hold one open without reading. Those four tests take an afternoon with a Python script and find this entire class of defect (chapter 64).

The same reasoning applies to the connection table: a device that never closes its own sockets accumulates state until it has none left. An embedded server should have a defined, bounded, aggressive policy for idle connections — a timeout that closes them — because it is not a web server with gigabytes of RAM and it will be talking to a client that crashes, unplugs and reboots without saying goodbye.

Try it. Most of this runs on a PC: lwIP compiles for Linux and Windows with a tap or pcap interface, which is much faster to iterate on than a board.

1. Build lwIP for the host first, with its unix port. You get the
   real stack, on a real network interface, with gdb and sanitisers
   available -- and no flash cycle. This is the single best way to
   learn it.

2. Write a UDP echo server with the RAW API, then the same thing with
   sockets, and compare the two source files. The difference IS the
   trade this chapter is about: one is a state machine, the other
   reads top to bottom and costs RAM.

3. Handle a chained pbuf deliberately. Send a 2000-byte UDP datagram
   so it fragments and arrives as a chain, then write a parser that
   walks p->next correctly. Then write the naive version that casts a
   struct onto p->payload and watch it read nonsense.

4. Leak a pbuf on purpose. Add an error path that returns without
   pbuf_free, enable LWIP_STATS, and print the pool free count every
   second while sending malformed packets. Watch it trend down. That
   graph is what you will look for in a real product.

5. Forget tcp_recved in a TCP receive callback and watch a transfer
   stall after a few kilobytes. Then add it back. Five minutes, and it
   removes a confusing symptom from your future.

6. On the board: pull the Ethernet cable while the device is
   controlling something, and confirm the control loop keeps its
   timing and the machine stays safe. If it does not, that is the
   finding -- and it matters more than any throughput number.
Golden rules
  • Three APIs: raw callbacks, netconn, sockets. Raw is the only bare-metal option; sockets cost RAM and copies.
  • lwIP is not thread-safe outside netconn and sockets. Use tcpip_callback to reach the stack from another task.
  • Never block inside a raw-API callback. It stalls the whole stack.
  • A pbuf may be a chain. Never cast a header struct onto p->payload and assume contiguity.
  • Reference counting is ownership: free what you finish, ref what you keep, and return buffers promptly.
  • Call tcp_recved, or the window closes and the connection stalls.
  • Data passed to tcp_write without a copy must stay valid until it is acknowledged.
  • Everything is statically sized in lwipopts.h, and the defaults are for examples, not products.
  • Throughput is window over round-trip time, not link speed.
  • Count TIME_WAIT in your connection budget, and time out idle connections aggressively.
  • Turn on LWIP_STATS and watch the pool free count for a downward trend.
  • No control path may block on the network. Unplugging the cable must not disturb the machine.

“Ha usato lwIP? Che problemi ha incontrato?”

“La prima decisione è quale API: raw a callback, che è l’unica utilizzabile bare-metal e costa meno RAM ma ti obbliga a rovesciare la logica in una macchina a stati; netconn; o socket BSD, comodissime ma con RTOS e con copie. E la regola che secondo me causa più danni è che lwIP non è thread-safe fuori da netconn e socket: se chiami funzioni della raw API da un altro task, il sistema funziona per ore e poi si corrompe. Quando devo, uso tcpip_callback per eseguire nel contesto dello stack. Poi i pbuf: non sono contigui, sono catene, quindi un parser che fa il cast di una struct su p->payload va bene sui pacchetti piccoli e legge spazzatura il giorno che il messaggio sta su due buffer. E il conteggio dei riferimenti è proprietà: il bug classico è il pbuf che perde su un percorso di errore — pacchetto malformato, connessione resettata a metà — quello che non hai mai provato. Una volta al giorno, e dopo nove giorni il dispositivo non risponde più, senza nessun crash e senza niente nei log. Per questo accendo LWIP_STATS e guardo il contatore dei buffer liberi: se cala nel tempo, sto perdendo. E in un prodotto industriale nessun percorso di controllo deve bloccarsi sulla rete: stacco il cavo e la macchina deve continuare a funzionare.”
