SPI and I2C

The two buses inside the box. Four wires and full duplex, or two wires and addressing — and the I2C lockup that needs a recovery sequence you have to write yourself, which is a real interview question because it only happens to people who have shipped something.

From the advert

Not named in this advert, which says “protocolli di comunicazione seriale” and means the ones that leave the box. These are the ones that do not: every sensor on the proprietary board in this job speaks one of the two, so you will use them before you use Modbus.

SPI: fast, simple, and a wire per device

🧒 Simple

Four wires: a clock, a wire out, a wire in, and one select wire per device. The controller pulls a device's select line low to say “I am talking to you”, then ticks the clock. On every tick a bit goes out and a bit comes back, at the same time.

It is fast and there is almost nothing to go wrong — except that every extra device needs another select wire, and pins run out.

🎓 Professional

Synchronous, full duplex, single controller. SCLK, MOSI, MISO, and one CS per peripheral. Tens of megahertz is routine, which is why it carries displays, flash memory, ADCs and radios.

There is no addressing, no acknowledgement and no error detection at all — if a device is absent you read whatever the floating MISO line happens to be, usually all ones or all zeros, and nothing tells you. Sanity-check by reading a known register: almost every SPI sensor has a “who am I” register with a fixed value, and checking it at startup turns a silent wiring fault into a clear error message.

The mode is the thing that catches people. Two bits, CPOL and CPHA, giving modes 0 to 3: CPOL is the idle level of the clock, CPHA selects whether data is sampled on the first or second edge. Get it wrong and you read shifted or garbage data with no error indication. Mode 0 and mode 3 are by far the most common; the datasheet states which, and the timing diagram in the datasheet is the authority when the prose is ambiguous.

Because it is full duplex, every transfer is an exchange: to read N bytes you must clock out N bytes of anything. That is why SPI driver APIs take both a transmit and a receive buffer, and why NULL for one of them means “send zeros” or “discard” rather than “skip”.

I2C: two wires, and everything that follows from that

🧒 Simple

Two wires shared by everything: one clock, one data. Because they are shared, no device is allowed to drive them high — each can only pull them down, and a resistor pulls them back up. That way two devices talking at once cannot fight and damage each other.

Each device has an address, so the controller says the address first and only that device replies. Fewer wires, more rules.

🎓 Professional

SDA and SCL, both open-drain with external pull-up resistors — typically 4.7 kΩ at 100 kHz standard mode, lower at 400 kHz fast mode. Open-drain is what makes multi-controller arbitration and clock stretching possible, and it is also what limits the speed: the rise time is set by the pull-up resistance and the bus capacitance, and too many devices on too long a track means the edges never get high enough.

A transaction is: START, 7-bit address plus a read/write bit, then bytes each followed by an ACK from the receiver, then STOP. The ACK is the useful part — unlike SPI, a missing device produces a NACK on the address byte, which is a real error you can report. A repeated START (a START without an intervening STOP) is how you do the standard write-register-address-then-read-data sequence without releasing the bus, and most sensor datasheets require it.

Clock stretching: a target that needs time can hold SCL low, and the controller must wait. Correct behaviour, occasionally not implemented properly on either side, and a common cause of interoperability problems with slow sensors and with software I2C implementations.

Address conflicts are a practical planning issue: many sensors offer only one or two selectable addresses, so two of the same part on one bus may be impossible. The fixes are an address-select pin, a second I2C peripheral, or an I2C multiplexer — and it is much better to discover this while reading the schematic than after the board is made (chapter 63).

⚠ Trap

An I2C bus can lock up permanently, and no amount of resetting your microcontroller will clear it. This is the question that separates people who have shipped an I2C product from people who have used one.

The mechanism: the controller is reset — by a watchdog, a debugger, or a power glitch — in the middle of a read, at a moment when the target is driving SDA low to output a zero bit. The target is now waiting for more clock pulses to finish its byte. The controller restarts, sees SDA stuck low, and concludes the bus is busy. It cannot issue a START, because a START requires SDA to fall while SCL is high, and SDA is already low. Deadlock, and it survives your reset because the stuck device is the other one.

The recovery sequence is standard and every robust driver should have it, run once at startup: 

/* 1. Take SCL and SDA off the I2C peripheral, make them GPIO open-drain.
   2. Clock SCL up to 9 times, checking SDA after each pulse.
      Nine pulses lets the target finish any byte it was mid-way through
      and then release SDA on the ACK slot.
   3. When SDA goes high, generate a manual STOP:
      SDA low, SCL high, then SDA high while SCL stays high.
   4. Hand the pins back to the I2C peripheral and re-init it. */

Related and worth knowing: on several STM32 families the I2C peripheral itself can latch into a busy state, and the erratum's fix is essentially the same — reset the peripheral through its own software-reset bit as well as bit-banging the bus clear. Read the errata for your part. That habit generalises well beyond I2C.

Try it. A cheap logic analyser with protocol decoding — under twenty euro — is the single highest-value purchase for this chapter, because both buses become readable.

1. Wire any I2C sensor and decode the bus. Look at a full transaction:
   START, address+W, ACK, register, ACK, repeated START, address+R, ACK,
   data, NACK, STOP. Seeing the ACK bits is what makes I2C click.

2. Deliberately address a device that is not there. Watch the NACK on
   the address byte, and confirm your driver reports it rather than
   returning stale data. Then do the SPI equivalent -- remove the device
   and read a register. You get plausible-looking bytes and no error at
   all. That contrast is the main reason to check a "who am I" register
   on every SPI part at startup.

3. Reproduce the I2C lockup on purpose, which is the exercise that
   matters. Start a multi-byte read, and halt the core at a breakpoint
   midway through (or hit the reset button). On restart, the bus is
   stuck and your driver hangs or times out forever. Now add the nine-
   clock recovery at init and watch it come back every time.

4. On SPI, set the mode wrong on purpose -- mode 0 instead of mode 3 --
   and look at the decoded bytes. No error is reported anywhere; the
   data is simply wrong. Then find the timing diagram in the sensor's
   datasheet and derive the correct mode from it rather than guessing.
Golden rules
  • SPI is fast, full duplex and has no error detection at all. A missing device reads as plausible bytes.
  • Check a “who am I” register on every SPI device at startup — it is the only error reporting you get.
  • CPOL and CPHA give four SPI modes, and the wrong one produces wrong data with no indication. The datasheet timing diagram is the authority.
  • Every SPI transfer is an exchange: to read N bytes you clock out N bytes.
  • I2C is open-drain with pull-ups, which is what enables arbitration and clock stretching and what limits the speed.
  • I2C acknowledges, so a missing device NACKs the address — a real, reportable error.
  • A repeated START is how you write a register address and then read without releasing the bus.
  • Plan addresses early. Two identical sensors may be impossible on one bus.
  • An I2C bus can lock with SDA held low by a target, and resetting your MCU does not clear it.
  • Ship the nine-clock recovery sequence in every I2C driver, run at startup.

“Che differenza c’è fra SPI e I2C, e quando sceglie l’uno o l’altro?”

“SPI è veloce e semplice — clock, due linee dati e un chip select per dispositivo, full duplex, decine di megahertz — ma non ha né indirizzamento né acknowledge: se il sensore non c’è leggo la linea che fluttua e nessuno mi dice niente. Per questo su ogni device SPI controllo il registro ‘who am I’ in fase di init, che è l’unica diagnostica che ho. E il modo, CPOL e CPHA, lo ricavo dal diagramma dei tempi del datasheet, perché sbagliandolo i dati sono sbagliati senza nessun errore. I2C usa due soli fili con indirizzo e ACK, quindi un dispositivo mancante dà un NACK sull’indirizzo, che è un errore vero che posso segnalare, e mi fa risparmiare pin. Costa velocità e mi obbliga a pensare ai conflitti di indirizzo. La cosa che metterei sempre in un driver I2C è la sequenza di recovery: se il micro si resetta a metà di una lettura, il target resta a tenere SDA basso in attesa di clock, io vedo il bus occupato e non riesco più a generare uno START — e resettare il mio micro non serve a niente, perché quello bloccato è l’altro. Si esce mettendo i pin in GPIO, dando fino a nove impulsi di clock finché SDA non si libera, e generando uno STOP a mano.”
