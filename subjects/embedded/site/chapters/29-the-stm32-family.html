The STM32 family, and choosing a part

Two thousand part numbers, one naming scheme, and a selection process in which clock speed is almost never the deciding factor. What the letters mean, what actually constrains the choice, and why the question is asked at all in an interview.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Esperienza (anche accademica o di stage) su ambiente di sviluppo STM32” — a line that appeared in the adverts this course was built from, and the one hard technical gate in the anchor posting. Chapter 30 is the toolchain half of that line; this is the silicon half.

Nobody expects a junior to have selected a part for production. They do expect you to read a part number, know which family you worked on and why, and not to believe that a faster core is a better choice.

Reading the part number

🧒 Simple

A part number like STM32F407VGT6 looks like noise and is actually a sentence. Each group of characters answers one question: which family, how fast and what peripherals, how many pins, how much flash, what package, what temperature range.

Learning to read it takes five minutes and saves you from ordering the wrong chip — or from reading the wrong manual for two hours.

🎓 Professional
STM32  F4  07  V   G   T   6
  |     |   |  |   |   |   |
  |     |   |  |   |   |   +-- temperature range (6 = -40..85 C, 7 = ..105)
  |     |   |  |   |   +------ package (T = LQFP, J = UFBGA, Y = WLCSP...)
  |     |   |  |   +---------- flash size (C=256K, E=512K, G=1M, I=2M)
  |     |   |  +-------------- pin count (T=36, C=48, R=64, V=100, Z=144)
  |     |   +----------------- sub-family / peripheral set
  |     +--------------------- family: F0 F1 F2 F3 F4 F7 H7 / L0 L4 L5 U5 / G0 G4 / WB WL
  +--------------------------- 32-bit ARM

The families divide by intent rather than by age:

  • F0, G0, C0 — cost-driven, Cortex-M0+. Where a job used to need an 8-bit part.
  • F1 — the old workhorse, M3, enormous installed base. Still in production equipment everywhere, which matters because maintenance work exists.
  • F4 — M4 with FPU, the default general-purpose choice for a decade and the most documented part on the internet. F7 / H7 — M7 with caches, for signal processing and heavy networking.
  • G4 — M4 aimed at motor control and digital power: fast ADCs, advanced timers, comparators. A cooling-system controller with pumps and fans is squarely in this space.
  • L0, L4, L5, U5 — low power, for battery products (chapter 28).
  • WB, WL — integrated radio, Bluetooth and LoRa.
  • MP1 — not a microcontroller in the sense of this course: a Cortex-A running Linux with an M4 beside it.

The practical fact behind the scheme: pin count and package are largely compatible within a family, so an F407V and an F405V share a footprint, and moving up in flash within the same line is a purchasing decision rather than a redesign. That compatibility is much of why ST is chosen in the first place.

What actually decides the choice

🧒 Simple

Beginners choose by speed and memory, because those are the numbers on the front page. Real projects almost never run out of speed first. They run out of the right pins: three serial ports and only two that can reach the pins the board layout needs, or a timer that cannot drive that particular pin.

🎓 Professional

The constraints in roughly the order they bind, which is also a good answer when an interviewer asks how you would choose:

  1. Peripherals, by count and by kind. Two CAN controllers or one? A USB device and Ethernet? Enough timers with complementary outputs and dead-time insertion for the motor stages (chapter 32)? This is where most candidate parts are eliminated.
  2. Pin mapping, not just pin count. Each function appears only on certain pins through the alternate-function mux (chapter 31), and two functions you need can collide on the same pin. CubeMX's pinout view is genuinely the right tool here, and this is the step beginners skip.
  3. Memory, with headroom. A project that fits in 100 % of flash on day one does not fit on day two hundred. A conventional rule is to plan for about half, because field update, a bootloader and diagnostics all arrive later (chapter 34).
  4. Power and thermal, if the product is battery or sealed.
  5. Availability and lifecycle. After 2021 nobody in this industry chooses a part without checking longevity commitments and real lead times. A technically perfect part with a fifty-week lead time is the wrong part, and choosing a family where several pin-compatible options exist is a genuine risk control.
  6. Ecosystem and continuity. The team's existing drivers, the debugger they own, and whether the vendor's HAL supports the peripheral you need in the way you need it.
  7. Unit cost, which matters at volume and is usually the last tie-breaker at low volume, because an engineering week costs more than a thousand chips.

A note that is worth saying in an interview because it shows you have thought past the datasheet: speed is often the wrong axis entirely. Moving from a 72 MHz M3 to a 400 MHz M7 can make a system less deterministic, because caches and a more complex bus matrix widen the spread between best and worst case (chapter 43). Real-time is about the worst case, not the average (chapter 37).

Development boards, and what to actually buy

🧒 Simple

You do not need a custom board to learn this. ST sells small boards with the chip, a debugger and the connectors already on them, for the price of a pizza.

🎓 Professional
  • Nucleo — about fifteen euro, an onboard ST-LINK, Arduino and Morpho headers, and a virtual COM port over the same USB cable. For learning this course, a Nucleo-F446RE or F401RE is the sensible default: the F4 line has the most written about it, and every chapter here maps onto it.
  • Discovery — more peripherals soldered on (screen, audio, sensors), good for exploring a specific capability.
  • Eval — everything, expensive, for real product evaluation.

Two accessories change what you can learn, and both are cheap. A logic analyser — a clone costs under twenty euro and turns framing, SPI and I²C from theory into something you can see. And a USB-to-RS485 adapter, which lets you talk Modbus RTU to your own board from a PC, which is the single most job-relevant thing you can build at a desk in this region's market.

The honest boundary, stated on the front page of this course and worth repeating here: the board teaches everything except what a board teaches. Layout, EMC, a supply that sags when a relay closes, a connector that vibrates loose — those need the hardware team and real products, and a CV should not claim them (chapter 70).

⚠ Trap

“It is all STM32, so the code will port.”

Within a family, mostly true. Across families, the peripherals are frequently different designs with the same name, and the differences are exactly the kind that compile cleanly and behave differently. The F1's GPIO configuration registers have a completely different layout from the F4's. The F4 and the L4 have different ADC designs with different calibration requirements. An F7 or H7 adds caches, so a DMA buffer that worked on an F4 now needs cache maintenance or a non-cacheable MPU region (chapter 27). Even the HAL changes shape between families, so code that “only uses the HAL” ports with edits and retesting, not by recompiling.

What makes a port cheap is not choosing the same vendor; it is the layering from chapter 35. If register access lives only in the hardware layer, a family change is a bounded rewrite of known files, and the device and application layers — along with their host tests — carry over unchanged. That is the concrete, checkable benefit of the architecture, and it is a much better answer in an interview than “we used the HAL”.

The related purchasing trap: choosing a part because a forum post or a tutorial used it. The tutorial's author had different constraints, and frequently an older silicon revision whose errata differ from the one you will buy.

Try it. The first four cost nothing and are the ones an interviewer's question actually probes.

1. Decode five part numbers, out loud, without the table:
   STM32F103C8T6, STM32F407VGT6, STM32G474RET6, STM32L476RGT6,
   STM32H743ZIT6. Family, sub-family, pins, flash, package, temp.
   The first one is the "blue pill" and knowing what it is matters,
   because half the tutorials on the internet use it.

2. Do a real selection exercise. Requirements: two CAN interfaces,
   one RS-485 UART, 8 PWM outputs with dead time, 12 analogue inputs,
   an EEPROM emulation area, -40..85 C, and availability today. Use
   ST's product selector, narrow it, and write down WHY each
   candidate survived or died. Then check lead times on a
   distributor. That exercise is the chapter.

3. Open CubeMX for your chosen part and try to assign all the
   functions from exercise 2 at once. Watch a conflict appear on a
   pin. That is the constraint nobody sees from the summary page.

4. Take one peripheral -- GPIO configuration is the clearest -- and
   compare its register section in the F1 reference manual with the
   F4's. Same peripheral name, different registers. That comparison
   is what "it is all STM32" misses.

5. Then buy a Nucleo-F446RE, a logic analyser clone and a USB-RS485
   adapter. About fifty euro total, and it unlocks every practical
   exercise in Parts 4 to 7 of this course.
Golden rules
  • The part number is a sentence: family, sub-family, pins, flash, package, temperature. Learn to read it.
  • Choose by peripherals and pin mapping first, not by clock speed.
  • Pin count is not pin availability. The alternate-function mux decides, and collisions are found in the pinout tool.
  • Leave memory headroom — a bootloader, diagnostics and field update all arrive later.
  • Availability and longevity are engineering constraints, and pin-compatible alternatives are a risk control.
  • Faster can be less deterministic. Caches and complex bus matrices widen the worst case.
  • Peripherals with the same name differ across families — GPIO, ADC, caches — and they differ in ways that compile cleanly.
  • What makes a port cheap is the layering, not the vendor.
  • A Nucleo plus a logic analyser plus a USB-RS485 adapter is about fifty euro and covers most of this course.
  • The board cannot teach you layout, EMC or a sagging supply. Do not claim them.

“Come sceglierebbe il microcontrollore per un prodotto nuovo?”

“Non parto dai megahertz, parto dalle periferiche: quanti CAN, quante seriali, quanti timer con uscite complementari e dead time se ci sono stadi di potenza, quanti canali analogici. Lì si elimina già quasi tutto. Poi guardo la mappatura dei pin, non il numero: ogni funzione esce solo su certi pin attraverso il mux delle alternate function, e due cose che mi servono possono collidere sullo stesso pin — è il passaggio che si salta più spesso e si paga in fase di layout. Poi memoria con margine, perché bootloader, diagnostica e aggiornamento in campo arrivano dopo e nessun progetto resta della dimensione del primo giorno; e poi disponibilità e longevità, che dal 2021 sono un vincolo di progetto a tutti gli effetti — preferisco una famiglia dove ho alternative pin-compatibili. Una cosa che dico volentieri: più veloce non vuol dire più adatto. Passando a un M7 con le cache il caso peggiore si allarga, e il real-time è il caso peggiore, non la media. E sul ‘tanto è sempre STM32, il codice si porta’: dentro la stessa famiglia sì, fra famiglie no — GPIO e ADC sono proprio progetti diversi con lo stesso nome. Quello che rende economico un porting non è il produttore, è avere gli accessi ai registri confinati nel livello hardware.”
