What a PLC is, for a firmware engineer

The machine at the other end of your cable does not think the way your firmware thinks. It has no interrupts, no tasks and no event loop — it reads the whole world, computes, writes the whole world, and repeats, forever. Understanding that one loop explains almost everything else about it.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Configurare l’interfaccia e la comunicazione tra schede elettroniche proprietarie e l’ecosistema dell’automazione industriale (PLC, sensori, attuatori)”, and the earlier line saying the role works “sia su firmware di sistemi embedded, che su software PLC”.

This course does not try to make you a PLC programmer. That is a different career, and it competes against periti elettrotecnici with real machine hours. Part 9 is the amount of the PLC world you need in order to be the device on the other end of the cable — which is a genuinely scarce skill, because neither side usually learns the other.

The scan cycle

🧒 Simple

A PLC does the same three things over and over, thousands of times a minute:

  1. Look at every input — every switch, every sensor — and write them all down.
  2. Run the whole program, using only what it wrote down.
  3. Set every output to whatever the program decided.

It never looks at an input in the middle of step 2. If a button is pressed and released during the program, the PLC simply never saw it.

🎓 Professional

The cycle is read inputs → execute → write outputs → housekeeping, and the key consequence is the process image: the program operates on a snapshot copied at the top of the scan, not on live I/O. Two things follow immediately, and both matter when you are the device talking to it:

A signal shorter than one scan may be missed entirely. Scan times are typically 1–20 ms. If your board pulses a digital output for 200 µs, the PLC will usually not see it. Either hold the signal for several scan times, or use a handshake where the PLC acknowledges and you clear (chapter 58). Fast events go to dedicated high-speed counter inputs or interrupt OBs, not to ordinary I/O.

Determinism is structural, not achieved. A PLC is predictable because the architecture forbids most of what makes firmware unpredictable: no dynamic allocation, no recursion, bounded loops, and a watchdog on the scan time itself. It is the extreme end of the static-allocation argument in chapter 11, enforced by the language rather than by discipline.

Physically: a CPU module on a DIN rail or in a rack, with digital-input, digital-output, analogue and communication modules beside it, all on a backplane. Memory is divided into inputs (I), outputs (Q), a work area (M), data blocks, and a retentive area that survives power loss — which is one of the first things to ask about when designing a fail-safe state.

IEC 61131-3, in five languages

🧒 Simple

PLC programs are often not typed as text at all. The most common form looks like an electrical wiring diagram: power flows from a rail on the left, through contacts, to a coil on the right. Electricians could read it before anyone called it programming, which is exactly why it won.

🎓 Professional

The standard defines five, and you should be able to recognise all five and write one:

  • LD — Ladder Diagram. Graphical, rungs of contacts and coils. Dominant in Italy and everywhere else. Readable by maintenance staff at 03:00, which is its entire justification and a very good one.
  • ST — Structured Text. Pascal-like, textual. The one a software engineer should reach for, and the one the companion plc-lab exercises are written in.
  • FBD — Function Block Diagram. Graphical blocks wired together; natural for analogue and control loops.
  • SFC — Sequential Function Chart. Steps and transitions — a state machine, drawn. For anything with a sequence, it is the right tool, and it is the direct relative of chapter 07.
  • IL — Instruction List. Assembly-like, deprecated in the third edition. Recognise it; do not learn it.

Concepts that carry over from your side, with different names: a POU is roughly a function; a FUNCTION_BLOCK is an object with persistent state between calls (a class with one method, effectively); TON, TOF and TP are the standard timer blocks; R_TRIG and F_TRIG are edge detectors. There is no sleep, because blocking the scan would stop the machine.

The vendors matter commercially. Siemens TIA Portal dominates in Emilia-Romagna machine building; Codesys is the vendor-neutral IEC 61131-3 environment that many other makers rebrand, and it is free to download, which is why learning material tends to be Codesys while adverts tend to say Siemens. The concepts transfer; the hardware configuration, tag tables and project organisation do not.

⚠ Trap

Do not assume that because you can program, you can program a PLC. This is the attitude that makes firmware engineers unpopular in an automation department, and it is wrong on the merits.

The hard parts of PLC work are not linguistic. They are: knowing what the machine does when power is lost mid-cycle; knowing that an emergency stop is wired normally closed so that a cut cable stops the machine rather than disabling the stop button — fail-safe by wiring, not by code; knowing which faults must latch until a human acknowledges them; and knowing that the maintenance electrician who has to fix it at 03:00 must be able to read what you wrote. Clever ST that nobody on site can follow is a liability, not a contribution.

Bring the humility to the interview. “I can read ladder, I write ST, and I know where my experience stops” is a strong answer. “It's just programming” is not.

Try it. Free, and it is the fastest possible way to stop guessing about this.

1. Download Codesys (free; the SoftPLC runs for 2 hours, then restarts --
   which is fine for learning and annoying by design). OpenPLC is a fully
   free alternative if you prefer.

2. Work through the five Structured Text programs in plc-lab:

     01_seal_in.st        latching, and why an E-stop is normally closed
     02_sequencer.st      TON/TOF/TP, the self-resetting timer idiom
     03_conveyor.st       R_TRIG edge counting, tracking by DISTANCE
     04_state_machine.st  the same cycle in ST and in SFC
     05_analog_pid.st     raw-count scaling and the 4-20 mA live zero

   Each one ends in a deliberate TRY THIS breakage. Do the breakages --
   that is where the learning is, not in the working version.

3. Then do the thing this chapter is really for: write a tiny ST program
   that toggles a bit, and a C program on your PC that polls it over
   Modbus TCP (chapter 53). The moment your code and a PLC exchange one
   boolean, the whole of Part 9 stops being abstract.

Golden rules
  • Read inputs, execute, write outputs, repeat. The program sees a snapshot, never live I/O.
  • A pulse shorter than one scan does not exist. Hold signals for several scans, or handshake.
  • Scan times are typically 1–20 ms, and a scan-time watchdog enforces them.
  • Determinism is structural: no dynamic allocation, no recursion, bounded loops.
  • Five languages; ladder dominates because maintenance staff can read it at 03:00.
  • An E-stop is wired normally closed — fail-safe by wiring, not by code.
  • Ask what is retentive before designing any behaviour that must survive a power cut.
  • Adverts say Siemens; free learning is Codesys. Concepts transfer, tooling does not.

“Ha esperienza di PLC? Sa cos’è il ciclo di scansione?”

“Sì: il PLC legge tutti gli ingressi in un’immagine di processo, esegue il programma su quella fotografia, scrive tutte le uscite, e ricomincia — tipicamente in qualche millisecondo. La conseguenza pratica che mi riguarda più da vicino, visto che arrivo dal lato firmware, è che un impulso più corto di una scansione per il PLC non è mai esistito: se la mia scheda alza un segnale per duecento microsecondi, non lo vede. Quindi o lo tengo alto per diverse scansioni, o faccio un handshake in cui il PLC mi conferma e io abbasso. Sul mio livello sono onesto: leggo il ladder e scrivo Structured Text, ho fatto degli esercizi miei in Codesys — latch con seal-in, temporizzatori, un SFC per un ciclo di lavorazione — ma non ho ore di macchina. So però che le parti difficili non sono il linguaggio: sono cosa fa la macchina se manca la tensione a metà ciclo, perché l’emergenza è cablata normalmente chiusa, e il fatto che a leggerlo alle tre di notte c’è il manutentore.”
