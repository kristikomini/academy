Industrial Ethernet: PROFINET, EtherCAT, EtherNet/IP, OPC UA

Four names that appear together in adverts and are not four versions of the same thing. Three are real-time field buses over Ethernet cabling, tied to different PLC vendors; the fourth is not a field bus at all. Which one you need is decided by whose PLC is in the cabinet, and it changes the whole architecture — and the budget.

From the advert

Not in the anchor advert, and listed as a set across the regional listings collected beside it. They belong here for a practical reason: a junior who can say what each one is, and that they are not alternatives you pick by preference, is ahead of most candidates — and the honest answer to “have you implemented one?” is almost always no, for reasons this chapter makes defensible.

Why ordinary Ethernet is not enough

🧒 Simple

Ordinary Ethernet makes no promise about when a message arrives. Usually it is fast; but if two devices transmit at once, a switch holds one back, and occasionally a message is delayed by milliseconds.

For a web page that does not matter. For eight motors that must move in step, it does: the command has to arrive at every axis at the same instant, every cycle, without exception. So the industry built protocols that use Ethernet cables and hardware but add their own rules about timing.

🎓 Professional

The requirement is determinism, which is chapter 37 applied to a network: a bounded worst case, not a good average. Standard switched Ethernet with TCP/IP gives you neither bounded latency nor bounded jitter, because queueing delay depends on other people's traffic. The three real-time protocols each solve it differently:

  • PROFINET (Siemens ecosystem, the default in most Italian plants). Runs over standard Ethernet hardware. RT traffic uses a dedicated EtherType and bypasses IP, and it shares the wire with ordinary TCP/IP traffic; cycle times of a few milliseconds are typical. IRT adds scheduled time slots and needs special switch silicon for microsecond precision. A device is described by a GSDML file that the engineering tool imports.
  • EtherCAT (Beckhoff). The clever one: a single frame is sent round a ring and each slave reads its own data out of it and writes its reply into the frame as it passes through, in hardware, without storing and forwarding. That is why cycle times reach tens of microseconds with a hundred axes. It requires a dedicated slave controller chip — an ESC such as an ET1100, or an MCU with EtherCAT peripherals — so the cost is in silicon and the device description is an ESI file.
  • EtherNet/IP (Rockwell, dominant in North America). Encapsulates the CIP object model over ordinary TCP and UDP; implicit cyclic messaging runs over UDP, explicit request-response over TCP. Runs on standard hardware; devices carry an EDS file.
  • OPC UA is not a field bus and grouping it with the others is the common misunderstanding. It is a platform-independent, secure, information-modelling protocol for machine-to-machine and machine-to-IT communication: browsable address spaces, typed objects, subscriptions, built-in authentication and encryption. Classic OPC UA over TCP is for supervision, MES and configuration, not for a motion loop. OPC UA PubSub over TSN is the initiative aiming at the deterministic case, and it is genuinely different from the classic client-server usage.

Two more names worth recognising: TSN (Time-Sensitive Networking), a set of IEEE standards adding scheduling and time synchronisation to plain Ethernet, which most of the above are converging towards; and IEEE 1588 / PTP, the precision time protocol that lets distributed nodes share a clock to sub-microsecond accuracy, which underpins the synchronised cases.

What implementing one actually involves

🧒 Simple

You do not write these protocols yourself. You buy a stack, or a module, or a chip — and then you pay to have the result tested and certified by the organisation that owns the protocol, so that it may carry the name.

That is why the choice is a business decision as much as an engineering one, and why it is decided early.

🎓 Professional

The routes, roughly in order of cost and control:

  • A drop-in module — Anybus, netX and similar — a small board that speaks the field bus on one side and a simple serial or SPI interface to your MCU on the other. Highest unit cost, lowest engineering cost, and you can support several protocols by fitting different modules to the same design. For a small manufacturer this is very often the right answer, and saying so in an interview reads as commercial sense rather than as a lack of ambition.
  • A commercial stack ported onto your MCU, with a licence fee and vendor support.
  • An open-source stack — SOEM as an EtherCAT master, or a p-net PROFINET device stack — which is excellent for learning and for prototypes, and which still leaves certification, conformance testing and long-term maintenance with you.
  • Dedicated silicon, where EtherCAT effectively requires an ESC, and some MCUs integrate industrial-protocol peripherals.

Besides the stack, every one of these brings obligations that surprise people: a device description file (GSDML, ESI, EDS) that must match the firmware exactly; conformance testing at an accredited lab; membership of the relevant organisation and licence terms governing the logo; and a vendor ID that has to be obtained. None of that is firmware work, and all of it lands on the project.

The honest firmware summary, which is also the useful interview line: you do not implement PROFINET, you integrate it — mapping your application's data into the process image the stack exposes, meeting its cycle deadlines, handling its state machine, and keeping your control loop deterministic underneath it (chapter 43).

Which one, and what it means for the architecture

🧒 Simple

The protocol is not chosen by what you like. It is chosen by which PLC the customer already owns, because their PLC decides what it can talk to.

🎓 Professional

The decision tree in practice:

  • Siemens PLC → PROFINET. In Italian manufacturing this is the majority case by a wide margin.
  • Beckhoff, or motion with many synchronised axes → EtherCAT.
  • Rockwell / Allen-Bradley, typically an American customer → EtherNet/IP.
  • MES, supervision, data to the IT world → OPC UA — or MQTT, which is lighter and often preferred for pure telemetry.
  • None of the above, or budget matters more than cycle time → Modbus TCP, which every PLC on the market can speak and which costs nothing in licences. A great many perfectly good industrial products ship with exactly this, and knowing when it is sufficient is worth as much as knowing the fancier options.

What it means for your firmware, which is the part a firmware engineer is actually accountable for: a real-time field bus imposes a cycle deadline you must meet every time, so the process image has to be updated within the cycle without ever blocking; it usually wants its own task at high priority in the RTOS (chapter 39); it adds a state machine for connection establishment and parameterisation that your application must follow; and it constrains part selection, RAM and sometimes the PHY. None of that is optional, and all of it is easier if the layering in chapter 35 already keeps your control logic independent of whoever is asking for the data.

⚠ Trap

Claiming one of these four on a CV, and being asked which conformance test you failed.

These names look like protocol knowledge and are largely ecosystem knowledge. Somebody who has integrated PROFINET has a GSDML file that took three attempts to match the firmware, an opinion about the alarm mechanism, and a memory of a conformance test that failed on something procedural. Somebody who has read about it has four bullet points. The gap is visible in one follow-up question, and this is the single easiest place in this whole course to overclaim (chapter 70).

What is both true and worth saying, if you have done the exercises below: “I have not integrated PROFINET on a product. I know it is Siemens's ecosystem, that RT traffic bypasses IP and shares the wire with normal TCP while IRT needs scheduled switches, that a device is described by a GSDML file which has to match the firmware, and that conformance testing and a vendor ID are part of the project rather than an afterthought. I have run an EtherCAT master on a PC with SOEM against a real slave, so I understand the process image and the cycle, and I would expect to be integrating a stack rather than writing one.” That answer is accurate, it is checkable, and it is far stronger than a claim that collapses.

The second half of the trap is architectural rather than personal: assuming a field bus can be added at the end. It changes the MCU, the RAM budget, the PHY, the task structure, the certification plan and the schedule. When somebody says “and then we will add PROFINET” late in a project, the honest response is that it is a new project for the communication half of the product — and being the person who says so early is valuable.

Try it. You can get real experience here on a PC, for nothing, and it is disproportionately impressive because almost no junior has done it.

1. Read one GSDML and one ESI file. Both are XML and both are freely
   downloadable from any vendor's site. Find the process-image
   definition: the actual bytes exchanged every cycle. That structure
   is what your firmware would have to fill.

2. Run an EtherCAT master on Linux with SOEM (Simple Open EtherCAT
   Master). Even without a slave, its examples and source show the
   cycle, the process image and the state machine -- and if you can
   borrow any EtherCAT slave device, you can scan and drive it in
   about fifty lines.

3. Capture PROFINET traffic in Wireshark, which decodes it, from any
   site or demo kit you can get near. Look at the RT frames: note the
   EtherType, that there is no IP header, and the cycle time between
   frames. Ten minutes and the protocol stops being an acronym.

4. Run an OPC UA server on your PC (open62541 or the Python
   opcua-asyncio) and browse it with UaExpert, the free client. Build
   a small information model -- a machine object with temperature and
   status variables -- and subscribe to changes. THIS is the one most
   likely to appear in a job you actually get, and it runs entirely on
   a laptop.

5. Then write the comparison table yourself, from what you saw: which
   needs special silicon, which shares the wire with TCP/IP, which is
   client-server rather than cyclic, which cycle times each reaches,
   and what file describes a device. Being able to produce that table
   from memory is the realistic goal of this chapter.
Golden rules
  • They are not alternatives you choose by preference. The customer's PLC decides.
  • Standard Ethernet gives no bounded latency; these protocols exist to add determinism.
  • PROFINET is Siemens, runs on standard hardware, RT bypasses IP, IRT needs scheduled switches, device described by GSDML.
  • EtherCAT processes a frame on the fly as it passes each slave, reaching tens of microseconds, and needs dedicated slave silicon; described by ESI.
  • EtherNet/IP is CIP over TCP and UDP, Rockwell's ecosystem, described by EDS.
  • OPC UA is not a field bus — it is secure information modelling for supervision and IT, with PubSub over TSN aiming at the deterministic case.
  • You integrate a stack, you do not write one: module, commercial stack, open-source stack, or dedicated silicon.
  • The device description file must match the firmware exactly, and conformance testing, membership and a vendor ID are project costs.
  • Modbus TCP is enough for a great many products, costs no licence, and every PLC speaks it.
  • A field bus imposes a cycle deadline, a high-priority task and a connection state machine on your firmware.
  • It cannot be added at the end. It changes the MCU, the RAM, the schedule and the certification plan.
  • Do not overclaim these. Say what you have run, and what you would expect to integrate.

“Conosce PROFINET, EtherCAT, EtherNet/IP, OPC UA?”

“So cosa sono e so soprattutto che non sono quattro alternative fra cui si sceglie per gusto: decide il PLC che ha il cliente. PROFINET è il mondo Siemens, gira su hardware Ethernet standard, il traffico RT salta l’IP e convive sul filo col TCP normale, mentre l’IRT vuole switch con slot programmati; il dispositivo è descritto da un file GSDML. EtherCAT è Beckhoff ed è l’idea più elegante: il frame gira nell’anello e ogni slave legge e scrive al volo mentre passa, in hardware — per questo arriva a decine di microsecondi con tanti assi — ma serve un chip slave dedicato. EtherNet/IP è CIP sopra TCP e UDP, mondo Rockwell, tipicamente clienti americani. E OPC UA non è un bus di campo, ed è l’errore più comune metterlo nella stessa lista: è modellazione dati sicura per supervisione e MES, client-server, non per un anello di controllo assi — il caso deterministico è PubSub su TSN. Sul lato pratico sono onesto: non ne ho integrato uno su un prodotto. So che non si scrivono, si integrano — modulo tipo Anbus, stack commerciale o open source — e che il file di descrizione deve combaciare col firmware e che la certificazione di conformità e il vendor ID sono costi di progetto, non dettagli. Ho provato un master EtherCAT su PC con SOEM e ho costruito un server OPC UA con open62541 per capire il modello. E dico sempre che per moltissimi prodotti Modbus TCP basta e non costa licenze.”
