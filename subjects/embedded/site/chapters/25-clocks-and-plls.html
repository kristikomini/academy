Clocks and PLLs

Every peripheral is asleep until something clocks it, every baud rate and every timer period is derived from a clock you chose, and the most common bug in embedded work is code that is entirely correct running on a peripheral that is not being clocked at all.

From the advert

Not named in any advert, and named in every debugging session. It is the prerequisite behind the advert's protocolli di comunicazione seriale: a UART's baud rate is a division of its peripheral clock, so “the other end sees garbage” is very often a clock-tree question rather than a protocol one.

The tree: source, PLL, prescalers, gates

🧒 Simple

A microcontroller needs a heartbeat. It can use a cheap one built into the chip, or an accurate one from a crystal on the board. That heartbeat is usually slow — 8 or 16 MHz — so a circuit called a PLL multiplies it up to the speed the processor runs at.

From there the fast clock is divided down again, in stages, for different parts of the chip: one speed for the core, slower ones for the peripheral buses. And at the very end, each peripheral has its own switch. That switch is off when the chip powers up.

🎓 Professional

The chain, in the order the reference manual draws it:

  • Sources. HSI, the internal RC oscillator — instantly available, typically ±1 % over temperature. HSE, an external crystal or oscillator — tens of parts per million, needs milliseconds to stabilise, and can fail. LSI and LSE, the slow pair, for the watchdog and the RTC (chapter 28).
  • The PLL, configured as divide, multiply, divide: on an F4 that is M, N, P, each with a legal range and an intermediate frequency that must stay inside a window. CubeMX's clock view exists because these constraints interact.
  • SYSCLK, selected from HSI, HSE or the PLL, then divided by the AHB prescaler to give HCLK — the core and memory clock — and again by the APB1 and APB2 prescalers for the peripheral buses. APB1 is usually limited to half the maximum, which is why the same timer can run at different speeds depending on which bus it is on.
  • Peripheral clock enables, one bit per peripheral in the RCC, all zero at reset.

Two rules that are easy to skip and expensive to skip. Flash wait states must be raised before the core speeds up and lowered only after it slows down: flash is slower than the core, and running 168 MHz against a zero-wait-state setting is a hard fault at best. And a timer clocked from an APB bus with a prescaler greater than 1 runs at twice the bus clock — an ST design quirk, stated in one line of the reference manual, and a perennial source of periods that are exactly half or double what was intended.

Worth knowing for a real product: the Clock Security System detects an HSE that stops — a cracked crystal, a bad solder joint — and switches back to HSI while raising an NMI. A machine whose crystal dies and silently keeps running at a different speed is a machine doing something unpredictable at full power; the CSS is a few lines and turns that into a defined event.

Accuracy: where it matters and where it does not

🧒 Simple

The built-in oscillator is convenient but not very accurate. For blinking an LED that is fine. For talking to another device it may not be: both ends have to agree on the speed of the bits, and if your clock is off by a couple of percent the other end starts reading the wrong bits.

🎓 Professional

The arithmetic that decides it, for an asynchronous link (chapter 46): the receiver samples in the middle of each bit after synchronising on the start edge, so by the tenth bit a constant frequency error has accumulated ten times. The usual budget is about 2 % total between the two ends, which means each end should stay within roughly 1 %. HSI at ±1 % across temperature is therefore marginal rather than safe, and that is the honest description: it usually works on the bench at room temperature and fails in a cabinet in August.

Where each source is the right answer:

  • HSI is fine for internal timing, PWM, ADC triggering, control loops, and any protocol with its own clock line — SPI and I²C carry a clock, so accuracy does not enter into it.
  • HSE (crystal) is required for asynchronous serial at any distance, for CAN — whose bit timing and sample point (chapter 49) assume a stable reference — for USB, and for Ethernet.
  • LSE (32.768 kHz) for a real-time clock that must keep the date across months, not for anything fast.

And the number nobody computes until it bites: the baud rate error from integer division. A UART divides its peripheral clock by an integer (with a fractional part on ST parts), so at some clock and baud combinations the achievable rate is 1.5 % off before the oscillator error is added. The reference manual prints a table of these; CubeMX shows the realised rate. Read it, rather than assuming the number you typed is the number on the wire.

Bringing the clock tree up, and proving it

🧒 Simple

Since everything depends on the clock, the first thing to do on a new board is prove the clock is what you think it is — not assume it. The chip can output its internal clock on a pin, so you can put a probe on it and see the real number.

🎓 Professional

The bring-up order, which is also the order things go wrong:

  1. Start on HSI and get something visible working — a pin toggling, a character on a UART. A board that does nothing at all is much harder to diagnose than a slow one.
  2. Enable HSE and wait for its ready flag with a timeout. No timeout means a board with a bad crystal hangs forever in SystemClock_Config, before any of your diagnostics exist — the worst possible place to hang.
  3. Set the flash wait states, then configure and enable the PLL, then switch SYSCLK to it, then read back the status bits to confirm the switch actually happened.
  4. Update SystemCoreClock. Every delay and baud calculation in CMSIS-based code reads that variable, and a stale value produces timing that is wrong by exactly the ratio you forgot.
  5. Prove it: route SYSCLK or HCLK to the MCO pin, divided down, and measure it with a scope or logic analyser. Thirty seconds, and it converts a belief into a measurement.

A cheap alternative when no scope is available: toggle a GPIO in a loop of known length, or set up a timer for exactly one second and blink. If the LED blinks at half a hertz, your clock is half what you think — and you have learned it in the first minute rather than in the middle of a protocol bug three weeks later.

⚠ Trap

The peripheral clock enable bit, and the fact that its absence is completely silent.

At reset every peripheral clock gate is closed. Write a peripheral's configuration registers with the gate closed and the writes go nowhere — on most STM32 parts no fault, no flag, nothing. Read back and you see zeros. Beginners conclude that the part is faulty or that the HAL is broken; the fix is one line in the RCC.

Two subtler versions of the same thing, both of which catch people who already know about the first. There is a documented delay between enabling a peripheral clock and being able to access the peripheral — ST's own errata describe it, and the HAL's macros insert a read-back for exactly this reason; enabling a clock and writing a register in the very next instruction can lose that write. And a GPIO port is a peripheral too, so a pin that does nothing when its timer is perfectly configured is frequently a GPIO port whose clock was never enabled.

The habit that makes all of it a non-event: after configuring any peripheral, read at least one register back and compare. It costs two lines and it converts the entire class of failure from a hunt into an assertion — which is the same argument as reading the map file in chapter 36 and the error counters in chapter 49.

Try it. Steps 1 and 2 need nothing but the documentation; the rest want a Nucleo.

1. Draw your own clock tree from the reference manual, on paper, for
   the part you have: source -> PLL M,N,P -> SYSCLK -> AHB -> APB1,
   APB2, with the real numbers and the legal limits beside each stage.
   Then open CubeMX's clock view and compare. Where they differ, you
   misread something -- find out what.

2. Compute a baud rate error by hand. With PCLK1 at 42 MHz and a
   target of 115200, what divisor does the UART need, what rate does
   that actually give, and what is the percentage error? Then do it
   for 921600 and see the error grow.

3. Enable a peripheral clock, write a configuration register, read it
   back, and print both -- then comment out the clock enable and do it
   again. Seeing the write vanish in silence, deliberately, once, is
   worth more than reading this paragraph.

4. Put HCLK on the MCO pin with a prescaler and measure it. If you
   have no scope, blink an LED from a timer configured for exactly
   1 Hz and time ten blinks with a phone.

5. Break the crystal path. Configure HSE, then run the board with the
   HSE selection pointing at a source that is not there, and confirm
   your startup code times out and falls back to HSI instead of
   hanging. Most published SystemClock_Config functions hang; fix
   yours and keep it.

6. Then enable the Clock Security System, start on HSE, and short the
   crystal pins briefly with a jumper wire while a counter prints. The
   NMI should fire and the board should keep running on HSI. That is a
   genuinely impressive thing to have done and to describe.
Golden rules
  • Every peripheral clock gate is closed at reset, and writes to an unclocked peripheral vanish silently.
  • Read a register back after configuring. Two lines; removes a whole class of bug.
  • Raise flash wait states before raising the clock, and lower them only afterwards.
  • Wait for every ready flag with a timeout. A crystal that never starts must not hang the board before diagnostics exist.
  • Update SystemCoreClock, or every delay and baud calculation is wrong by a fixed ratio.
  • An APB timer clock is doubled when its prescaler is not 1. It is one line in the manual and it explains most factor-of-two timing bugs.
  • HSI is marginal for asynchronous serial, and fine for anything with its own clock line.
  • Budget about 2 % total frequency error across an asynchronous link, and include the integer-division baud error, not just the oscillator.
  • Measure the clock on MCO rather than believing it.
  • Enable the Clock Security System in a product, so a dead crystal is an event rather than undefined behaviour.

“Una periferica configurata bene non fa niente. Da dove parte?”

“Dal clock, sempre. Al reset tutti i gate dei clock delle periferiche sono chiusi, e su STM32 se scrivo i registri di una periferica non clockata le scritture spariscono senza nessun errore: rileggo e trovo zeri. Quindi la prima cosa che faccio è proprio quella, rileggere un registro dopo averlo scritto — sono due righe e mi tolgono di mezzo un’intera famiglia di problemi. E mi ricordo che anche la porta GPIO è una periferica: tante volte il timer è giusto e il pin non si muove perché manca il clock della porta. Sull’albero dei clock la sequenza è sorgente, PLL, SYSCLK, prescaler AHB e APB, e due cose che non salto mai: i wait state della flash vanno alzati prima di alzare la frequenza, e ogni attesa di un flag di ready ha un timeout — se il quarzo non parte, il codice deve ripiegare su HSI, non restare appeso dentro SystemClock_Config, che è il punto peggiore dove potersi piantare. Sull’accuratezza: con SPI o I2C il clock viaggia sul filo e non me ne preoccupo, ma su UART o CAN serve il quarzo — l’oscillatore interno all’uno per cento è al limite, funziona sul banco e ti lascia a piedi in quadro d’estate. E l’errore del baud rate lo calcolo, perché la divisione intera da sola può portarne un altro uno e mezzo per cento.”
