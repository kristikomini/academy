The oscilloscope and the logic analyser

Two instruments that look similar and answer opposite questions. One tells you what the voltage is doing; the other tells you what the bits are saying. Knowing which question you have is most of knowing how to find the fault — and it is the conversation the advert calls collaborare con il team hardware.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Collaborare con il team hardware per il debug e la validazione dei sistemi” — a line that appeared in the adverts this course was built from. That collaboration happens standing at a bench with an instrument between you, and being able to drive it, or at least to read what it shows, is what makes you a participant rather than a spectator.

Which instrument answers which question

🧒 Simple

A logic analyser only asks one thing about a wire: is it high or low? It asks that about many wires at once, for a long time, and it can turn the pattern back into the message being sent.

An oscilloscope asks a harder question: exactly what voltage is on this wire, at every instant? It shows the shape — how fast the edge is, whether it overshoots, whether the supply dips when something switches on.

So: a logic analyser answers “what was said, and when”. A scope answers “is this signal actually healthy”.

The practical difference in cost and reach: a cheap USB logic analyser (about twenty euro) gives you eight channels at 24 MS/s with software decoding of UART, SPI and I²C, CAN and more — and for firmware work it is the higher-value instrument by a distance, because most firmware questions are digital and about order and timing. A scope is what you borrow from the hardware team when the answer turns out not to be digital, and the moment it does, you need them anyway.

Key numbers to be able to state: a scope's bandwidth should be about five times the fastest edge frequency of interest (a 100 MHz scope will show a 10 ns edge as much slower than it is); sample rate should be several times the bandwidth; and a logic analyser must sample several times faster than the signal it is capturing, so 24 MS/s is fine for a 1 MHz SPI clock and useless for a 20 MHz one.

Triggering is the whole skill

🧒 Simple

The instrument cannot record forever. The art is telling it exactly when to start recording, so that the moment you care about is in the middle of the picture instead of never captured at all.

And the best trigger is often one your own firmware provides: toggle a spare pin at the moment the error is detected, and tell the instrument to capture around that pin.

🎓 Professional

Triggers, in increasing order of usefulness:

  • Edge — the default. Fine for a repetitive signal, useless for a rare event.
  • Pulse width — trigger on a pulse narrower than X, which is how you catch a glitch you cannot see at a zoomed-out timebase.
  • Protocol trigger — on a specific I²C address, a UART byte, a CAN identifier. When the instrument supports it, this turns a needle-in-a-haystack into a one-click capture.
  • A firmware trigger pin, and this is the technique worth taking away from the chapter. Where your code detects the anomaly — a CRC failure, a timeout, an unexpected state — toggle a dedicated pin. Trigger the instrument on that pin with pre-trigger capture so the recording contains what happened before the detection. You have then used the one thing your firmware knows and the instrument does not: what “wrong” means.

Two mechanical points that cause more confusion than they should. Ground matters: a long ground lead on a probe is an inductor, and it manufactures ringing that is not on the board — if you are looking at fast edges, use the spring ground, not the crocodile clip. And a x10 probe loads the circuit ten times less than a x1 probe, which is why a signal can look clean on x10 and ring on x1, and why the scope must be told which is fitted or every voltage reading is out by a factor of ten.

Reading a bus and a rail together

🧒 Simple

The most useful captures are the ones with two things on the same screen: what the software thought it was doing, and what the wire actually did. When they disagree, the disagreement is the fault.

🎓 Professional

Captures that solve real problems, all of which you can set up yourself:

  • A decoded bus beside a firmware pin. Channel 0 is your “ISR running” pin, channels 1–3 are SPI. Now you can see that the interrupt fired 40 µs after the byte arrived, or that two transfers overlapped, or that your handler ran twice for one event.
  • The supply rail during an event. AC-coupled, small volts-per-division, while a relay closes or a motor starts. A rail that dips 400 mV explains resets, ADC readings that move (chapter 33) and I²C devices that lock up — and no amount of firmware fixes it.
  • The bus and the enable line. On RS-485 (chapter 45), capture the driver-enable pin with the data: releasing the line too early truncates the last character, too late holds the bus and collides with the reply. It is the single most common RS-485 fault and it is instantly visible.
  • Two nodes that disagree. Probe the same wire at both ends of a long cable and compare — reflections, missing termination and ground offset all show up as a difference.

And the discipline that makes the capture worth something afterwards: save it and annotate it. A saved capture with a one-line description is evidence in a defect report and can be reopened months later; a photo of a screen taken on a phone is a memory. Free software such as the sigrok or Saleae ecosystems saves sessions to a file, and attaching one to a ticket is what turning an accusation into a fact looks like at the bench.

⚠ Trap

Firmware is blamed for a hardware fault for two weeks, because nobody put an instrument on the wire.

The pattern is always the same. A device answers nine messages in ten. Firmware adds retries, then timeouts, then a state machine to recover; the failure rate improves and never goes away. Two minutes with an analyser and a scope would have shown an I²C rise time far too slow for the pull-up resistors fitted, or an RS-485 enable released one bit early, or a 5 V device whose logic threshold the 3.3 V part never quite reaches.

The habit worth forming early: when software and hardware disagree about what happened, measure the wire before changing either. The measurement costs minutes and settles the question, and it changes your standing with the hardware team permanently — arriving with “here is the capture, the rise time is 2.3 µs and the bus is running at 400 kHz” is a completely different conversation from “your board does not work”.

The inverse error is just as common and worth naming: assuming the instrument is right. Probes lie in specific, learnable ways — a long ground lead invents ringing, an under-bandwidth scope invents slow edges, a logic analyser sampling too slowly invents missing pulses, and a probe on a high-impedance node changes it by being there. When a measurement is surprising, check the instrument's setup before believing the surprise.

Try it. The twenty-euro logic analyser from chapter 29 does everything here except the two scope exercises.

1. Decode your own bus. Put the analyser on your I2C or SPI lines,
   capture a transaction, and turn on protocol decoding. Compare what
   the decoder says against what your code intended to send. Doing
   this once removes most of the mystery from chapter 48.

2. Measure an ISR properly. Pin high on entry, low on exit; capture
   for a second; read duration, period and worst case from the
   analyser's measurements. Then add load elsewhere in the system and
   watch the jitter appear. That is chapter 43, measured.

3. Build a firmware trigger. Toggle a spare pin where your code
   detects an error -- a CRC failure or a timeout -- and set the
   analyser to trigger on it with pre-trigger capture. Now you have a
   recording of what happened BEFORE the fault, which is the part
   that matters.

4. Catch a glitch with a pulse-width trigger: generate a deliberate
   200 ns pulse once a second among normal traffic, and find it. At a
   zoomed-out timebase it is invisible to an edge trigger.

5. Prove the ground-lead effect, if you can borrow a scope. Look at a
   fast edge with the long crocodile ground, then with the spring
   ground. The ringing in the first is the probe, not the board --
   and knowing that stops you chasing a phantom.

6. Then look at a supply rail, AC-coupled, while switching something
   inductive. If you see more than a few tens of millivolts of dip,
   you have found something the firmware could never have fixed.
Golden rules
  • Logic analyser for “what was said and when”; scope for “is this signal healthy”.
  • For firmware work the analyser is the higher-value instrument, and it costs about twenty euro.
  • Sample several times faster than the signal, and match scope bandwidth to the edge, not to the clock.
  • Triggering is the skill. Edge, pulse width, protocol — and best of all a pin your firmware toggles when it detects the fault.
  • Use pre-trigger capture, because the cause is before the symptom.
  • Put a firmware pin on the same capture as the bus. The disagreement between intent and wire is the fault.
  • A long probe ground lead manufactures ringing. Use the spring ground for fast edges.
  • x10 versus x1 changes loading and scaling. Tell the scope which is fitted.
  • Look at the supply rail during switching events. Dips explain resets, ADC drift and locked-up buses.
  • Measure the wire before changing software or hardware when the two disagree.
  • Save and annotate the capture. It is evidence; a phone photo is a memory.
