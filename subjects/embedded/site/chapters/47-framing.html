Framing: knowing where a message starts

A UART delivers a stream of bytes. Your application needs a stream of messages, and nothing in the hardware tells you where one ends and the next begins. Getting that boundary wrong produces a bug that looks exactly like a hardware fault, which is why it is worth designing rather than discovering.

From the advert

Not named in this advert, which says “Implementare e testare protocolli di comunicazione seriale”. This is most of what the word testare in that line actually costs: writing the handler is the easy half, and proving it survives a truncated frame is the half that takes the time.

LogiFlow chapter 32d covers the same idea from the PC side, where a library has usually already solved it. Here you are writing the parser.

A byte stream is not a message stream

🧒 Simple

Imagine somebody reading you letters, one at a time, with no pauses and no punctuation. You would hear all the letters correctly and still have no idea where one word ends.

That is what a serial port gives you. The bytes are right; the boundaries are your problem. And if you ever get the boundary wrong, everything after it is wrong too, until something puts you back in step.

🎓 Professional

Three assumptions beginners make that are all false, and each costs a day:

“A read returns one message.” It returns whatever bytes had arrived. One call may hand you half a message, or two and a half messages. This is true on a UART and it is equally true of a TCP socket, which is a byte stream with the same property (chapter 53).

“Messages arrive whole.” A cable pulled out mid-transmission, a device reset, or a buffer overrun at either end leaves a partial message in your buffer forever unless you have designed a way out.

“If I lose sync I can restart the device.” You cannot — it is a machine in a plant. Resynchronisation must be automatic and must be designed in, because it is the only thing standing between one corrupted byte and a permanently dead link. This is the requirement most often missed.

Four ways to mark a boundary

🧒 Simple

There are only four basic ideas: use a special byte to mark the end, say up front how long the message is, use silence as the separator, or make every message the same fixed size.

🎓 Professional

1. Delimiter. A reserved byte marks the end — newline for a text protocol, 0x7E for HDLC and SLIP. Simple and self-resynchronising: after any corruption, the next delimiter puts you back in step, which is a genuinely valuable property.

The cost is that the delimiter must never occur in the payload, so binary data needs escaping. Classic byte stuffing replaces the delimiter inside the payload with a two-byte sequence, which makes the encoded length data-dependent — worst case doubles. COBS (Consistent Overhead Byte Stuffing) solves that properly: it removes all zero bytes from the payload with a strictly bounded overhead of one byte per 254, so 0x00 becomes an unambiguous frame delimiter. If you are designing a binary protocol from scratch, COBS plus a zero delimiter is the answer worth knowing.

2. Length prefix. A header gives the payload length. Efficient, no escaping, and it lets you allocate or reserve exactly once. The weakness is that it is not self-resynchronising: if the length field itself is corrupted you consume the wrong number of bytes and every subsequent message is misparsed. Mitigate with a fixed sync word before the length, a sanity check on the length against your buffer size, and a checksum that will fail and force a rescan.

3. Silence. Time is the delimiter: a quiet line means the message ended. This is Modbus RTU's 3.5-character rule. It needs no escaping and no overhead at all, and it is why RTU frames are so compact — but it makes framing a timing property, which is hard on a general-purpose OS and natural on a microcontroller with UART idle-line detection (chapter 46).

4. Fixed length. Every message the same size. Trivial and used more than people admit, in sensor links and internal board-to-board buses. Resynchronisation still needs a sync word, or one lost byte shifts you permanently.

Whichever you choose, add a checksum or CRC. A parity bit catches one bit; CRC-16 catches all single and double bit errors, all odd numbers of errors, and any burst up to 16 bits, for two bytes and a small table. A plain additive checksum is much weaker — it cannot detect reordered bytes and misses many multi-bit patterns — and is worth using only where the standard demands it.

⚠ Trap

A length field you have not sanity-checked is a remote crash, and on a networked device it is a security bug rather than a robustness one. 

uint16_t len = (buf[2] << 8) | buf[3];
memcpy(frame, &buf[4], len);          /* len can be 65535. frame is 256. */

Check the length against your actual buffer before you use it, every time, and treat a failure as a reason to discard and resynchronise rather than to continue. The same check belongs on every index derived from received data. Once the board has an Ethernet port, the attacker chooses that length (chapter 14).

The companion trap is having no timeout. If the parser is waiting for the remaining bytes of a frame that will never arrive, it must give up — after an inter-character timeout — discard, and go back to hunting for a start. A parser that can enter a state it cannot leave is not a parser, it is a latch, and it takes exactly one pulled cable to prove it.

Try it. Entirely on a PC. Framing is pure logic, so this is one of the places where off-target testing (chapter 64) is not a compromise at all — it is better than testing on the board, because you can inject faults precisely.

1. Write the parser as a state machine that consumes ONE BYTE at a time
   and returns "no frame yet" or "here is a frame". That shape is the
   whole trick: it never blocks, it never assumes a read boundary, and
   it compiles and runs on your laptop.

     typedef enum { HUNT, LEN, PAYLOAD, CRC_LO, CRC_HI } st_t;
     bool frame_feed(uint8_t b, frame_t *out);

2. Now test it the way it will actually be attacked. Feed it:

     - a good frame, one byte at a time            -> accepted
     - a good frame, all at once                   -> accepted
     - TWO frames in one buffer                    -> both accepted
     - a frame split after every possible byte     -> accepted, all N
     - one byte flipped in the payload             -> rejected by CRC
     - a length field of 0xFFFF                    -> rejected, no crash
     - half a frame, then silence, then a good one -> the good one is
                                                      accepted
     - a megabyte of random bytes, then a good one -> accepted

   That last two are the resynchronisation tests, and they are the ones
   people skip. Loop the random-bytes test with a different seed each
   run in CI; it is a fuzzer in three lines.

3. Only once all of that passes, connect it to a real UART. Every bug
   you would otherwise have chased with an oscilloscope has already
   been found on your laptop, in milliseconds, reproducibly.
Golden rules
  • A read returns bytes, not messages — possibly half a message, possibly two.
  • Resynchronisation must be designed in, because you cannot go and restart a machine in a plant.
  • Delimiters self-resynchronise; length prefixes do not. Give a length-prefixed protocol a sync word and a CRC.
  • COBS gives a bounded one-byte-per-254 overhead and makes zero an unambiguous delimiter.
  • Silence as a delimiter costs nothing and makes framing a timing property — natural with UART idle-line detection.
  • CRC-16 catches all bursts up to 16 bits; an additive checksum is much weaker and misses reordering.
  • Sanity-check every received length against your buffer. On a networked device the attacker picks it.
  • Every parser state needs a timeout out of it. A parser that can get stuck is a latch.
  • Write the parser as a byte-at-a-time state machine, so it is testable on a PC and never assumes a read boundary.

“Come gestisce la ricezione di messaggi su una seriale?”

“La prima cosa che tengo presente è che la seriale mi dà byte, non messaggi: una lettura può darmi mezzo frame o due e mezzo, quindi il parser lo scrivo come una macchina a stati che consuma un byte alla volta e risponde ‘non ancora’ oppure ‘ecco il frame’. Così non blocca mai e non assume niente su dove finisce una lettura — e, cosa non secondaria, lo compilo e lo testo sul PC senza scheda. Sul delimitare i messaggi la scelta dipende: con un delimitatore mi risincronizzo da solo dopo una corruzione, ma sul binario mi serve l’escaping, e in quel caso uso COBS che ha un overhead limitato; con un campo lunghezza sono più efficiente ma non mi risincronizzo, e allora ci metto una parola di sincronismo davanti e un CRC. Su Modbus RTU il delimitatore è il silenzio, e lì me lo fa l’hardware con l’idle line. Due cose che controllo sempre: la lunghezza ricevuta la confronto con il mio buffer prima di usarla, perché altrimenti è una memcpy da 65535 byte in un array da 256, e con una porta Ethernet quella non è robustezza ma sicurezza; e ogni stato del parser deve avere un timeout per uscirne, altrimenti basta staccare un cavo a metà frame e resta lì per sempre.”
