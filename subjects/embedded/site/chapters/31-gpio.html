GPIO

The first peripheral anybody touches and the one with the most ways to be quietly wrong: four modes, two output drivers, a pull network, a speed setting that is really a slew rate, a mux with sixteen functions per pin, and one register that exists specifically so two pieces of code cannot corrupt each other's pins.

From the advert

Not named in any advert, because it is assumed the way breathing is assumed. Every line about sensori e attuatori and about the PLC on the other end of the cable lands here in the end: the signal becomes a pin, and the pin has a configuration that is either right or subtly wrong.

Four modes, and the drivers behind them

🧒 Simple

Each pin can be one of four things: an input you read, an output you drive, a connection handed over to another peripheral inside the chip (a UART, a timer), or an analogue pin wired straight to the ADC.

When it is an output there are two ways to drive it. The usual one pushes the pin both up and down by itself. The other one can only pull it down, and needs a resistor to pull it up — which sounds worse until you want several devices sharing one wire, because then only one of them ever fights.

🎓 Professional
  • Input, with the Schmitt trigger on, readable in the IDR. The internal pull-up or pull-down is selected separately, and “no pull” on an unconnected pin means a floating input, which reads randomly and burns current.
  • Output push-pull — a transistor pair driving both rails. The default for an LED, a relay driver input, a chip select.
  • Output open-drain — the pin can only pull low; a resistor (external, or the internal pull-up if the bus is slow and short) provides the high. This is what makes a shared bus possible: I²C requires it, and it is also how you drive a 5 V input from a 3.3 V part when the other side has its own pull-up to 5 V and the pin is five-volt tolerant.
  • Alternate function — the pin belongs to a peripheral, selected by a four-bit AF number in AFRL/AFRH. Which peripheral is on which AF number for which pin is a table in the datasheet, not a guess.
  • Analogue — the digital input buffer is disconnected, which is both what the ADC needs (chapter 33) and the lowest-power state for an unused pin.

The speed setting is not a speed. It is the slew rate of the output driver, and the trade is real: fast edges are needed for fast signals and are also what radiate, ring and couple into neighbours. The habit is to use the slowest setting that meets the signal's needs — low for an LED or a relay, high only for SPI at speed or an external memory bus. A board that fails EMC testing surprisingly often has every pin set to very high for no reason.

BSRR: the register that exists because of concurrency

🧒 Simple

The obvious way to set one pin is to read all sixteen, change your one, and write them all back. That works until an interrupt happens in the middle and changes a different pin — because when your code writes back, it writes the old values of every other pin too, undoing that change.

So the chip gives you a register where writing a 1 sets a pin and writing a 0 does nothing at all. No reading, no putting back, nothing to interrupt. It is one instruction and it cannot go wrong.

🎓 Professional
/* Read-modify-write: three operations, interruptible between them. */
GPIOA->ODR |= (1u << 5);        /* an ISR touching PA7 in between is lost  */

/* BSRR: one 32-bit write, atomic by construction.                        */
GPIOA->BSRR = (1u << 5);        /* set PA5   (low half sets)              */
GPIOA->BSRR = (1u << (5 + 16)); /* clear PA5 (high half resets)           */

The low sixteen bits set, the high sixteen reset, and zeros are ignored — so one write can change several pins in either direction simultaneously, and no read is involved, which also makes it faster. Reset wins over set if you write both for the same pin. On parts with a separate BRR, that is just the reset half with its own address.

Two related habits. Read IDR, not ODR, when you want to know what a pin is: ODR is what you asked for, IDR is what the pin is actually at, and on an open-drain output they differ precisely when something external is holding the line — which is information, not noise. And in an RTOS or an interrupt-heavy design, treat any read-modify-write on a shared peripheral register as a critical section unless the hardware gives you an atomic alternative; BSRR is that alternative for GPIO, and the general problem is chapter 42.

The world on the other side of the pin

🧒 Simple

A pin is not a variable. A button does not produce one clean change when pressed; the contacts bounce and produce a burst of them. A long wire picks up interference from a motor starting nearby. A 24 V industrial signal will destroy a 3.3 V pin.

So between the chip and the world there is always something: a resistor, an opto-isolator, a filter. Firmware has to know what is there.

🎓 Professional
  • Debouncing is a firmware job and a small one done right: sample the input on a periodic tick and require N consecutive agreeing samples, or timestamp the first edge and ignore changes for a settling period. What you should not do is delay(20) inside an interrupt, which is the version everybody writes first and which blocks everything else (chapter 26).
  • EXTI lines give an interrupt per pin number — and the constraint is structural: PA0 and PB0 share EXTI0, so two edge-sensitive inputs must be on different pin numbers. That is a schematic-level constraint discovered in firmware, and it is the sort of thing worth catching in review (chapter 63).
  • 24 V industrial inputs never reach the MCU directly. An opto-isolator or a divider with clamping sits in between, and it usually inverts — so active-low in the field is a 1 at the pin. That inversion belongs in one named function in the hardware layer, not sprinkled through the application as if (!pin) (chapter 35).
  • Outputs driving anything inductive — a relay, a solenoid — need a flyback diode on the board, and the pin drives a transistor, never the coil. Knowing this is firmware knowing its limits, and it is exactly the conversation the advert's collaborare con il team hardware describes.
  • Reset state. Between power-on and your configuration code, pins are inputs. If a line must be at a defined level during that window — a driver enable, a relay that must stay open — the board needs an external pull, not a line of firmware. This is a firmware engineer's most frequent useful contribution to a schematic review.
⚠ Trap

The pin is configured perfectly and does nothing, and the cause is one of four prerequisites rather than the code in front of you.

In order of how often they are the answer: the GPIO port clock is not enabled (chapter 25); the pin is in the wrong alternate function number, so a UART is transmitting into nothing while the pin sits as a plain output; the pin is one of the special ones — PA13/PA14 are SWD and reconfiguring them drops your debugger, PB3/PB4 are JTAG on many parts and default to it, PC13 to PC15 come from the backup domain and can only drive a few hundred microamps at low speed; or the peripheral that owns the pin is configured but not enabled.

All four are found the same way, and it is the habit this whole part of the course keeps repeating: read the registers back and look at them. A debugger's peripheral view shows MODER, OTYPER, PUPDR, AFR, IDR and ODR side by side; thirty seconds there beats thirty minutes of rereading source, because the registers cannot be wrong about what the hardware believes.

The specific cruelty of the SWD case deserves naming: configure PA13 as a GPIO in your initialisation and the board becomes unreachable by the debugger, immediately and permanently, because the reconfiguration happens microseconds after reset and before the debugger can attach. The recovery is to hold the board in reset while connecting (the “connect under reset” option) or to boot from the system bootloader with BOOT0, and everybody who has done it once checks the pin list forever after.

Try it. Steps 1 to 3 want a Nucleo and cost nothing else; step 5 wants the logic analyser from chapter 29.

1. Blink an LED with the registers, not the HAL. Enable the port
   clock in RCC, set MODER to output, then toggle with BSRR only --
   no read-modify-write anywhere. Fifteen lines, and it is the
   "hello world" that actually teaches something.

2. Prove BSRR matters. Toggle PA5 in the main loop with
   `ODR |= / ODR &= ~`, and toggle PA6 in a fast timer ISR the same
   way. Run it and watch pins occasionally miss a transition. Then
   change both to BSRR and watch it stop. That experiment is worth
   more than any explanation of atomicity.

3. Read IDR versus ODR on an open-drain output: drive it high, then
   short it to ground through a 1 k resistor, and print both. ODR
   says 1, IDR says 0. That difference is how you detect a stuck bus.

4. Configure a button with EXTI and count edges with no debouncing at
   all. Press it fifty times and read the counter -- it will be far
   more than fifty. Then add the N-consecutive-samples filter on a
   1 ms tick and repeat. Now you know what bouncing costs.

5. Set an output to "low" speed and to "very high" speed in turn and
   look at the edge on the logic analyser or scope. Then do it with a
   30 cm wire attached and look at the ringing. That is the EMC
   argument made visible in two minutes.

6. Read the datasheet's alternate-function table for your part and
   find every pin that can carry USART2_TX. Then find one pin where
   two functions you might plausibly want collide. That table is what
   chapter 29's selection exercise was really about.
Golden rules
  • Four modes: input, output, alternate function, analogue. Unused pins go to analogue or get a defined pull — never left floating.
  • Push-pull drives both rails; open-drain only pulls low and is what makes a shared bus and some level shifting possible.
  • The speed setting is slew rate. Use the slowest that works; fast edges radiate.
  • Use BSRR. A read-modify-write on ODR loses an interrupt's change to another pin in the same port.
  • Read IDR for what the pin is, ODR for what you asked for, and on open-drain the difference is a diagnostic.
  • Alternate function is a numbered table in the datasheet, not a guess.
  • PA13 and PA14 are SWD. Reconfigure them and you lose the debugger, instantly and permanently, until you connect under reset.
  • EXTI is shared by pin number across ports. Two interrupt inputs need two different pin numbers.
  • Debounce on a periodic tick, never with a delay inside an ISR.
  • Field signals are conditioned and usually inverted. Put that inversion in one named function in the hardware layer.
  • Between reset and your init, pins are inputs. Anything that must be safe in that window needs an external pull, not firmware.

“Perché si usa BSRR invece di scrivere direttamente su ODR?”

“Perché scrivere su ODR con un |= è in realtà leggi, modifica, riscrivi: tre operazioni, e in mezzo può arrivare un interrupt che cambia un altro pin della stessa porta. Quando poi riscrivo, riscrivo anche i vecchi valori di tutti gli altri pin e quella modifica sparisce — ed è un bug orribile da trovare, perché capita una volta ogni tanto e solo sotto carico. BSRR invece è una scrittura sola: i sedici bit bassi mettono a uno, i sedici alti mettono a zero, gli zeri non fanno niente. Non c’è lettura, quindi non c’è niente da interrompere, ed è anche più veloce. L’altra cosa che guardo sempre è la differenza fra IDR e ODR: ODR è quello che ho chiesto, IDR è com’è davvero il pin, e su un open-drain la differenza mi dice che qualcuno sta tenendo giù la linea — su I2C è proprio così che si accorge di un bus bloccato. E quando un pin non fa niente non riguardo il codice: guardo i registri col debugger. Nove volte su dieci è il clock della porta, o l’alternate function sbagliata, o un pin speciale — PA13 e PA14 sono l’SWD, se li riconfiguro perdo il debugger e devo ricollegarmi in connect under reset.”
