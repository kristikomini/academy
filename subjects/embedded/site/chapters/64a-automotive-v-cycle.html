The automotive V-cycle: requirements, architecture, integration

Why an automotive advert asks for requisiti e architetture and attività di build e integrazione rather than for features: what the V-model actually obliges you to produce, what Automotive SPICE is checking, where AUTOSAR fits, and what an integration engineer does on a Tuesday.

From the advert

Not in the anchor advert, which is a small-company firmware role where one person owns the whole board. This chapter comes from the second posting in the corpus — an Embedded Integration Engineer in Automotive, propulsion — whose responsibility lines are “Progettare, sviluppare e mantenere requisiti e architetture software”, “Implementare e integrare componenti software” and “Supportare le attività di build e integrazione software”.

That is a genuinely different job from the rest of this course, and the difference is worth naming: in a large automotive programme you are rarely the author of the code that runs. You are the person who makes ten suppliers' deliverables become one binary that boots, and who can prove which requirement each line of it answers. Chapter 64 is the testing half of this; chapter 65 is the standards half.

The V, and why both sides are the same list

🧒 Simple

Draw a V. Going down the left side you get more and more specific: what the vehicle must do, then what this control unit must do, then how the software is divided into pieces, then how each piece works. Going up the right side you test, in the opposite order: each piece alone, then the pieces together, then the whole control unit, then the vehicle.

The only idea in the diagram is this: each step on the way up tests the step at the same height on the way down. If nothing on the left says what “correct” means, then nothing on the right can test it — it can only check that the software does what it happens to do.

🎓 Professional

In automotive the V is not a preference, it is an audited process. The framework is Automotive SPICE (ASPICE), and its process names are the vocabulary a programme actually speaks in:

  • SYS.1 / SYS.2 / SYS.3 — stakeholder requirements, system requirements, system architecture. Above your level, but they are where your requirements come from.
  • SWE.1 software requirements analysis — the ECU-level requirements, derived from and traced back to SYS.2.
  • SWE.2 software architectural design — components, interfaces, the dynamic behaviour, and the resource and timing budgets.
  • SWE.3 detailed design and unit construction — the code.
  • SWE.4 unit verification, SWE.5 software integration and integration test, SWE.6 software qualification test. The advert's job title sits squarely on SWE.5.

The artefact that holds the whole thing together is bidirectional traceability: every requirement points down to the architecture element and the test that covers it, and every test points back up to the requirement it exists for. A requirement nothing tests, and a test tracing to no requirement, are both findings — the second one surprises people, but a test with no requirement means either an undocumented feature or a test measuring nothing anybody agreed to.

Requirements live in a tool for exactly this reason: DOORS, Polarion, codebeamer. The tool is not the point — the point is that a requirement has an identifier, a version, a status and links, and a sentence in a Word file has none of those. The practical skill is writing requirements that can be tested: one obligation per statement, no “fast” or “robust”, a number with a unit and a tolerance, and an explicit behaviour for the failure case. Most review comments you will receive are one of those four.

Layered on top for anything that can hurt somebody: ISO 26262, functional safety, with ASIL A to D classifying how bad a failure would be and tightening the obligations accordingly. Propulsion work is usually where the higher ASILs live. That is chapter 65, and the connection to this chapter is that ISO 26262 is what turns “we should trace requirements” into “we must, and here is the evidence”.

AUTOSAR in one page, because you will be asked

🧒 Simple

Car makers got tired of every supplier writing its own version of the same plumbing — talk to CAN, store a fault, read a sensor pin, answer a diagnostic tool. So they standardised the plumbing and left only the actual behaviour to be written.

The result is a layered stack. At the bottom, drivers that touch the chip. In the middle, the standard services. At the top, the application pieces, which are written as if they had no idea what hardware they are on — and a generated layer in between connects them. Most of it is not written but configured, in very large XML files, by tools.

🎓 Professional

AUTOSAR Classic — the one on a microcontroller; Adaptive is the POSIX-and-Ethernet one for high-performance computers, and knowing the difference is worth a sentence. Four layers:

  • MCAL, the microcontroller abstraction layer: the only code that touches registers, usually supplied by the silicon vendor. This is where everything in chapter 23 went.
  • BSW, the basic software: the CAN stack and PDU router, NVRAM, the DCM (diagnostic communication manager — which is the UDS server from chapter 49b), the DEM (diagnostic event manager, which owns the DTCs), the OS, and the watchdog manager.
  • RTE, the runtime environment: generated glue. An application component asks for a value through a port; the RTE decides whether that means a variable read or a CAN frame on another ECU, and the component never knows.
  • SWC, the application software components — the behaviour, often generated from Simulink models rather than hand-written, especially in propulsion.

The consequence for an integration engineer: a large part of the “source code” is configuration. ARXML files describe components, ports, signals, timing and the mapping onto tasks; a configurator generates the RTE and the BSW; and a merge conflict in a generated artefact is a genuinely different problem from a merge conflict in C. Hence chapter 66 matters more here than it looks, and hence “does the build reproduce bit for bit from a clean checkout” is a real question with a frequently embarrassing answer.

Honest boundary: plenty of automotive work — including many propulsion prototypes — is not AUTOSAR at all, and small suppliers ship bare-metal or FreeRTOS firmware into vehicles. Claim familiarity with the architecture and the vocabulary, which is checkable and true after reading this; do not claim to have configured a BSW stack if you have not, because the follow-up question is about a tool you have never opened.

What “build and integration” actually means as a day

🧒 Simple

Ten teams send you their pieces. Your job is to turn them into one working control unit, every day, automatically — and when it stops working, to find out whose piece did it and prove it before anyone argues.

So the work is: keep the build reproducible, keep a record of exactly which version of everything went into each binary, run the tests that catch integration problems specifically, and be the person who can answer “what changed?”.

🎓 Professional

Concretely, and this is the list to talk about in an interview:

  • The build. Make or CMake over a vendor compiler, dozens of variants (engine type, market, ECU hardware revision), and a linker script that must keep the bootloader, the application and the calibration area apart — chapter 36. The deliverable is usually a hex or s19 file plus an A2L describing where every calibration value lives in memory.
  • The baseline. A released binary is worthless without the exact set of source, configuration and tool versions that produced it. “Which compiler version?” is a safety question, not an administrative one, because a qualified toolchain is part of the argument that the software is correct.
  • Integration tests, which are not unit tests: they check the interfaces between components and the timing when everything runs together. Task overruns, stack high-water marks, CPU load, bus load, and the CAN matrix — every signal present, in the right frame, at the right cycle time.
  • The bench. HiL, and the Vector toolchain that surrounds it: CANoe and CANalyzer for observing and simulating the bus, CANape for calibration and measurement, a flashing tool for the UDS sequence in chapter 49b. Restbus simulation — pretending to be every ECU except the one under test — is how a single control unit is exercised without a vehicle.
  • The defect flow. A failure arrives as a bus trace and a description. Turning that into “component X violates requirement Y under condition Z” is the skill, and it is the same bisection habit as chapter 69, applied to other people's code.
⚠ Trap

The integration engineer becomes the person who gets blamed for every defect and owns no code to fix — and the way out is evidence, not argument.

The structural problem is real: integration is where defects appear, and the appearance is at your desk. If the only artefact you can produce is “the build is red”, then the conversation is about opinions and you will lose it daily.

Three habits that change the position entirely. Make every failure reproducible from a clean checkout with one command, so nobody can attribute it to your machine. Bisect to a change, not to a component — an automated daily build gives you the previous green baseline for free, and “it appeared in this commit” ends the discussion that “I think it's the CAN stack” starts. And write the defect against a requirement: not “the torque signal looks wrong” but “SWE.1 requirement 4711 says the signal is updated every 10 ms with tolerance 1 ms; measured 24 ms in baseline 2026-09-14”. The first is an accusation, the second is a fact with an owner.

The related trap in the other direction: a green build that proves nothing, because the integration tests only check that components compile together. Compiling together is not integrating; timing and interface semantics are where integration defects live, and a test suite that never measures a cycle time will pass every one of them.

Try it. Nothing here needs a vehicle, a licence or an ECU. All of it is doable on the same PC you use for the rest of this course, and all of it is demonstrable in an interview.

1. Write five real requirements for something you have already built
   -- the framing layer from chapter 47 is ideal. One obligation each,
   a number with a unit and a tolerance, and a defined behaviour when
   the input is invalid. Then have somebody try to test them and watch
   which ones turn out to be untestable. That exercise teaches more
   about SWE.1 than any course.

2. Build the traceability matrix by hand, in a table: requirement id,
   architecture element, source file, test case. Three columns of five
   rows. The point is to feel where it rots -- a renamed function, a
   deleted test -- because that is what the tooling exists to prevent.

3. Make a variant build. Take a small project, add two configurations
   that differ in one feature, and make ONE command build both into
   separate output directories with the feature name in the artefact.
   Then add a third variant and see what you had to change; if it was
   more than one line, your build system is the problem the advert is
   describing.

4. Prove reproducibility. Build twice from a clean checkout and diff
   the binaries. Embedded timestamps and __DATE__ are the usual
   culprits. Fixing that is a genuine, concrete integration
   contribution, and it is a good answer to "what did you improve?".

5. Run a CAN matrix check against yourself. Define five signals in a
   DBC file (open-source editors exist), generate traffic with the
   virtual bus from chapter 49a, and write a script that asserts every
   signal appears at its specified cycle time within tolerance. That
   is, in miniature, exactly what a bus integration test does.

6. Then read one public ASPICE process description end to end, and
   map each of steps 1 to 5 above onto SWE.1, SWE.2, SWE.5 and SWE.6.
   Being able to say which process your evidence belongs to is what
   makes the vocabulary yours rather than memorised.
Golden rules
  • Each right-hand side of the V tests the left-hand side at the same height. If nothing on the left defines correct, nothing on the right can test it.
  • Traceability is bidirectional. A requirement with no test and a test with no requirement are both defects in the process.
  • A requirement that cannot be tested is not a requirement. One obligation, a number with a unit and a tolerance, and a defined failure behaviour.
  • SWE.5 is the integration seat, and ISO 26262 with its ASIL levels is what makes the evidence mandatory rather than advisable.
  • AUTOSAR Classic is four layers — MCAL, BSW, RTE, SWC — and the DCM and DEM are the UDS server and the fault memory you already know.
  • Much of the source is configuration, in generated XML, so build reproducibility and version discipline matter more here than in hand-written firmware.
  • A baseline is source plus configuration plus tool versions, not a binary. “Which compiler version” is a safety question.
  • Compiling together is not integrating. Integration defects live in timing and interface semantics, so measure cycle times, CPU load, stack high-water marks and bus load.
  • Bisect to a change, not to a component. A daily green baseline turns a blame conversation into a commit hash.
  • Write every defect against a requirement id with a measured number. That is the difference between an accusation and a fact with an owner.
  • Claim the vocabulary and the architecture, not the tools you have not opened. The follow-up question is always about the tool.

“Cosa vuol dire per lei ‘integrazione software’ in ambito automotive?”

“Vuol dire essere quello che prende i deliverable di più team e li fa diventare un binario unico che gira, ogni giorno, in modo riproducibile. Il contesto è il modello a V, e in Automotive SPICE la posizione si chiama SWE.5: sotto ci sono i requisiti software, SWE.1, e l’architettura, SWE.2, e la regola che tiene insieme tutto è la tracciabilità bidirezionale — ogni requisito arriva a un test, ogni test risale a un requisito. Un test che non risale a niente per me è un problema tanto quanto un requisito senza test. Nel quotidiano sono tre cose: il build, con le sue varianti e il linker script che tiene separati bootloader, applicativo e area di calibrazione; la baseline, cioè sapere esattamente quali versioni di sorgenti, configurazione e toolchain hanno prodotto quel binario, che in ISO 26262 è una domanda di sicurezza e non burocrazia; e i test di integrazione veri, che non sono ‘compila tutto insieme’ ma tempi di ciclo, carico CPU, stack, carico bus e matrice CAN. La cosa che ho imparato è che chi fa integrazione si prende la colpa di tutti se porta opinioni: io porto un commit. Build pulito, bisezione fino alla modifica, e il difetto scritto contro un requisito con un numero misurato — non ‘il segnale sembra sbagliato’ ma ‘il requisito dice dieci millisecondi più o meno uno, misurati ventiquattro’.”
