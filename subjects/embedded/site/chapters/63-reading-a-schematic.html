Reading a schematic

Not to design one — to find your pin on it, follow the net to whatever is really on the other end, and ask the hardware engineer a question that saves both of you a day. Nets, rails, pull-ups, level shifters, and the six things firmware should always check before writing a driver.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Collaborare con il team hardware per il debug e la validazione dei sistemi” — a line that appeared in the adverts this course was built from. Collaboration here has a concrete meaning: the schematic is the shared document, and an engineer who cannot read it can only report symptoms.

Finding your way around the sheets

🧒 Simple

A schematic is not a picture of the board. It is a diagram of what is connected to what, arranged for reading rather than for building: the power supply on one sheet, the processor on another, the connectors on a third.

Lines are wires. Where two lines cross without a dot they are not connected; with a dot, they are. And a wire does not have to be drawn all the way: if the same name appears in two places, it is the same wire. That last convention is what makes a big schematic readable, and it is the one that confuses everybody at first.

🎓 Professional
  • Net labels are connections. SENSOR_SCL on sheet 2 and SENSOR_SCL on sheet 5 are one net. Searching the PDF for a net name is how you follow a signal, and it is faster than tracing lines.
  • Reference designators: R for resistor, C for capacitor, U for integrated circuit, Q for transistor, D for diode, J for connector, TP for test point, FB for ferrite bead. R47 on the schematic is R47 in the bill of materials and R47 silkscreened on the board, which is how you get from “this pull-up is wrong” to a component you can physically point at.
  • Power symbols are nets too: every 3V3 symbol on every sheet is one net. Separate symbols such as VDDA, 5V, 24V, GND, AGND are separate nets, and where two grounds meet — a single point, a ferrite, a zero-ohm link — is often exactly what you need to know when a measurement is noisy.
  • Off-sheet connectors and hierarchical blocks carry signals between sheets; the sheet index at the front tells you which block is where.
  • Test points are the hardware team telling you where to probe. Learn where yours are before you need them (chapter 62).
  • DNP / NF — do not populate — marks a component drawn but not fitted. This is a classic source of “the schematic says there is a pull-up” when there is not one on the board in front of you, and the assembly drawing or BOM is the authority, not the schematic.

The six things to check before writing a driver

🧒 Simple

Before writing code for a chip on the board, there are a handful of questions the schematic answers in two minutes, and each of them can waste a day if you guess instead.

🎓 Professional
  1. Which pin, and which alternate function? Find the net from the peripheral to the part, then check the pin supports that function in the datasheet's alternate-function table (chapter 31). A board designed without checking that table is a board that needs bit-banging or a rework.
  2. What voltage is on that net? A 5 V device on a 3.3 V pin needs level shifting, and a 3.3 V output into a 5 V input may not reach the input's logic-high threshold even though nothing is damaged — the fault then looks intermittent and temperature-dependent.
  3. Is it pulled up, pulled down, or floating? For I²C, what value are the pull-ups — because that value sets the rise time and therefore the maximum bus speed. For an input, does it have a defined level before your firmware configures the pin (during the window between reset and init)?
  4. Is the signal inverted? An opto-isolator or a transistor stage usually inverts, so “active low” in the field is a 1 at the pin. Read it off the schematic once and put it in one named function in the hardware layer (chapter 35).
  5. What is the address, and are there address-select pins? An I²C device's address depends on how its A0/A1 pins are strapped, and the schematic is where that is decided. Half of “the sensor does not answer” is a wrong address.
  6. What does this chip need in order to start? A reset line held by the MCU, an enable pin, a power sequence, a crystal, a boot-mode strap. Many devices need a minimum delay after power-up before they answer, and that delay belongs in your init code.

Two more that are worth checking on any board with actuators, because they are safety-relevant rather than merely annoying: what happens to every output between power-on and your configuration code (pins are inputs then, so anything that must stay off needs a resistor on the board), and what the board does when the MCU is in reset or the watchdog fires.

Talking to the hardware engineer

🧒 Simple

The most useful thing you can bring to a hardware colleague is a specific observation and a specific question, rather than a complaint. “It does not work” starts an argument. “This line is at 1.8 V when it should be at 3.3, and here is the capture” starts an investigation.

🎓 Professional

What firmware can usefully contribute to a schematic review, in descending order of value — and being invited to those reviews is a career step, because it means the board improves before it is built:

  • Pin-function conflicts, found in the alternate-function table. This is the single most valuable check, and it is pure firmware knowledge.
  • Reset-state hazards: outputs that must be safe before firmware runs.
  • Debug access: are SWD pins available on a header, is there a serial port for diagnostics, are there test points on the buses you will have to probe, and is there a recovery path such as BOOT0 (chapter 34)? Asking for these costs nothing before layout and is impossible after.
  • EXTI collisions: two interrupt inputs on the same pin number of different ports cannot both work.
  • Spare pins brought out, ideally two, for the instrumentation in chapter 61. A spare pin on a test point has paid for itself the first time you need to time an ISR on a customer's machine.
  • Sensor front ends: filtering and impedance suited to the sampling you intend (chapter 33).

And the vocabulary that makes the conversation efficient, which is also chapter 67 in practice: net, rail, pull-up, open-drain, to assert and de-assert, rise time, decoupling, ferrite, flyback diode, opto, strap, rework, DNP, test point. Using the right word for the thing is most of being taken seriously across the discipline boundary.

⚠ Trap

The schematic is not the board — and the difference is where two days go.

The schematic says what was intended. The board in your hand is a particular revision, possibly with components not fitted, possibly with a bodge wire somebody added last month and did not document, possibly rev B while the PDF is rev C. Firmware written against the drawing then fails on the hardware for reasons that look like software bugs.

Three habits that cost minutes. Check the revision printed on the board against the one on the schematic, every time you pick up a different unit. Look at the board for missing components and bodge wires before trusting a net — DNP positions are visibly empty. And measure the net when behaviour contradicts the drawing: a multimeter in continuity mode settles “is this pin actually connected to that chip” in ten seconds, and it is the measurement most firmware engineers never think to make.

The related trap, which is worse because it produces confident wrong conclusions: two boards that are not the same. When a bug reproduces on one unit and not another, compare the hardware revisions before theorising, because you may be debugging a difference in the boards rather than in the code. Record the board revision in your commit messages and defect reports for exactly this reason — it is a piece of context that cannot be recovered later.

Try it. Every Nucleo and Discovery board publishes its full schematic as a PDF, so this needs no hardware beyond the board you already have.

1. Download the schematic for your Nucleo and find the user LED.
   Follow the net from the MCU pin to the LED and its resistor, and
   answer: which port and pin, is it active high or low, and what
   current does that resistor set? Then confirm with your own code.

2. Find the ST-LINK section and identify the SWD pins, the virtual
   COM port pins, and the solder bridges that connect or disconnect
   them. Those bridges are why a Nucleo's UART sometimes goes to the
   PC and sometimes to the header.

3. Find one DNP component on the schematic and then look for it on
   the board. Seeing an empty pad that the drawing shows populated,
   once, permanently changes how much you trust a PDF.

4. Do the six-point check for one real peripheral -- the I2C sensor
   on a Discovery board, or a device on a shield: pin and alternate
   function, voltage, pull-ups and their value, inversion, address
   straps, and what it needs to start. Write the six answers down
   BEFORE writing any driver code.

5. Use a multimeter in continuity mode to confirm one net you just
   traced on paper. Ten seconds, and it is the habit that resolves
   "the schematic says it is connected" arguments.

6. Then write the review comments you would send for a board that
   used PA13 for an LED, put two interrupt inputs on PA0 and PB0, and
   brought out no spare test points. Those three comments are worth
   more to a hardware team than most firmware reviews.
Golden rules
  • Same net name means same wire, across sheets. Search the PDF rather than tracing lines.
  • Crossing lines are not connected unless there is a dot.
  • DNP means drawn but not fitted. The BOM and the board, not the schematic, are the authority.
  • Check pin and alternate function against the datasheet table before writing a driver — and before the board is laid out, if you are invited.
  • Check the voltage on the net: a 3.3 V output may never reach a 5 V input's threshold, which looks intermittent.
  • Check pull-ups and their value; on I²C they set the rise time and so the maximum speed.
  • Assume field inputs are inverted by their conditioning, and put the inversion in one named function.
  • Check address straps — half of “the sensor does not answer” is a wrong I²C address.
  • Ask what every output does before firmware runs, and what happens during reset or a watchdog event.
  • Ask for debug access and two spare pins before layout. It is free then and impossible afterwards.
  • The schematic is intent; the board is fact. Check the revision, look for bodges, and measure continuity when the two disagree.
  • Record the board revision in commits and defect reports.

Su che pin arriva, e quel pin supporta l’alternate function che mi serve — questo lo verifico sulla tabella del datasheet, ed è anche la cosa più utile che posso dire in una revisione di schema, prima che la scheda sia fatta. Che tensione c’è sulla net, perché un’uscita a 3,3 volt su un ingresso a 5 può non arrivare alla soglia e il guasto sembra intermittente. Se c’è un pull-up e di che valore, che su I2C mi decide il tempo di salita e quindi la velocità massima. Se il segnale è invertito, perché un opto o uno stadio a transistor invertono quasi sempre e quell’inversione la metto in una funzione sola nel livello hardware. Come sono strappati i pin di indirizzo. E cosa serve al dispositivo per partire: reset, enable, un tempo minimo dopo l’alimentazione. Poi la cosa che ho imparato a non dimenticare: lo schema è l’intenzione, la scheda è il fatto. Controllo la revisione stampata sulla scheda, guardo se ci sono componenti non montati o fili volanti, e se il comportamento contraddice il disegno metto il multimetro in continuità: dieci secondi e la discussione è chiusa.”
