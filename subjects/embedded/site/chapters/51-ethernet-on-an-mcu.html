Ethernet on a microcontroller

Two chips pretending to be one: a MAC inside the microcontroller and a PHY outside it, joined by a bus called RMII. Descriptors, buffers, and the division of labour between silicon that handles the wire and firmware that handles everything else.

From the advert

This chapter answers “Conoscenza approfondita dei protocolli di comunicazione seriale e Ethernet” — a line that appeared in the adverts this course was built from. The word approfondita is doing the work: a candidate who has plugged in a cable and seen a web page has not met this chapter, and the questions that separate the two are about descriptors and buffers rather than about IP addresses.

MAC, PHY, and the bus between them

🧒 Simple

Ethernet on a small board is two pieces. Inside the microcontroller is the part that assembles and checks frames — the addresses, the length, the checksum. Outside, in a separate small chip, is the part that actually drives electricity down the cable and recovers it at the other end.

They talk to each other over a small parallel bus, and there is a second, slower connection used only for configuration: asking the outside chip whether a cable is plugged in, and at what speed the other end agreed to talk.

🎓 Professional
  • The MAC is inside the MCU. It builds and parses frames, filters by destination address, computes and checks the frame CRC, and moves payload to and from memory — almost always by DMA, because at 100 Mbit/s a frame arrives every 12 µs at worst and no CPU should be copying it by hand.
  • The PHY is an external chip. It does the analogue work: line driving, equalisation, clock recovery, auto-negotiation, and link detection. Between them sits a magnetics module — a small transformer block, often inside the RJ45 connector — which provides galvanic isolation. That isolation is why Ethernet survives being run between two machines with different ground potentials, and it is worth knowing when somebody proposes replacing it.
  • MII uses 16 pins at 25 MHz; RMII uses 7 at 50 MHz and is what nearly every design chooses, because pins are scarce (chapter 29). RMII needs a single 50 MHz reference clock shared by MAC and PHY, and getting that clock wrong — wrong source, wrong pin, missing entirely — is the most common reason a new board's Ethernet never comes up at all.
  • MDIO / MDC is the slow two-wire management bus to the PHY's registers: link status, speed, duplex, auto-negotiation results, and the PHY address strapped on the board. Your driver reads these at start-up and on link change, and if the strapped PHY address does not match what the software expects, everything else fails silently.

What the hardware does not do: nothing above the frame. IP, ARP, UDP, TCP, DHCP and everything else are software, which is chapter 52. Many STM32 MACs will offload the IP, TCP and UDP checksums, which is worth enabling because it is free performance — but it has to be enabled consistently in both the MAC and the stack, or you get frames with zero checksums that a switch silently drops.

Descriptors and buffers

🧒 Simple

The MAC does not ask your program for permission to receive a frame. It has a list of memory blocks you have handed it in advance, and it fills them as frames arrive, ticking each one off as it goes.

Your job is to take the filled ones, do something with them, and give them back. If you are slow, the list runs out and frames are simply dropped — and once that starts, it tends to keep happening.

🎓 Professional

The structure is a ring of descriptors, each pointing at a buffer and carrying an ownership bit: owned by the DMA or owned by the CPU. Receiving is a loop of: find a descriptor the DMA has handed back, process or queue its buffer, then set the ownership bit back to the DMA. Transmitting is the mirror image.

The details that decide whether it works under load:

  • Buffer count is a design decision, not a default. Four receive buffers is fine on a bench and not fine on a busy plant network where broadcast traffic arrives in bursts. Count the overruns the MAC reports and expose that counter (the same argument as the CAN error counters).
  • Return descriptors promptly. A design that holds a buffer for the whole duration of application processing starves the ring. Either copy out quickly or use a stack with proper buffer ownership, which is what lwIP's pbuf model exists for.
  • Alignment and placement matter. Descriptors and buffers must sit where the Ethernet DMA can reach them — on some STM32 parts that excludes CCM RAM entirely, which produces a driver that works perfectly except that it never receives anything.
  • On a Cortex-M7, caches. This is the classic: the DMA writes the frame into SRAM, the CPU reads a stale cache line, and the packet appears corrupted or empty — while the debugger shows the memory as correct, because the debugger reads through a different path (chapter 22). Either place buffers in a non-cacheable MPU region or invalidate and clean correctly, and expect to lose a day to this once.
  • A frame is up to 1518 bytes. With a handful of buffers each way plus the stack's own memory, Ethernet costs tens of kilobytes of RAM before any application data, which is why it changes part selection.

Bringing a link up, in order

🧒 Simple

When Ethernet does not work on a new board, the fault is almost always in one of three places, and they can be checked in order: is there a link at all, are frames being received, and is the software above answering.

🎓 Professional
  1. Link. The PHY's LEDs and its status register agree, or they do not. No link means hardware: the magnetics, the cable, the 50 MHz reference clock, the PHY's power or reset line. No software above this level can help.
  2. MDIO. Can you read the PHY's identifier registers and get a sensible manufacturer value? If that returns 0xFFFF or zeros, the management bus or the PHY address is wrong, and nothing else will work.
  3. Frames. Put the MAC in promiscuous mode and count received frames. On any real network there is background traffic within seconds — ARP, broadcasts, discovery. Zero frames with a good link points at descriptors, buffer placement or the DMA configuration.
  4. ARP. Ping the board from a PC and watch for an ARP request arriving and a reply going out. This is the first point where the stack participates.
  5. ICMP, then your protocol. Only now is ping meaningful.

And the instrument that makes all of this fast: Wireshark on a PC, with the board on its own switch or connected directly. A capture answers “did the frame leave, and was it well-formed” in seconds, which is the Ethernet equivalent of putting a logic analyser on a serial line. Learning to read a capture is not optional for anybody who claims Ethernet on a CV.

⚠ Trap

It works on the bench with a direct cable and falls apart on the customer's network, because a plant network is nothing like a desk.

On your desk there are two devices and almost no traffic. On a real network there is broadcast and multicast traffic arriving continuously, other devices probing, a switch that may enforce port security, and possibly a storm when somebody miswires a loop. A receive path sized for the bench overruns; a stack that allocates per packet fragments; a device that replies to every broadcast wastes its own CPU; and a discovery protocol you never tested floods you with packets you do not understand.

Three things to build in from the start. Filter in hardware: configure the MAC's address filter and multicast hash so that traffic which is not for you is dropped in silicon rather than in your CPU. Bound everything — a fixed pool of buffers, a fixed number of connections, and defined behaviour when the pool is empty, which is dropping and counting rather than blocking (chapter 61). Test under load deliberately: a broadcast flood from a PC for an hour, while your device carries its normal traffic, is a twenty-minute experiment that finds what six months in a factory would.

The related organisational trap: the moment a device has an Ethernet port, it belongs to the customer's IT department as well as to their maintenance team. Fixed address or DHCP, which VLAN, which ports must be open, what happens when the network is down for an hour — these become requirements, and they are much cheaper to ask about at design time than to discover at installation (chapter 53).

Try it. A Nucleo-F746ZG or any board with an RJ45 is about twenty-five euro, and the PC side needs only Wireshark.

1. Before writing any code, read the PHY over MDIO and print its ID
   registers, link status, speed and duplex. If that works, the
   hardware path is proven and everything after it is software.

2. Put the MAC in promiscuous mode, count received frames, and print
   the count once a second. Plug into any office network and watch
   background traffic arrive. Zero frames with a good link means
   descriptors or buffer placement.

3. Print the first 32 bytes of one received frame as hex and decode
   it by hand: destination MAC, source MAC, EtherType. Doing this once
   makes every later capture readable.

4. Run Wireshark on a PC connected directly to the board, filter on
   your board's MAC address, and watch your ARP reply go out. Then
   deliberately break the checksum offload configuration and watch the
   PC drop your frames -- a bug that is invisible on the board and
   obvious in a capture.

5. Instrument the ring: count receive overruns and transmit
   descriptor exhaustion, and expose both. Then flood the board with
   broadcast traffic from the PC (ping -f to a broadcast address, or
   iperf) and watch the counters move. Size your buffers from what you
   see rather than from the example project.

6. If your part is an M7, put a receive buffer in cacheable memory
   on purpose and watch reception break in a way the debugger denies.
   Then fix it with an MPU region. That is a day saved, later.
Golden rules
  • MAC inside, PHY outside, magnetics for isolation. RMII between them, with one shared 50 MHz reference.
  • A missing or wrong RMII reference clock is the most common reason a new board's Ethernet never comes up.
  • MDIO first. If the PHY ID registers do not read sensibly, nothing above will work.
  • The MAC owns a ring of descriptors with an ownership bit; return them promptly or the ring starves.
  • Buffer count is a design decision. Count overruns and expose the counter.
  • Buffers must live where the Ethernet DMA can reach them — not CCM RAM on many parts.
  • On an M7, cache maintenance or a non-cacheable region, and the debugger will hide the bug.
  • Enable checksum offload consistently in both MAC and stack, or a switch drops your frames.
  • Filter in hardware so traffic that is not yours never reaches your CPU.
  • Bring up in order: link, MDIO, frames, ARP, ICMP, and use Wireshark from the first minute.
  • Test under a broadcast flood; a bench with two devices proves almost nothing.

“Ethernet su microcontrollore: cosa fa l’hardware e cosa resta al firmware?”

“L’hardware fa il livello due e basta. Dentro l’MCU c’è il MAC, che monta e verifica le trame, filtra sull’indirizzo, calcola il CRC e sposta i dati in memoria via DMA; fuori c’è il PHY, che fa la parte analogica, e in mezzo le magnetiche che danno l’isolamento galvanico — che è il motivo per cui l’Ethernet sopravvive fra due macchine con masse diverse. Il collegamento è RMII, sette pin e un riferimento a 50 MHz condiviso: se quel clock è sbagliato la scheda non parte proprio, ed è il guasto numero uno sulle schede nuove. Poi c’è l’MDIO, e la prima cosa che faccio in bring-up è leggere i registri di identificazione del PHY: se quelli tornano, il percorso hardware è dimostrato. Tutto il resto è software: IP, ARP, UDP, TCP. Al firmware resta la gestione dei descrittori — un anello con un bit di proprietà — e soprattutto il dimensionamento dei buffer, che non è un default: su una rete di stabilimento arriva un sacco di traffico broadcast, e un percorso di ricezione tarato sul banco va in overrun. Quindi filtro in hardware quello che non è per me, conto gli overrun e li espongo, e provo il dispositivo sotto flood di broadcast prima di consegnarlo. E uso Wireshark dal primo minuto: una cattura risponde in due secondi alla domanda se la trama è uscita ed è ben formata.”
