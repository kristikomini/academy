ADC, DAC and the analogue front end

Turning a voltage into a number, and the four things that decide whether that number means anything: the reference it is measured against, the time the sampling capacitor was given to charge, what was done about noise, and whether anybody wrote down the units.

From the advert

Not named in the advert, and unavoidable in the product behind it: a liquid cooling system measures temperatures, pressures and flow, and every one of those arrives as a voltage. This chapter is also the direct counterpart of plc-lab exercise 5 — scaling a raw count into engineering units is the same problem on both sides of the cable, and chapter 58 is where the two sides agree on the convention.

From volts to counts, and back to units

🧒 Simple

An ADC compares the voltage on a pin against a reference voltage and reports where it falls, as a whole number. With 12 bits it reports one of 4096 steps: zero means ground, 4095 means the reference.

That number is meaningless on its own. It only becomes a temperature or a pressure when you apply the sensor's own relationship — and when you know how accurate the reference itself is, because everything is measured relative to it.

🎓 Professional

The conversion is V = raw × VREF / 4095, and the accuracy of the whole chain is the accuracy of VREF. On many boards VREF is simply the 3.3 V supply, so a regulator specified at ±2 % makes every reading ±2 % no matter how many bits the ADC has. Resolution is not accuracy, and a 16-bit result on a 2 % reference is a precisely reported wrong number. When accuracy matters, the answers are a dedicated voltage reference, or a ratiometric measurement — powering the sensor from the same reference so the ratio cancels — which costs nothing and is frequently the right engineering answer.

ST parts help with two built-ins worth knowing about: an internal reference channel with a factory-calibrated value in system memory, from which the actual VDDA can be computed at run time, and an internal temperature sensor with its own calibration points. Reading the calibration constants rather than the datasheet's typical values is the difference between a temperature reading that is a few degrees out and one that is usable.

On the scaling itself, the conventions this course keeps returning to:

  • Scaled integers, not floats. Store tenths of a degree in an int16_t, not 23.4 in a float. It is exact, it is what the PLC expects, and it avoids dragging floating-point into an interrupt path.
  • Do the arithmetic in a wide enough type — (int32_t)raw * span / 4095 overflows a 16-bit intermediate long before you expect, which is chapter 3 arriving in the real world.
  • Name the unit in the identifier. temp_c_x10, press_mbar. The unit lives in the name or it lives in somebody's memory, and the second one is how a Mars orbiter was lost.
  • Range-check before converting. A raw value at 0 or at full scale usually means a disconnected or shorted sensor, not a real extreme — and reporting “−40 °C” from a broken wire is worse than reporting a fault.

Sampling time, impedance, and the capacitor nobody pictures

🧒 Simple

Inside the chip the ADC does not measure the pin directly. It connects a small capacitor to the pin, waits for it to charge to the same voltage, disconnects it, and then measures the capacitor.

If it does not wait long enough, the capacitor never quite reaches the pin's voltage and the reading is low — and how long is long enough depends on what is connected outside. A sensor through a big resistor charges it slowly.

🎓 Professional

This is the single most common cause of readings that are “nearly right”. The sample and hold capacitor charges through the source impedance, so the required sampling time is a multiple of that RC — roughly nine time constants for 12-bit settling. The datasheet states a maximum source impedance for each sampling-time setting, and the practical consequences are:

  • A high-impedance divider needs a long sampling time or an op-amp buffer in front of it. A 100 kΩ divider straight into an ADC at the shortest sampling time reads low, consistently, and looks like a calibration error.
  • A multiplexed ADC carries charge between channels. Scanning from a 3 V channel to a 0.1 V channel with too short a sampling time shows the second channel pulled up by the first — the classic “channel 2 follows channel 1” report, and the cure is sampling time, not software.
  • A small capacitor at the pin (typically 100 nF, right at the connector) supplies the charge locally and makes the whole problem smaller. That is a schematic conversation (chapter 63), and knowing to ask for it is firmware being useful at review time.

Two more facts that decide whether a measurement is real. Aliasing: anything faster than half your sampling rate does not vanish, it comes back disguised as a slow signal, and no amount of software fixes it — an RC low-pass filter before the pin is the only answer. And oversampling: averaging N samples reduces random noise by the square root of N, so 16 samples buy two extra effective bits for free, but averaging does nothing at all against a periodic interferer such as a switching regulator or 50 Hz mains — for that, sample synchronously with the interference or filter it in hardware.

The architecture that makes all of this deterministic: a timer triggers the ADC, the ADC feeds DMA, and the CPU is handed a complete block of samples taken at exact instants (chapters 32 and 27). Sampling in a software loop means your sample instants jitter with whatever else the system was doing, which is invisible in the data and fatal to any frequency analysis.

Output: DAC and PWM as an analogue source

🧒 Simple

Going the other way — producing a voltage — there are two options. A real DAC sets a voltage directly. Or you can switch a pin on and off quickly and smooth it with a resistor and capacitor, which is cheaper and slower.

🎓 Professional

A DAC gives a clean voltage with a settling time in microseconds, but it drives almost no current — anything real needs a buffer amplifier after it. Typical uses are a reference for a comparator, an offset, or a waveform played out of a table by DMA on a timer trigger, which costs no CPU at all.

PWM plus an RC filter is the industrial workhorse for slow set-points: cheap, available on many more pins, and the trade is explicit — the filter's cut-off must be far below the PWM frequency to suppress ripple, and that same filter sets how slowly the output can change. Ripple and response time are the same knob turned in opposite directions, and saying that out loud is what distinguishes an engineering answer from a recipe.

The industrial context worth carrying into an interview: outside the board, analogue signalling is usually 4–20 mA, not a voltage, because a current loop is immune to voltage drop along a long cable and because 0 mA is distinguishable from a valid zero — a broken wire reads 0 and 4 mA means “zero, and I am alive”. That is the same design instinct as the heartbeat in chapter 58: make the absence of a signal detectable rather than plausible.

⚠ Trap

The reading is noisy, so a filter is added in software — and the filter hides a hardware fault for six months.

It is the most natural sequence in the world. The value jumps around by twenty counts, somebody adds a moving average, the display goes still, and the ticket is closed. What was actually happening was a ground loop, or an unfiltered switching regulator, or a sensor cable run beside a motor cable — and the average has not removed the interference, it has removed your ability to see it. When the interference gets worse in the field, the same average now hides a genuine measurement error, and the report reads “temperature reads 4 degrees low sometimes”.

The discipline: look at the raw samples before filtering anything. Capture a few thousand raw values to RAM, dump them, and look at the distribution. Random scatter around a mean is noise, and averaging is the right answer. A periodic pattern is an interferer, and its frequency names the culprit — 50 Hz is mains, tens of kilohertz is a switching supply, and something at exactly your PWM frequency is your own output coupling back into your own input. Steps that correlate with a relay or a motor are a ground problem. Only the first of those is a firmware problem.

And keep the filtered and unfiltered values both available in diagnostics. A device that can report its own raw measurement is a device somebody can debug over a cable instead of on site, which is the same argument as the error counters in chapter 49.

Try it. A Nucleo, a potentiometer and a few resistors cover all of this.

1. Read a potentiometer and print raw counts. Turn it to both ends
   and confirm you see roughly 0 and 4095. Then compute volts and
   print both. Keep the raw value visible forever after -- that habit
   is exercise 5.

2. Demonstrate the sampling-time trap on purpose. Put a 100 k
   resistor in series with the pot's wiper and repeat with the
   SHORTEST sampling time: the reading is low. Now raise the sampling
   time and watch it come right. Then add 100 nF at the pin and watch
   the short setting work again. Three minutes, and it is the most
   common analogue bug in the field.

3. Demonstrate channel crosstalk. Scan two channels, one tied to
   3.3 V and one to ground, with a short sampling time -- watch the
   grounded one read above zero. Fix it with sampling time.

4. Oversample honestly. Capture 1000 raw samples of a fixed voltage
   into an array and compute mean and standard deviation. Then
   average 16 and recompute. The noise should drop by about four.
   That is the square-root rule confirmed on your own board.

5. Then dump those 1000 raw samples over the UART and plot them.
   Decide from the PLOT whether you have noise or an interferer
   BEFORE writing any filter. If there is a periodic component, find
   its frequency and name the source.

6. Timer-trigger the ADC into a DMA buffer at exactly 1 kHz and
   confirm, with a toggling pin on the DMA complete interrupt, that
   the block arrives at a fixed rate no matter what the main loop is
   doing. Compare against sampling in a software loop while sending
   UART traffic -- the jitter is visible.
Golden rules
  • Accuracy is the reference's accuracy. Resolution is not accuracy; a 12-bit result on a 2 % supply is 2 % wrong.
  • Prefer ratiometric measurement, or a real reference, when the number matters.
  • Read the factory calibration constants for the internal reference and temperature sensor instead of the typical values.
  • Sampling time must suit the source impedance, or readings are low and channels bleed into each other.
  • Aliasing cannot be fixed in software. Filter before the pin.
  • Averaging beats random noise by the square root of N and does nothing against a periodic interferer.
  • Look at raw samples before adding any filter, and keep the raw value available in diagnostics.
  • Timer-trigger the ADC and DMA the result. Software sampling jitters invisibly.
  • Scaled integers with the unit in the identifier, and wide enough intermediate types.
  • Full scale and zero usually mean a broken sensor, so range-check and report a fault rather than a plausible extreme.
  • A DAC needs a buffer; PWM plus RC trades ripple against response time; and in the field, 4–20 mA exists so that a broken wire is distinguishable from a real zero.

“La lettura di un sensore è rumorosa. Cosa fa?”

“La prima cosa che non faccio è mettere subito una media: la media non toglie il disturbo, toglie la possibilità di vederlo. Prima catturo qualche migliaio di campioni grezzi in RAM, li tiro fuori e li guardo. Se è dispersione casuale intorno a un valore medio, allora sì, è rumore e la media è la risposta giusta: mediando N campioni il rumore scende di radice di N. Ma se nel grafico vedo una periodicità, quella frequenza mi dice il colpevole: cinquanta hertz è la rete, decine di kilohertz è un alimentatore switching, e se è esattamente la mia frequenza di PWM allora mi sto accoppiando da solo. E se i gradini arrivano quando scatta un relè è un problema di massa. Solo il primo caso è un problema di firmware. Poi ci sono due cose che guardo sempre: il tempo di campionamento rispetto all’impedenza della sorgente — con un partitore ad alta impedenza e il sampling time più corto la lettura viene bassa e sembra un errore di taratura — e il fondo scala: zero o 4095 di solito vuol dire sensore staccato o in corto, e riportare meno quaranta gradi per un filo rotto è peggio che riportare un guasto. E nella diagnostica tengo esposto sia il valore filtrato sia quello grezzo.”
