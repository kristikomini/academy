Low power, sleep modes and the watchdog

Two mechanisms that look unrelated and belong together: the one that decides how little the chip does while waiting, and the one that decides what happens when it stops doing anything at all. The second is the clearest example in this course of a safety feature that is trivial to install and trivial to defeat.

From the advert

Not named in this advert — a mains-powered controller in a cabinet rarely needs sleep modes. The watchdog half is universal: every industrial and automotive product in the corpus has one, it is asked about in interviews, and functional safety assumes it.

Sleep modes: what stays awake, and what it costs to wake

🧒 Simple

Most of the time a controller is waiting: for a byte, for a timer, for a button. Waiting in a busy loop burns current for nothing. Every microcontroller can therefore switch parts of itself off and be woken by an event.

There is a ladder. At the top, only the processor stops and everything else keeps running — wakes in a moment, saves a little. At the bottom, almost everything stops including the memory, so waking up means starting again from the beginning — saves enormously, costs your variables.

🎓 Professional

The ST naming, which maps onto every other vendor's with different words:

  • Sleep — the core clock stops, peripherals and RAM keep running. Entered with WFI (wait for interrupt) or WFE, exited by any enabled interrupt in a few cycles, with all state intact. This is the one that should be the default in almost every project: an idle loop that is __WFI() instead of while(1); costs nothing in complexity and cuts current meaningfully.
  • Stop — clocks off, PLL off, RAM and register contents retained, woken by EXTI lines and the RTC. Microamps rather than milliamps, and wake-up takes microseconds because the oscillator and PLL must restart — which is the number to budget for, not the current.
  • Standby — nearly everything off, RAM lost except a small backup domain, wake-up is a reset. Design consequence: anything that must survive goes in backup registers or in flash, and your startup code must be able to tell an ordinary power-on from a standby wake-up.

Three practical rules. The power state of the I/O pins matters as much as the core — a floating input on a CMOS pin oscillates and can burn more current than the sleeping chip; unused pins go to analogue or to a defined pull. Measure, do not estimate: the datasheet number is a die at 25 °C with peripherals off, and a real board's consumption is usually dominated by something outside the MCU, such as a regulator's quiescent current or a pull-up on an I²C bus. And a debugger keeps the core powered, so current measured with SWD attached is not the product's current, and a breakpoint in a low-power path often prevents the mode being entered at all.

For a battery product the figure that decides the design is the average current: duty cycle times active current plus the rest of the time at sleeping current, plus the wake-up energy multiplied by how often you wake. Waking ten times a second to check something that changes once an hour is the usual mistake, and the fix is an interrupt instead of a poll (chapter 26).

The watchdog: what it is actually for

🧒 Simple

A watchdog is a countdown timer inside the chip that resets the board when it reaches zero. Your program has to keep resetting the countdown — “kicking” or “feeding” it — to show it is still alive.

If the program locks up, crashes, or gets stuck waiting for something that never comes, the countdown finishes and the board restarts. On a machine in a factory, a device that restarts in two seconds is enormously better than one that hangs until somebody notices.

🎓 Professional

On STM32 there are two, and the difference is the interesting part:

  • IWDG, the independent watchdog. Clocked from the LSI, its own internal oscillator, so it keeps counting even if the main clock tree has collapsed — which is precisely the failure it exists for. Once started it cannot be stopped, and its timing is only as accurate as the LSI, meaning tens of percent.
  • WWDG, the window watchdog. Clocked from the system clock, and it faults both when you are late and when you are early. The window is the point: a kick that arrives too soon suggests code running at the wrong rate or a loop kicking in a place it should not.

The design rules that separate a watchdog that protects a product from one that decorates it:

  • Kick from one place only, in the main supervisory loop, and only after checking that everything that must be alive is. In an RTOS, each task sets a flag or updates a timestamp and the supervisor kicks only if every task has reported since the last pass — that turns one hung task into a reset instead of a silent partial failure.
  • Never kick from a timer interrupt. A periodic ISR keeps running while the application is deadlocked, so the watchdog reports a healthy system forever. This is the commonest way to neutralise a watchdog, and it is usually done for the most reasonable-sounding reason: “the main loop was occasionally too slow”.
  • Set the timeout from the measured worst case, with margin — long enough that a legitimate slow path never trips it, short enough that the product's own requirement for recovery time is met.
  • Record the reset cause. The RCC keeps flags saying whether the last reset was power-on, pin, software or watchdog. Read them at startup, clear them, and count watchdog resets in non-volatile storage. A product that resets twice a week and cannot tell you is a product nobody can fix; one that reports “IWDG reset, 14 times, last at uptime 3 h 20” has already half-diagnosed itself.

And the honest limitation to say out loud in an interview: a watchdog is a recovery mechanism, not a correctness mechanism. It converts a hang into a restart. It cannot detect wrong output, and a device that restarts every thirty seconds and is counted as working is a hidden fault, not a protected system.

Why the two chapters are one chapter

🧒 Simple

Sleeping and watchdogs collide: while the chip is asleep the countdown may still be running. If it is, the board resets while doing nothing wrong. So the two have to be designed together.

🎓 Professional

The interaction, concretely. IWDG runs from LSI, which stays alive in Stop mode — so a device that sleeps for longer than the watchdog period is reset by it. Three legitimate answers, and which you choose is a real design decision: make the watchdog period longer than the longest sleep; wake periodically just to kick, paying that energy on purpose; or use the debug freeze bits so the behaviour is at least predictable during development. What is not acceptable is disabling the watchdog around the sleep, because the sleep path is exactly where a hang is hardest to notice.

The debug freeze registers deserve a line of their own: they stop the watchdog counters while the core is halted by the debugger. Without them, every breakpoint becomes a reset and the watchdog gets disabled “temporarily” during development — and a temporarily disabled watchdog has a way of shipping.

⚠ Trap

The watchdog is kicked from a timer interrupt, and it now certifies that the timer works.

It always starts sensibly. The main loop occasionally takes longer than the watchdog period during a flash write or a long protocol frame, the board resets, and moving the kick into the 1 ms timer ISR makes the problem disappear. It disappears because the watchdog no longer measures anything the application does: the ISR runs whatever state the application is in, including deadlocked on a mutex, stuck in a while waiting for a flag that will never set, or looping in a state machine that can never leave its state. The product now has a watchdog in the schematic, in the safety documentation, and in no useful sense at all.

The correct fix to the original problem is the supervisory pattern: every participant reports, one place kicks. Each task or subsystem updates its own “I ran at time T” timestamp; a single supervisor checks all of them against their expected periods and kicks only when all are current. The slow flash write then declares its own longer deadline honestly instead of being hidden, and a hung task is caught, which is the entire point.

The same anti-pattern, worth recognising in other clothes: a heartbeat counter incremented by a timer rather than by the code path it claims to monitor (chapter 58), and a CI job that reports success because it ran, not because it tested anything (chapter 64a). A monitor driven by something other than the thing it monitors always reports health.

Try it. The watchdog exercises need a board; a Nucleo and its onboard debugger are enough, and a multimeter in series with the supply is enough for the current ones.

1. Turn your idle loop into __WFI() and measure the difference. Even
   on a Nucleo with everything else powered, the drop is visible on a
   cheap multimeter. That single line is the highest-value low-power
   change in most projects.

2. Enable IWDG with a 2-second timeout, kick it in the main loop, and
   then deliberately hang: add a `while (1);` behind a button press.
   Watch the board come back. Do it once, so you trust it.

3. Read the reset cause at startup and print it -- power-on, pin,
   software, IWDG, WWDG. Then store a watchdog-reset counter in
   backup registers or flash and print that too. This is a genuinely
   professional touch and takes twenty lines.

4. Build the supervisory pattern with three fake tasks: each updates
   its own timestamp at its own period, a supervisor checks all three
   and kicks only if all are current. Then stop ONE task and confirm
   the board resets. Then move the kick into a timer ISR and repeat,
   and watch it never reset -- that comparison is the whole trap
   above, demonstrated in ten minutes.

5. Set the debug freeze bits and confirm you can breakpoint without
   resetting. Then clear them and feel why everybody disables the
   watchdog during development.

6. If you have a Stop-mode capable board: sleep for 10 s with IWDG
   running at a 2 s timeout and watch it reset. Then fix it by
   lengthening the period, and by waking to kick, and decide which
   you would ship.
Golden rules
  • Idle should be __WFI(), not a spin loop. It is one line.
  • Sleep keeps state; Standby wakes through reset. Anything that must survive goes to backup registers or flash.
  • Budget wake-up time, not just current — the PLL has to restart.
  • Floating inputs cost more than a sleeping core. Give every unused pin a defined state.
  • Measure current without the debugger attached. SWD keeps the core alive.
  • IWDG runs from its own oscillator, so it survives a collapsed clock tree; that is what it is for.
  • Kick from one place, after checking that everything alive reported in.
  • Never kick from a timer interrupt. It converts the watchdog into a test that the timer works.
  • Read and record the reset cause, and count watchdog resets in non-volatile storage.
  • Design sleep and watchdog together, and never disable the watchdog around a sleep.
  • A watchdog recovers, it does not correct. A device that resets regularly is faulty, not protected.

“Come usa il watchdog?”

“Uso l’IWDG, quello indipendente, perché ha il suo oscillatore interno e continua a contare anche se l’albero dei clock è andato — che è esattamente il guasto per cui esiste. La regola che considero non negoziabile è che il kick si fa da un punto solo, nel ciclo supervisore, e solo dopo aver verificato che tutto quello che deve essere vivo abbia dato segno di vita: con un RTOS ogni task aggiorna il suo timestamp e il supervisore fa il kick solo se sono tutti aggiornati. Quello che non faccio mai è metterlo dentro un interrupt di timer: l’ISR continua a girare anche con l’applicativo bloccato su un mutex, quindi il watchdog smette di misurare l’applicazione e certifica che funziona il timer. Succede sempre per il motivo più ragionevole del mondo, cioè che ogni tanto il main loop era troppo lento — ma la risposta giusta è dichiarare la scadenza vera di quel percorso lento, non nascondere il problema. Poi leggo sempre la causa del reset dai flag dell’RCC e tengo un contatore dei reset da watchdog in memoria non volatile: un prodotto che si resetta due volte a settimana e non sa dirtelo non lo aggiusta nessuno. E lo dico chiaro: è un meccanismo di recupero, non di correttezza. Se un dispositivo si riavvia regolarmente è guasto, non protetto.”
