MQTT and the plant network

A protocol designed for devices that are small, numerous and behind a firewall. Publish and subscribe through a broker, three delivery guarantees, a will message that announces your death, and the reason IT will accept it when they will not accept an open port on your machine.

From the advert

Not in the anchor advert, and increasingly present in regional listings once a product ships with Ethernet. It is the protocol that gets a machine's data onto a network an IT department will approve, and it is the usual answer to “we want to see the machines from the office” — a sentence every firmware engineer in this market eventually hears.

Publish, subscribe, broker

🧒 Simple

With Modbus, somebody has to ask your device for each value, and to ask they have to be able to reach it. MQTT turns that around: your device connects outwards to a server called a broker, and posts its values there under names like plant1/chiller3/temp_in. Anybody who wants those values subscribes to the name.

Nobody connects to your machine at all. That single change is why this protocol spread: it works through firewalls, through mobile networks, and across the internet, without opening anything on the factory side.

🎓 Professional
  • Topics are hierarchical strings. Subscribers may wildcard a level with + or everything below with #. Design the hierarchy early — site, line, machine, then the measurement — because a topic structure is an interface and changing it later breaks everybody's dashboards.
  • Publishers and subscribers do not know each other, which is the real architectural difference from Modbus: adding a second consumer of the data costs nothing on the device.
  • QoS 0, 1, 2 — at most once, at least once, exactly once. QoS 1 is the right default: it costs one acknowledgement and it means duplicates are possible, so make your messages idempotent and timestamped. QoS 2 is a four-step handshake that is rarely worth its cost on a constrained device, and QoS 0 is for high-rate telemetry where losing a sample is harmless.
  • Retained messages: the broker keeps the last message on a topic and gives it to any new subscriber immediately. That is how a dashboard opened at 3 a.m. shows the current temperature instead of a blank until the next publish.
  • The last will is the feature worth knowing by name in an interview: at connect time the device tells the broker what to publish if the connection drops unexpectedly. Set the will to status = offline retained, publish status = online retained after connecting, and every consumer learns about a dead machine within the keep-alive interval — without polling anything. It is the network version of the heartbeat.
  • Keep-alive and clean session. The keep-alive interval sets how quickly a vanished device is noticed; a persistent session means the broker holds subscriptions and queued QoS 1 messages across a reconnect, which is usually what an intermittently connected machine wants.

MQTT itself is tiny — a two-byte fixed header on the smallest packet — and embedded client libraries such as the lwIP MQTT client, Paho embedded C or coreMQTT are a few kilobytes. The expensive part is not MQTT; it is TLS, which the next section is about.

Designing what a machine publishes

🧒 Simple

Publishing everything all the time floods the network and the database for no benefit. Publishing too little means nobody can diagnose anything. The useful middle is: send values when they change meaningfully, send everything occasionally anyway, and send events immediately.

🎓 Professional
  • Report by exception, with a heartbeat. Publish a value when it moves more than a deadband, and publish everything on a slow timer regardless — so a consumer that joined late, or missed a message, converges within a known time. Pure change-of-value telemetry leaves gaps nobody can explain later.
  • Timestamp at the source, with a monotonic uptime at minimum and real time if the device has it. Broker arrival time is not measurement time, and the difference matters exactly when the network was slow, which is exactly when you are investigating.
  • JSON or binary? JSON is readable, debuggable and interoperable, and costs bytes and parsing; a compact binary or Sparkplug B encoding costs interoperability. For a machine publishing a few dozen values a second, JSON is almost always the right trade, and being able to justify the choice is what an interviewer is listening for.
  • Separate telemetry from commands. If the device also subscribes to a command topic, treat every arriving command as untrusted input: validate the value, check the machine state permits it, and apply the same interlocks the local interface has. The broker is not an authority.
  • Bound everything. A queue of outgoing messages with a fixed size that drops and counts when full, a publish rate limit, and no allocation per message (chapter 52).

And the rule this course keeps repeating, because it is the one that separates an industrial device from a hobby project: telemetry must never be able to disturb control. If the broker is unreachable, the machine keeps running; if the publish queue is full, values are dropped rather than the control loop delayed; and nothing in the control path ever blocks on a socket.

Security, and the conversation with IT

🧒 Simple

Because MQTT can reach across the internet, it has to be secured properly — encrypted, and with the device proving who it is. On a small chip that costs real memory and real time, and it has to be planned rather than added at the end.

🎓 Professional
  • TLS is the real cost. mbedTLS on a Cortex-M costs tens of kilobytes of flash and a large chunk of RAM per connection, plus a handshake that takes hundreds of milliseconds to seconds. It changes part selection (chapter 29), it wants hardware acceleration if the part has it, and it needs a real entropy source — a weak random number generator is a broken TLS, silently.
  • Certificates need time. Validating a server certificate requires the device to know the date, so a product without an RTC or NTP either cannot validate expiry or must be designed around it. This surprises people late in a project.
  • Credentials must be per device and provisioned somewhere sensible — not a shared username and password compiled into the firmware, which is a single point of compromise for an entire fleet and is exactly what regulators now ask about.
  • Direction matters. The device dials out, so the factory firewall needs one outbound rule and nothing inbound. That is a conversation IT will say yes to, and it is why this protocol succeeds where “please open port 502 to our machine” fails.

The architecture most industrial products converge on, and a good thing to be able to draw: Modbus TCP or a field bus inward, on the closed machine network, for control; MQTT outward over TLS, initiated by the device, for telemetry and remote support. Control stays local and deterministic; observation goes anywhere. In Europe the Cyber Resilience Act is making the secure half of that a documented obligation rather than a preference, which is worth knowing exists even as a junior.

⚠ Trap

The reconnect logic is written last, is never tested, and is what actually decides whether the product works.

A connected device is the easy case and the one everybody builds. In the field the connection drops constantly: a switch reboots, the site's internet fails for an hour, the broker restarts, a firewall drops idle connections overnight. The failure modes that follow are all predictable and all skipped:

  • A tight reconnect loop that hammers the broker every second and, multiplied by two hundred machines after a site-wide outage, keeps the broker down. The fix is exponential backoff with jitter — and the jitter is not optional, because without it every device retries in lockstep.
  • Unbounded buffering while offline. An hour of disconnection at one message per second is 3600 messages, and a queue that grows until memory runs out turns a network problem into a device crash. Decide what to keep: latest values only, or a bounded ring of recent history, and drop the rest with a counter.
  • A blocked control loop. If publishing is synchronous and the socket is full, the machine stops. This is the one that hurts someone.
  • No visible state. The device must be able to say locally — on a display, an LED or over Modbus — whether it is connected, when it last succeeded, and how many messages it has dropped. Otherwise every remote-monitoring complaint is unfalsifiable.

Test it by breaking it: pull the cable for an hour, restart the broker mid-publish, block the port with a firewall rule, and let a hundred simulated devices reconnect at once. That afternoon is worth more than any amount of work on the happy path, and it is the same lesson as the power-cut test in chapter 34 — the mechanism exists for the failure, so test the failure.

Try it. All of it works on a PC before it needs a board; a broker on your laptop costs one command.

1. Run Mosquitto locally and use its command-line tools to feel the
   model:

     mosquitto_sub -t 'plant1/#' -v
     mosquitto_pub -t 'plant1/chiller3/temp_in' -m '21.4' -r

   The -r makes it retained: kill the subscriber, restart it, and the
   value is still there. That is the dashboard-at-3am feature.

2. Set a last will. Connect a client with a will of
   plant1/chiller3/status = offline (retained), publish online after
   connecting, then kill the client with -9 rather than closing it
   cleanly. Watch offline appear by itself. Nothing else in this
   chapter is as useful to be able to describe.

3. Design the topic hierarchy for a machine you know, on paper, and
   then try to answer three questions with wildcards: all temperatures
   in the plant; everything from one machine; every machine's status.
   If any of those is awkward, the hierarchy is wrong -- and it is
   much cheaper to find out now.

4. Then implement it on the board with the lwIP MQTT client:
   report-by-exception with a deadband, a full refresh every 30
   seconds, timestamps from uptime, and a bounded outgoing queue with
   a drop counter exposed over your existing interface.

5. Break it deliberately: unplug for ten minutes, restart the broker
   mid-publish, and confirm backoff with jitter, bounded buffering and
   an undisturbed control loop. Then check that your local status
   display told the truth the whole time.

6. Only then add TLS, and measure what it cost you in flash, RAM and
   handshake time. Those three numbers are a good answer to "have you
   worked with secure connectivity?".
Golden rules
  • The device dials out to a broker, so nothing needs to be opened inbound — which is why IT accepts it.
  • Design the topic hierarchy early. It is an interface, and dashboards depend on it.
  • QoS 1 by default, so make messages idempotent and timestamped at the source.
  • Retained messages give a late subscriber the current value immediately.
  • Set a last will, and publish an online status on connect; that is how absence becomes detectable.
  • Report by exception with a periodic full refresh, so a late or lossy consumer converges.
  • Treat inbound commands as untrusted: validate, check state, apply the same interlocks as the local interface.
  • Telemetry must never disturb control. Bounded queue, drop and count, no blocking in the control path.
  • Reconnect with exponential backoff and jitter, or a fleet takes the broker down together.
  • Bound offline buffering, and decide explicitly what to keep.
  • Expose connection state and drop counts locally, or remote-monitoring complaints cannot be investigated.
  • TLS is the real cost — flash, RAM, handshake time, entropy and a clock for certificate validity — and credentials must be per device.

“Il cliente vuole vedere le macchine dall’ufficio. Come lo imposterebbe?”

“Non aprendo porte sulla macchina. La macchina si collega lei verso un broker MQTT e pubblica i suoi valori su topic gerarchici — stabilimento, linea, macchina, misura — e chi vuole i dati si iscrive. Per l’IT del cliente è una regola di firewall in uscita e niente in ingresso, ed è il motivo per cui dicono di sì. Pubblico a variazione con una banda morta, più un aggiornamento completo periodico così chi si collega dopo converge, con il timestamp messo alla sorgente, non quello di arrivo al broker. Uso QoS 1, quindi i messaggi devono essere idempotenti perché i duplicati sono possibili. E due cose che considero obbligatorie: il retained, così una dashboard aperta di notte vede subito l’ultimo valore invece del vuoto, e soprattutto il last will — dico al broker cosa pubblicare se cado, tipo status offline, e appena connesso pubblico online: così l’assenza di una macchina si nota da sola senza che nessuno faccia polling. La parte che poi decide se il prodotto funziona davvero non è la connessione ma la riconnessione: backoff esponenziale con jitter, se no dopo un blackout duecento macchine ripartono insieme e tengono giù il broker; buffer offline limitato che scarta e conta; e soprattutto la telemetria non deve mai poter disturbare il controllo — se il broker non c’è, la macchina continua a lavorare.”
